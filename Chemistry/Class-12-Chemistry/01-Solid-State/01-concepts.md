# TOPIC 1 — WHAT IS A SOLID, AND WHY ARE THERE TWO KINDS?

## The everyday picture

Think of three rooms full of people.

```
  GAS                 LIQUID                SOLID
  people running      people walking        people standing in
  all over a          shoulder to           a marked grid, each
  huge ground         shoulder in a         on his own square,
                      moving crowd          only shivering

  far apart           touching but          touching and FIXED
  no fixed place      sliding               in place
```

In a **solid**:

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF SOLIDS                                  |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  1. Definite mass, definite volume and definite shape.              |
  |  2. The particles are very close together - intermolecular          |
  |     distances are SHORT.                                            |
  |  3. The forces of attraction between particles are STRONG.          |
  |  4. The particles cannot move about; they only VIBRATE about        |
  |     their fixed mean positions.                                     |
  |  5. Solids are INCOMPRESSIBLE and RIGID.                            |
  |  6. They have HIGH density.                                         |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## But not all solids are equally "solid"

Take common salt and take window glass. Both feel solid. Yet:

- Break a salt crystal and it splits along clean flat faces.
- Break glass and it shatters into random curved pieces.
- Salt melts sharply at 801 degrees C.
- Glass does not melt at one temperature; it slowly softens over a range.

So there are two families.

```
             SOLIDS
                |
        +-------+--------+
        |                |
   CRYSTALLINE      AMORPHOUS
   "long range        "short range
    order"             order only"

   NaCl, sugar,       glass, rubber,
   diamond, quartz,   plastic, quartz
   metals             GLASS, starch
```

```
  LONG RANGE ORDER means the pattern repeats identically
  over and over, all the way across the crystal:

     o  o  o  o  o  o  o  o        <- crystalline: perfect rows,
     o  o  o  o  o  o  o  o           the pattern at one end tells
     o  o  o  o  o  o  o  o           you the pattern at the other
     o  o  o  o  o  o  o  o


  SHORT RANGE ORDER means a few neighbours look tidy, but
  further away the arrangement is a mess:

     o   o  o     o    o            <- amorphous: order dies out
       o   o    o   o     o            after a couple of neighbours
     o    o  o     o   o
        o     o  o    o   o
```

## THE COMPARISON TABLE — write this out, it is a guaranteed question

| Property | CRYSTALLINE SOLID | AMORPHOUS SOLID |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Definite, regular geometrical shape | Irregular shape |
| Order of arrangement | **Long range order** | **Short range order** only |
| Melting point | **Sharp and definite** melting point | **No sharp** m.p.; softens over a range |
| Heat of fusion | Definite heat of fusion | Heat of fusion is **not definite** |
| Cleavage | Clean cleavage — cuts into smooth flat faces when a knife-edge is used | Irregular cut — breaks into pieces of **uneven** surfaces |
| Anisotropy | **ANISOTROPIC** — properties (refractive index, conductivity, thermal expansion) are DIFFERENT in different directions | **ISOTROPIC** — properties are the SAME in all directions |
| Nature | **TRUE solids** | **PSEUDO solids** or **SUPERCOOLED LIQUIDS** |
| Rigidity | Rigid and incompressible | Rigid but can slowly flow |
| Examples | NaCl, KCl, diamond, graphite, quartz, ice, metals, sugar | Glass, rubber, plastics, quartz glass, starch, tar |

```
  ANISOTROPY - the one-line reason (this is what earns the mark)

  In a crystal the arrangement of particles is DIFFERENT along
  different directions, so a property measured along one direction
  differs from the same property along another.

      direction 1:   o - o - o - o - o        particles close together
      direction 2:   o --- o --- o            particles further apart

  In an amorphous solid the arrangement is random, so every direction
  looks statistically the same -> ISOTROPIC.
```

```
  WHY IS GLASS CALLED A "SUPERCOOLED LIQUID"?

  Because it has the disordered arrangement of a liquid but the
  rigidity of a solid, and it can FLOW extremely slowly.
  Very old window panes in ancient buildings are found to be
  slightly THICKER at the BOTTOM than at the top - the glass has
  crept downward over centuries.
```

> **TRAP:** Quartz and quartz glass are made of exactly the same stuff, SiO2.
> **Quartz is crystalline. Quartz GLASS is amorphous.** Examiners love this pair.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 1.1
*Why is glass called a pseudo solid? Give one property that proves it is not a true solid.*

```
  ANSWER

  Glass is called a pseudo solid because it has only SHORT RANGE
  order, like a liquid, although it is rigid like a solid.

  PROOF:  it has NO sharp melting point. On heating it softens
          gradually over a range of temperature, and it can be
          moulded and blown - exactly like a very thick liquid.
          A true crystalline solid melts at ONE fixed temperature.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 1.2
*Classify: (a) tetra phosphorus decoxide (b) ammonium phosphate (c) glass
(d) rubber (e) copper (f) benzene.*

```
  (a) P4O10           - crystalline (molecular)
  (b) (NH4)3PO4       - crystalline (ionic)
  (c) glass           - AMORPHOUS
  (d) rubber          - AMORPHOUS
  (e) copper          - crystalline (metallic)
  (f) benzene         - crystalline (molecular, when solid)
```

# TOPIC 2 — THE FOUR TYPES OF CRYSTALLINE SOLIDS

Crystalline solids are sorted by **what particle sits at the lattice point** and
**what force holds those particles together.**

```
                 CRYSTALLINE SOLIDS
                          |
      +---------+---------+---------+-----------+
      |         |                   |           |
   IONIC    COVALENT           MOLECULAR    METALLIC
            (network)               |
                            +-------+-------+
                            |       |       |
                       non-polar  polar  hydrogen
                                          bonded
```

## 2.1 IONIC SOLIDS

```
  Particles at the lattice points:  POSITIVE and NEGATIVE IONS
  Binding force:                    strong ELECTROSTATIC (coulombic) force

    +  -  +  -  +          Na+ and Cl- alternate in every direction.
    -  +  -  +  -          Each is pulled by all its opposite neighbours.
    +  -  +  -  +
    -  +  -  +  -

  Hard and BRITTLE.  High melting point.
  In the SOLID state:  ions are locked  ->  INSULATOR.
  MOLTEN or in AQUEOUS solution: ions are free  ->  CONDUCTS.

  Examples:  NaCl , KCl , CaF2 , ZnS , MgO , CsCl
```

```
  WHY IONIC SOLIDS ARE BRITTLE  -  the shifted-layer picture

   BEFORE a blow:              AFTER a small shift of one layer:

   + - + - + - +               + - + - + - +
   - + - + - + -                 - + - + - + -      like charges now
   + - + - + - +               + - + - + - +        face each other
   - + - + - + -                 - + - + - + -      -> REPULSION
                                                    -> crystal cracks
```

## 2.2 COVALENT (NETWORK) SOLIDS

```
  Particles:      ATOMS
  Binding force:  COVALENT BONDS running through the WHOLE crystal
                  (the whole crystal is one giant molecule)

  Very hard, very high melting point, insulators.

  Examples:  DIAMOND , SILICON CARBIDE (SiC) , QUARTZ (SiO2) , AlN

  DIAMOND                             GRAPHITE
  every C is sp3, bonded to 4         every C is sp2, bonded to 3
  others in a 3-D network             others in flat sheets

        C                              C---C---C---C
       /|\                            /     \ /     \
      C C C   ... forever            C---C---C---C---C   <- one sheet
                                     ..............     <- weak van der
                                     C---C---C---C---C      Waals gap

  hardest known substance             SOFT and SLIPPERY
  NON-conductor                       CONDUCTS electricity
  (all 4 electrons are in bonds)      (the 4th electron is delocalised)
```

> **TRAP:** Graphite is a covalent solid but it **conducts** and it is **soft**.
> Both facts come from the same cause: only 3 of carbon's 4 valence electrons are
> used in bonding, and the sheets are held by weak forces so they slide.

## 2.3 MOLECULAR SOLIDS

The lattice points hold whole **molecules**. Three sub-types:

```
  (a) NON-POLAR MOLECULAR SOLIDS
      Molecules: non-polar (or single atoms)
      Force:     weak LONDON / DISPERSION forces
      Soft, very low melting point, gases or liquids at room temperature,
      insulators.
      Examples:  H2 , Cl2 , I2 , CO2 (dry ice) , solid Ar , CH4

  (b) POLAR MOLECULAR SOLIDS
      Molecules: polar (have a dipole)
      Force:     DIPOLE-DIPOLE attraction
      Soft, low melting point (higher than non-polar), insulators.
      Examples:  solid HCl , solid SO2 , solid NH3

           H-Cl  ...  H-Cl  ...  H-Cl
           d+ d-      d+ d-      d+ d-

  (c) HYDROGEN-BONDED MOLECULAR SOLIDS
      Force:     HYDROGEN BONDING (strongest of the three)
      Hard-ish, melting point highest of the molecular group,
      insulators (non-conductors).
      Examples:  ICE (H2O) , solid NH3 is borderline, HF

           O           the dotted line is the hydrogen bond
          / \
         H   H . . . O
                    / \
                   H   H
```

## 2.4 METALLIC SOLIDS

```
  Particles:      POSITIVE metal KERNELS (metal ions)
  Binding force:  METALLIC BOND = attraction between the kernels
                  and a SEA OF FREE ELECTRONS

     +   +   +   +          the dots are delocalised electrons
       .   .   .            free to move anywhere in the crystal
     +   +   +   +
       .   .   .
     +   +   +   +

  MALLEABLE and DUCTILE (layers slide without repulsion, because the
  electron sea keeps holding everything together).
  Good conductors of heat and electricity IN THE SOLID STATE.
  Lustrous. Melting points from low (Hg, Ga) to very high (W).

  Examples:  Cu , Ag , Fe , Al , Mg , all alloys
```

## THE BIG CLASSIFICATION TABLE — learn this, it is a 4-mark answer

| Type of solid | Constituent particles | Binding force | Physical nature | Melting point | Electrical conductivity | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IONIC** | Positive and negative ions | Strong electrostatic (coulombic) | Hard but brittle | High (800–2800 °C) | Insulator when solid; **conductor when molten or aqueous** | NaCl, KCl, MgO, CaF2, ZnS |
| **COVALENT (network)** | Atoms | Covalent bonds throughout the crystal | Very hard (graphite soft) | Very high (>1500 °C) | Insulator (**graphite is an exception — conducts**) | Diamond, SiC, SiO2, AlN, graphite |
| **MOLECULAR — non-polar** | Non-polar molecules / atoms | Weak London dispersion forces | Very soft | Very low | Insulator | H2, Cl2, I2, CO2, Ar |
| **MOLECULAR — polar** | Polar molecules | Dipole–dipole forces | Soft | Low | Insulator | solid HCl, SO2 |
| **MOLECULAR — H-bonded** | Molecules with H bonded to N/O/F | Hydrogen bonds | Hard-ish, brittle | Low but highest of the molecular family | Insulator | Ice (H2O), HF |
| **METALLIC** | Positive kernels + electron sea | Metallic bond | Malleable, ductile, lustrous | Low to very high | **Good conductor in solid AND molten state** | Cu, Ag, Fe, Mg, alloys |

### WORKED EXAMPLE 2.1
*Classify each and name the binding force: (a) SiC (b) I2 (c) P4 (d) Brass (e) Rb
(f) LiBr (g) Si (h) Plastic (i) Solid CO2 (j) Graphite.*

```
  (a) SiC        - COVALENT network      - covalent bonds
  (b) I2         - MOLECULAR non-polar   - London dispersion forces
  (c) P4         - MOLECULAR non-polar   - London dispersion forces
  (d) Brass      - METALLIC (an alloy)   - metallic bond
  (e) Rb         - METALLIC              - metallic bond
  (f) LiBr       - IONIC                 - electrostatic force
  (g) Si         - COVALENT network      - covalent bonds
  (h) Plastic    - AMORPHOUS             - (not crystalline at all)
  (i) Solid CO2  - MOLECULAR non-polar   - London dispersion forces
  (j) Graphite   - COVALENT network      - covalent + weak van der Waals
                                           between sheets
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 2.2
*Ionic solids conduct electricity in the molten state but not when solid. Why?*

```
  In the SOLID state the ions are held tightly at fixed lattice points
  by strong electrostatic forces, so they cannot move.  No moving
  charge  ->  no current.

  On MELTING (or dissolving in water) those forces are overcome, the
  ions become FREE to move towards the electrodes, and the melt
  conducts.
```

> **TRAP:** "Which solid conducts in the solid state?" — **metals** and **graphite**.
> Ionic solids conduct only when molten or dissolved.

# TOPIC 3 — CRYSTAL LATTICE AND UNIT CELL

## The tiles-on-a-floor analogy

Look at a tiled bathroom floor. You do not need to memorise the whole floor.
You memorise **one tile** and the rule "repeat it left–right and up–down".

```
  SPACE LATTICE / CRYSTAL LATTICE
      = the regular three dimensional arrangement of POINTS in space,
        where each point shows the position of a particle
        (atom, ion or molecule).
      = the whole tiled floor.

      Each point is a LATTICE POINT or LATTICE SITE.
      Every lattice point has EXACTLY the same surroundings.

  UNIT CELL
      = the SMALLEST repeating portion of the crystal lattice which,
        when repeated in three dimensions, generates the entire lattice.
      = ONE tile.
```

```
     A 2-D LATTICE                        THE UNIT CELL OF IT

     o   o   o   o   o                       o-------o
                                             |       |
     o   o   o   o   o                       |       |   <- repeat this
                                             |       |      in 2 directions
     o   o   o   o   o                       o-------o
                                             a
     o   o   o   o   o
```

## The six parameters of a unit cell

A unit cell is a box. To describe a box you need **three edge lengths** and
**three angles between those edges**.

```
                        z
                        |
                        |
                        c
                        |
                        |        gamma = angle between a and b
                        o------- b ------- y      alpha = angle between b and c
                       /  \                       beta  = angle between a and c
                      /    \
                     a   (alpha, beta, gamma are the
                    /      angles at the corner o)
                   x

  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |   EDGE LENGTHS (intercepts) :   a  ,  b  ,  c                  |
  |   ANGLES                    :   alpha , beta , gamma           |
  |                                                                |
  |      alpha  is between  b and c                                |
  |      beta   is between  a and c                                |
  |      gamma  is between  a and b                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+

  MEMORY:  each Greek letter sits OPPOSITE its own letter -
           alpha faces a, beta faces b, gamma faces c.
```

## Primitive and centred unit cells

```
  PRIMITIVE (simple) unit cell
      lattice points ONLY at the 8 corners.

  CENTRED unit cells - extra points besides the corners:

     BODY CENTRED (I)   -  corners + ONE point at the centre of the body
     FACE CENTRED (F)   -  corners + one point at the centre of EACH of
                           the 6 faces
     END CENTRED (C)    -  corners + one point at the centre of any TWO
                           OPPOSITE faces
```

## THE SEVEN CRYSTAL SYSTEMS AND THE FOURTEEN BRAVAIS LATTICES

There are only **7** possible box shapes, and only **14** distinct ways to put
lattice points into them. These 14 are the **Bravais lattices**.

| No. | Crystal system | Edge lengths | Angles | Possible lattices | Count | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cubic** | a = b = c | alpha = beta = gamma = 90° | Primitive, Body centred, Face centred | **3** | NaCl, ZnS, KCl, Cu, diamond |
| 2 | **Tetragonal** | a = b ≠ c | alpha = beta = gamma = 90° | Primitive, Body centred | **2** | White tin, SnO2, TiO2, CaSO4 |
| 3 | **Orthorhombic (Rhombic)** | a ≠ b ≠ c | alpha = beta = gamma = 90° | Primitive, Body centred, Face centred, End centred | **4** | Rhombic sulphur, KNO3, BaSO4 |
| 4 | **Hexagonal** | a = b ≠ c | alpha = beta = 90°, gamma = 120° | Primitive | **1** | Graphite, ZnO, CdS |
| 5 | **Rhombohedral (Trigonal)** | a = b = c | alpha = beta = gamma ≠ 90° | Primitive | **1** | Calcite CaCO3, HgS (cinnabar) |
| 6 | **Monoclinic** | a ≠ b ≠ c | alpha = gamma = 90°, beta ≠ 90° | Primitive, End centred | **2** | Monoclinic sulphur, Na2SO4.10H2O |
| 7 | **Triclinic** | a ≠ b ≠ c | alpha ≠ beta ≠ gamma ≠ 90° | Primitive | **1** | K2Cr2O7, CuSO4.5H2O, H3BO3 |
| | | | | **TOTAL** | **14** | |

```
  CHECK THE COUNT:   3 + 2 + 4 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1  =  14      correct.

  MEMORY for the counts:  "3 2 4 1 1 2 1"
                           C T O H R M T
                           (Cubic Tetragonal Orthorhombic Hexagonal
                            Rhombohedral Monoclinic Triclinic)
```

> **TRAP:** The **cubic** system is the MOST symmetric; the **triclinic** system is the
> LEAST symmetric. Orthorhombic is the only system with all four types (4 lattices).

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.1
*A crystal has a = b = c and alpha = beta = gamma = 90°. Name the system and say how
many Bravais lattices it has.*

```
  a = b = c and all angles 90 degrees  ->  CUBIC system.
  It has 3 Bravais lattices:  simple (primitive), body centred,
  face centred.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.2
*Name the crystal system for a = b ≠ c, alpha = beta = 90°, gamma = 120°. Give one example.*

```
  Two equal edges, two right angles and one 120 degree angle
  ->  HEXAGONAL system.   Example:  graphite  (or ZnO, CdS).
  It has only 1 Bravais lattice (primitive).
```

# TOPIC 4 — THE THREE CUBIC UNIT CELLS AND HOW MANY ATOMS EACH HOLDS

This is the **single most important counting skill** in the chapter.

## The sharing rule

An atom sitting on the boundary of a cube is **shared with the neighbouring cubes**.
Only its share belongs to this cell.

```
  +--------------------+---------------------+------------------+
  |  POSITION          |  SHARED BETWEEN     |  CONTRIBUTION    |
  |                    |  HOW MANY CELLS     |  TO ONE CELL     |
  +--------------------+---------------------+------------------+
  |  CORNER            |         8           |       1/8        |
  |  EDGE centre       |         4           |       1/4        |
  |  FACE centre       |         2           |       1/2        |
  |  BODY centre       |         1           |        1         |
  +--------------------+---------------------+------------------+

  WHY?  Stack cubes like sugar cubes.
        A corner is the meeting point of 8 cubes.
        An edge is shared by 4 cubes lying around that edge.
        A face is the wall between exactly 2 cubes.
        The body centre is inside one cube only.
```

## 4.1 SIMPLE CUBIC (SC) — also called primitive cubic

```
        o---------------o
       /|              /|
      / |             / |          Atoms ONLY at the 8 corners.
     o---------------o  |
     |  |            |  |          In a real crystal the corner atoms
     |  |            |  |          TOUCH along the EDGE.
     |  o------------|--o
     | /             | /
     |/              |/
     o---------------o

     |<------ a ---->|

  LOOKING AT ONE FACE (atoms drawn to size, touching along the edge):

        ( O )-------( O )
          |           |             a  =  r + r  =  2 r
          |           |
          |           |
        ( O )-------( O )

  ATOMS PER UNIT CELL

        Z  =  8 corners  x  1/8  =  1

  Coordination number = 6      (each atom touches 6 others:
                                left, right, front, back, up, down)
  Example: POLONIUM (Po) is the only element with a simple cubic structure.
```

## 4.2 BODY CENTRED CUBIC (BCC)

```
        o---------------o
       /|              /|
      / |             / |          8 corners + 1 atom at the exact
     o---------------o  |          CENTRE of the cube.
     |  |     *      |  |
     |  |  (centre)  |  |          Corner atoms do NOT touch each other.
     |  o------------|--o          They touch the BODY CENTRE atom, i.e.
     | /             | /           the atoms touch along the BODY DIAGONAL.
     |/              |/
     o---------------o

  THE BODY DIAGONAL (cut the cube from one corner to the opposite corner):

     O========o========O           r + 2r + r  =  4 r   =  body diagonal
     corner   body   corner
             centre                body diagonal  =  sqrt(3) x a

                                   so   sqrt(3) a  =  4 r
                                             4 r
                                   a  =  -----------
                                          sqrt(3)

  ATOMS PER UNIT CELL

        corners :  8  x  1/8  =  1
        body    :  1  x  1    =  1
                             -----
        Z       =              2

  Coordination number = 8
  Examples: Li , Na , K , Rb , Cs , Ba , Cr , Mo , W , alpha-Fe
```

## 4.3 FACE CENTRED CUBIC (FCC) — also called cubic close packed (CCP)

```
        o-------o-------o
       /|              /|
      o |     o       o |         8 corners
     /  |            /  |         + 1 atom at the centre of EACH of
    o-------o-------o   |           the 6 faces
    |   |     o     |   |
    |   o-------o---|---o         The atoms touch along the FACE DIAGONAL.
    |  /            |  /
    | o      o      | o
    |/              |/
    o-------o-------o

  ONE FACE, atoms drawn to size:

        ( O )---------------( O )
             \             /
               \  ( O )  /              face diagonal  =  r + 2r + r  =  4 r
               /         \
             /             \            face diagonal  =  sqrt(2) x a
        ( O )---------------( O )
                                        so   sqrt(2) a  =  4 r
                                                  4 r
                                        a  =  ----------- =  2 sqrt(2) r
                                               sqrt(2)

  ATOMS PER UNIT CELL

        corners :  8  x  1/8  =  1
        faces   :  6  x  1/2  =  3
                             -----
        Z       =              4

  Coordination number = 12
  Examples: Cu , Ag , Au , Al , Ni , Pt , Pb
```

## THE SUMMARY BOX — copy this onto your palm

```
  +--------+-------+-----------+---------------+---------+-------------+
  | TYPE   | corner| other     | Z = atoms per | C.N.    | a in terms  |
  |        |       | atoms     | unit cell     |         | of r        |
  +--------+-------+-----------+---------------+---------+-------------+
  | SC     | 8x1/8 |   none    |      1        |    6    | a = 2 r     |
  | BCC    | 8x1/8 | 1 body    |      2        |    8    | a = 4r/sqrt3|
  | FCC    | 8x1/8 | 6x1/2 face|      4        |   12    | a = 4r/sqrt2|
  | HCP    |   -   |     -     |      6        |   12    |      -      |
  +--------+-------+-----------+---------------+---------+-------------+
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.1
*Calculate the number of atoms per unit cell in a face centred cubic lattice.*

```
  Atoms at 8 corners, each shared by 8 cells:
        8  x  1/8  =  1

  Atoms at 6 face centres, each shared by 2 cells:
        6  x  1/2  =  3

  TOTAL   Z  =  1  +  3  =  4  atoms per unit cell
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.2
*A compound has atoms of element A at the corners of a cube and atoms of element B
at the body centre. What is the formula?*

```
  A  :  8 corners  x  1/8  =  1
  B  :  1 body     x  1    =  1

  A : B  =  1 : 1     ->   formula  AB
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.3
*Element A is at the corners and element B at the face centres. Write the formula.*

```
  A  :  8  x  1/8  =  1
  B  :  6  x  1/2  =  3

  A : B  =  1 : 3     ->   formula  AB3
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.4
*A cubic solid has A at the corners and B at the body centre. If one atom of A is
missing from one corner, what is the formula?*

```
  Only 7 corners now carry A:
      A  =  7  x  1/8  =  7/8
      B  =  1

  A : B  =  7/8 : 1  =  7 : 8      ->   formula  A7B8
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.5
*A cubic cell has atom X at the corners, Y at the face centres and Z at the body
centre. Find the formula.*

```
  X  =  8 x 1/8  =  1
  Y  =  6 x 1/2  =  3
  Z  =  1 x 1    =  1

  X : Y : Z  =  1 : 3 : 1    ->   formula  X Y3 Z
```

> **TRAP:** Do not count an atom at the corner as a whole atom. Every counting
> mistake in an exam comes from forgetting the 1/8, 1/4 and 1/2 shares.

# TOPIC 5 — CLOSE PACKED STRUCTURES

## The question being asked

If you drop identical marbles into a box and shake it, how do they settle?
Nature always chooses the arrangement that **wastes the least space** and gives
each ball the **most neighbours**.

## 5.1 CLOSE PACKING IN ONE DIMENSION

```
  A single row of touching spheres:

        ( O )( O )( O )( O )( O )( O )

  Each sphere touches 2 others  ->  COORDINATION NUMBER = 2
```

## 5.2 CLOSE PACKING IN TWO DIMENSIONS

Stack rows on top of each other. There are two ways.

```
  (a) SQUARE CLOSE PACKING   -  "AAAA type"
      Each row sits EXACTLY above the row below.

        ( O )( O )( O )( O )      <- row A
        ( O )( O )( O )( O )      <- row A  (exactly above)
        ( O )( O )( O )( O )      <- row A

      Each sphere touches 4 others   ->  C.N. = 4
      Packing efficiency  =  52.4 %
      The empty space is a SQUARE hole.

              O-----O
              |     |     <- square void
              O-----O


  (b) HEXAGONAL CLOSE PACKING  -  "ABAB type"
      Each row sits in the DEPRESSIONS of the row below.

        ( O )( O )( O )( O )         <- row A
           ( O )( O )( O )           <- row B, pushed into the hollows
        ( O )( O )( O )( O )         <- row A

      Each sphere touches 6 others   ->  C.N. = 6
      Packing efficiency  =  60.4 %
      The empty space is a TRIANGULAR void.

              O
             / \        <- triangular void
            O---O

      THIS IS THE MORE EFFICIENT ONE.  Nature chooses it.
```

## 5.3 CLOSE PACKING IN THREE DIMENSIONS

Now stack the 2-D hexagonal layers on one another.

```
  LAYER A - a hexagonal close packed layer seen from above.
  Its triangular voids point two different ways:

        O       O       O
            a       b                a = void pointing UP     (tetrahedral
        O       O       O                                      site above)
            b       a                b = void pointing DOWN
        O       O       O

  Put LAYER B into the "a" voids. Now some of the "b" voids of layer A
  are still open, and B has created new voids of its own.

  When you place the THIRD layer you have TWO CHOICES:
```

```
  CHOICE 1 - HEXAGONAL CLOSE PACKING (hcp)     pattern  A B A B A B ...

      layer C is placed EXACTLY above layer A.

          A     O   O   O   O
                 \ /     \ /
          B       O       O
                 / \     / \
          A     O   O   O   O          <- identical to the first A layer
                 \ /     \ /
          B       O       O
                 / \     / \
          A     O   O   O   O

      Coordination number  =  12
      Packing efficiency   =  74 %
      Unit cell            =  HEXAGONAL,   Z = 6
      Examples:  Mg , Zn , Cd , Be , Ti , Co


  CHOICE 2 - CUBIC CLOSE PACKING (ccp) = FACE CENTRED CUBIC (fcc)
             pattern  A B C A B C ...

      layer C is placed over the voids that are still UNCOVERED,
      so it is above NEITHER A nor B. Only the fourth layer repeats A.

          A     O   O   O   O
          B       O   O   O
          C         O   O   O          <- a NEW position
          A     O   O   O   O          <- now it repeats

      Coordination number  =  12
      Packing efficiency   =  74 %
      Unit cell            =  FACE CENTRED CUBIC,   Z = 4
      Examples:  Cu , Ag , Au , Al , Ni , Pt
```

```
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  hcp  and  ccp  have the SAME coordination number (12) and the    |
  |  SAME packing efficiency (74 %).  They differ ONLY in the         |
  |  STACKING SEQUENCE:                                               |
  |                                                                   |
  |        hcp  ->  A B A B A B                                       |
  |        ccp  ->  A B C A B C                                       |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

```
  WHY IS THE COORDINATION NUMBER 12 ?

     6 neighbours in its OWN layer
     3 neighbours in the layer ABOVE
     3 neighbours in the layer BELOW
     --------------------------------
    12 neighbours in total

              o   o   o        <- 3 above
            \  |  /
        o - ( O ) - o          <- 6 in the same layer
            /  |  \
              o   o   o        <- 3 below
```

## Summary of the packing types

| Packing | Sequence | Coordination number | Packing efficiency | Free space | Unit cell / Z | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-D row | — | 2 | — | — | — | — |
| 2-D square | AAA | 4 | 52.4 % | 47.6 % | — | — |
| 2-D hexagonal | ABAB | 6 | 60.4 % | 39.6 % | — | — |
| Simple cubic (3-D) | AAAA | 6 | 52.4 % | 47.6 % | SC, Z = 1 | Po |
| Body centred cubic | — | 8 | 68 % | 32 % | BCC, Z = 2 | Na, K, Fe, Cr, W |
| **hcp** | **ABAB** | **12** | **74 %** | **26 %** | Hexagonal, Z = 6 | Mg, Zn, Cd |
| **ccp / fcc** | **ABCABC** | **12** | **74 %** | **26 %** | FCC, Z = 4 | Cu, Ag, Au, Al |

> **TRAP:** "Which has the highest packing efficiency?" — hcp **and** ccp, both 74%.
> If the options include both, and only one may be chosen, the question usually
> says "cubic", which means **ccp / fcc**.

# TOPIC 6 — PACKING EFFICIENCY (THE DERIVATIONS)

```
                        volume occupied by the atoms in the unit cell
  packing efficiency =  --------------------------------------------  x 100
                              total volume of the unit cell


                        Z  x  (4/3) pi r^3
                     =  --------------------  x  100
                                a^3
```

Everything depends on writing **a in terms of r**, which you already know.

## 6.1 SIMPLE CUBIC

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Z = 1                                                              |
  |  Atoms touch along the EDGE  ->   a = 2 r                           |
  |                                                                     |
  |  volume of atoms  =  1  x  (4/3) pi r^3                             |
  |  volume of cell   =  a^3  =  (2r)^3  =  8 r^3                       |
  |                                                                     |
  |                       (4/3) pi r^3                                  |
  |  P.E.  =  100  x  ---------------------                             |
  |                          8 r^3                                      |
  |                                                                     |
  |                        4 pi              pi                         |
  |        =  100  x  -------------  =  100 x ---                       |
  |                     3 x 8                  6                        |
  |                                                                     |
  |                     3.1416                                          |
  |        =  100  x  ----------  =  52.36 %   approx  52.4 %           |
  |                       6                                             |
  |                                                                     |
  |  EMPTY SPACE  =  47.6 %                                             |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 6.2 BODY CENTRED CUBIC

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Z = 2                                                              |
  |  Atoms touch along the BODY DIAGONAL.                               |
  |                                                                     |
  |  face diagonal  b  =  sqrt( a^2 + a^2 )  =  sqrt(2) a               |
  |  body diagonal  c  =  sqrt( b^2 + a^2 )                             |
  |                    =  sqrt( 2a^2 + a^2 )  =  sqrt(3) a              |
  |                                                                     |
  |  Along the body diagonal there are 3 atoms in a line:               |
  |        corner (r) + body centre (2r) + corner (r)  =  4 r           |
  |                                                                     |
  |        sqrt(3) a  =  4 r          ->      a  =  4r / sqrt(3)        |
  |                                                                     |
  |  volume of atoms  =  2  x  (4/3) pi r^3   =  (8/3) pi r^3           |
  |                              4 r  3        64 r^3                   |
  |  volume of cell   =  a^3 = ( ------ )  =  ---------                 |
  |                             sqrt(3)       3 sqrt(3)                 |
  |                                                                     |
  |                      (8/3) pi r^3                                   |
  |  P.E.  =  100  x  --------------------                              |
  |                    64 r^3 / (3 sqrt3)                               |
  |                                                                     |
  |                     8 pi       3 sqrt(3)                            |
  |        =  100  x  -------  x  -----------                           |
  |                      3             64                               |
  |                                                                     |
  |                     8 pi sqrt(3)          pi sqrt(3)                |
  |        =  100  x  ---------------  =  100 x ----------              |
  |                         64                       8                  |
  |                                                                     |
  |                     3.1416 x 1.732                                  |
  |        =  100  x  -----------------  =  100 x 0.6802  =  68.02 %    |
  |                          8                                          |
  |                                                                     |
  |  EMPTY SPACE  =  32 %                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 6.3 FACE CENTRED CUBIC (and hcp — same answer)

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Z = 4                                                              |
  |  Atoms touch along the FACE DIAGONAL.                               |
  |                                                                     |
  |  face diagonal  =  sqrt(2) a                                        |
  |  Along it there are 3 atoms:  r + 2r + r  =  4 r                    |
  |                                                                     |
  |        sqrt(2) a  =  4 r      ->    a  =  4r/sqrt(2)  = 2 sqrt(2) r |
  |                                                                     |
  |  volume of atoms  =  4 x (4/3) pi r^3  =  (16/3) pi r^3             |
  |  volume of cell   =  a^3  =  ( 2 sqrt2 r )^3  =  16 sqrt(2) r^3     |
  |                                                                     |
  |                     (16/3) pi r^3           pi                      |
  |  P.E.  =  100  x  -------------------  =  ----------- x 100         |
  |                    16 sqrt(2) r^3          3 sqrt(2)                |
  |                                                                     |
  |                     3.1416                                          |
  |        =  100  x  -----------  =  100 x 0.7405  =  74.05 %          |
  |                    4.2426                                           |
  |                                                                     |
  |  EMPTY SPACE  =  26 %                                               |
  |                                                                     |
  |  hcp gives EXACTLY the same 74 %.                                   |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## The three answers, in one line

```
   SC  52.4 %   <   BCC  68 %   <   FCC = HCP  74 %

   free space:  47.6 %     32 %          26 %
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 6.1
*Silver crystallises in the fcc structure with r = 144.5 pm. Calculate the edge length
and the volume of the unit cell.*

```
  For FCC:      a  =  2 sqrt(2) r  =  2 x 1.4142 x 144.5

                a  =  2.8284 x 144.5  =  408.7 pm

  volume  =  a^3  =  ( 408.7 x 10^-10 cm )^3
                  =  ( 4.087 x 10^-8 )^3
                  =  68.27 x 10^-24  =  6.827 x 10^-23  cm^3
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 6.2
*Chromium has a bcc structure with a = 288 pm. Find the atomic radius.*

```
  For BCC:      a  =  4 r / sqrt(3)      ->     r  =  sqrt(3) a / 4

                r  =  ( 1.732 x 288 ) / 4

                r  =  498.8 / 4  =  124.7 pm
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 6.3
*Calculate the percentage of free space in a body centred cubic crystal.*

```
  Packing efficiency of BCC  =  68 %

  Free space  =  100 - 68  =  32 %
```

# TOPIC 7 — VOIDS (HOLES) IN CLOSE PACKED STRUCTURES

Even the best packing leaves **26% empty space**. That space is not one big hole;
it is many tiny holes of two shapes.

## 7.1 TETRAHEDRAL VOID

```
  Formed when ONE sphere sits in the hollow made by THREE spheres
  of the layer below.  4 spheres in total surround the hole, and
  their centres form a TETRAHEDRON.

           ( O )                <- one sphere on top
          /  |  \
         /   .   \              the dot is the VOID
        /  /   \  \
     ( O )-----( O )
          \     /
           ( O )                <- three spheres below

  Coordination number of the void  =  4
  It is a SMALL hole.

  SIZE:      r(void) / r(sphere)  =  0.225
             so   r(void)  =  0.225 x r
```

## 7.2 OCTAHEDRAL VOID

```
  Formed between TWO sets of THREE spheres - a triangle pointing one way
  in the lower layer and a triangle pointing the other way above it.
  6 spheres surround the hole and their centres form an OCTAHEDRON.

           ( O )-----( O )
             \   .   /               upper triangle (3 spheres)
              \     /
               ( O )
                 .    <- the VOID sits in the middle
               ( O )
              /     \                lower triangle (3 spheres)
             /       \
           ( O )-----( O )

  Coordination number of the void  =  6
  It is a BIGGER hole than the tetrahedral void.

  SIZE:      r(void) / r(sphere)  =  0.414
             so   r(void)  =  0.414 x r
```

## 7.3 HOW MANY VOIDS?

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |   If a close packed structure contains  N  spheres, then            |
  |                                                                     |
  |        number of OCTAHEDRAL voids   =   N        (1 per sphere)     |
  |        number of TETRAHEDRAL voids  =   2 N      (2 per sphere)     |
  |                                                                     |
  |        total number of voids        =   3 N                         |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+

  In an FCC unit cell (Z = 4):
        octahedral voids   =  4     (1 at the body centre + 12 edges x 1/4)
        tetrahedral voids  =  8     (one in each of the 8 small cubes)
```

```
  WHERE THEY SIT IN AN FCC CELL

  OCTAHEDRAL voids:                      TETRAHEDRAL voids:

     o-----+-----o                        o-----------o
     |           |                        | .   .     |     divide the cube
     +     *     +      * = body          |           |     into 8 small cubes;
     |           |          centre        |   .   .   |     the CENTRE of each
     o-----+-----o      + = edge          |           |     small cube is a
                            centres       o-----------o     tetrahedral void

     1  +  12 x 1/4  =  4                     8 small cubes  =  8 voids
```

## 7.4 USING VOIDS TO FIND A FORMULA

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.1
*Atoms of element B form an hcp lattice and atoms of A occupy 2/3 of the
tetrahedral voids. What is the formula?*

```
  Let there be  N  atoms of B.
  Tetrahedral voids  =  2 N
  A occupies 2/3 of them:      A  =  (2/3) x 2N  =  4N/3

  A : B   =   4N/3  :  N   =   4 : 3

  FORMULA:   A4 B3
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.2
*In a compound, oxide ions form a ccp lattice and metal M occupies all the
octahedral voids. Write the formula.*

```
  ccp of O2-  ->  Z = 4 oxide ions per cell
  Octahedral voids  =  4,  all filled by M   ->  4 M ions

  M : O  =  4 : 4  =  1 : 1     ->    formula   MO
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.3
*A crystal has X in ccp and Y in ALL the tetrahedral voids. Formula?*

```
  X (ccp)             =  4
  Tetrahedral voids   =  8,  all occupied by Y

  X : Y  =  4 : 8  =  1 : 2     ->    formula   X Y2
                                       (this is the fluorite, CaF2, type)
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.4
*Ferric oxide has O2- in hcp and Fe3+ in two-thirds of the octahedral voids.
Show that the formula is Fe2O3.*

```
  Let O2- ions  =  N
  Octahedral voids  =  N
  Fe3+ fills 2/3 of them:   Fe  =  (2/3) N

  Fe : O  =  (2/3)N : N  =  2 : 3      ->    Fe2 O3      (proved)
```

> **TRAP:** Tetrahedral voids are **twice** as many as the spheres but **smaller**.
> Octahedral voids are **equal** in number to the spheres but **bigger**.
> Students swap these constantly. Memory line:
> **"TETRA is TWO in number but TINY; OCTA is ONE in number but OPEN."**

# TOPIC 8 — RADIUS RATIO RULE

## The idea

A cation must sit in a hole made by the anions. If the cation is **too small** it
rattles about and the structure is unstable; if it is **bigger** it can push the
anions apart and take more neighbours.

```
                       radius of the CATION          r(+)
  RADIUS RATIO   =   -----------------------  =   --------
                       radius of the ANION           r(-)
```

## The table — this is a straight 2-mark or 4-mark question

| Radius ratio r(+)/r(−) | Coordination number | Shape of the void / geometry | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.155 – 0.225 | **3** | Trigonal planar | B2O3 |
| 0.225 – 0.414 | **4** | **Tetrahedral** | ZnS, CuCl |
| 0.414 – 0.732 | **6** | **Octahedral** | NaCl, MgO, KBr |
| 0.732 – 1.000 | **8** | **Body centred cubic** | CsCl, CsBr, NH4Cl |

```
  MEMORY LADDER - each limit and the C.N. it unlocks

      0.155  ->  3       (triangle)
      0.225  ->  4       (tetrahedron)
      0.414  ->  6       (octahedron)
      0.732  ->  8       (cube)

  Notice 0.225 and 0.414 are exactly the tetrahedral and octahedral
  VOID sizes from Topic 7. They are the SAME numbers.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 8.1
*Given r(Na+) = 95 pm and r(Cl−) = 181 pm, predict the coordination number and
the structure of NaCl.*

```
   r(+)        95
  ------  =  -------  =  0.525
   r(-)       181

  0.525 lies between 0.414 and 0.732
       ->  coordination number  =  6
       ->  OCTAHEDRAL arrangement
       ->  rock salt (NaCl) structure.     CORRECT.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 8.2
*r(Cs+) = 169 pm, r(Cl−) = 181 pm. Predict the structure of CsCl.*

```
   169 / 181  =  0.933

  0.933 lies between 0.732 and 1.000
       ->  coordination number  =  8
       ->  BODY CENTRED CUBIC arrangement (CsCl type).
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 8.3
*r(Zn2+) = 74 pm, r(S2−) = 184 pm. Which void does Zn2+ occupy?*

```
   74 / 184  =  0.402

  0.402 lies between 0.225 and 0.414
       ->  coordination number  =  4
       ->  Zn2+ occupies a TETRAHEDRAL void.   (zinc blende structure)
```

> **TRAP:** Always put the **cation on top** of the fraction. Flipping it gives a
> number greater than 1 and a wrong prediction.

# TOPIC 9 — DENSITY OF A UNIT CELL

This is the **guaranteed numerical** of the chapter. Learn the derivation, then
drill it.

## The derivation

```
  mass of one unit cell  =  (number of atoms in it) x (mass of one atom)

                                                       M
       mass of ONE atom  =  --------------------  =  ------
                            Avogadro's number          NA

  So   mass of unit cell  =  Z  x  M / NA

       volume of unit cell (cubic)  =  a^3

                 mass            Z  x  M
       density = -------  =  ----------------
                volume         a^3  x  NA
```

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |                              Z  x  M                                |
  |                     d  =  ----------------                          |
  |                             a^3  x  NA                              |
  |                                                                     |
  |     d   density              g / cm^3                               |
  |     Z   atoms per unit cell  (SC = 1, BCC = 2, FCC = 4)             |
  |     M   molar / atomic mass  g / mol                                |
  |     a   edge length          cm                                     |
  |     NA  6.022 x 10^23        / mol                                  |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+

  UNIT CONVERSIONS YOU WILL NEED EVERY SINGLE TIME:

        1 pm  =  10^-12 m  =  10^-10 cm
        1 A   =  10^-10 m  =  10^-8  cm
        1 nm  =  10^-9  m  =  10^-7  cm

        so    a = 400 pm    ->    a = 400 x 10^-10 cm = 4.0 x 10^-8 cm
```

## The four ways this is asked

```
  1.  Given a, Z, M   ->  find  d          d  =  Z M / (a^3 NA)
  2.  Given d, a, M   ->  find  Z          Z  =  d a^3 NA / M
                          (then name the structure: 1=SC, 2=BCC, 4=FCC)
  3.  Given d, Z, a   ->  find  M          M  =  d a^3 NA / Z
  4.  Given d, Z, M   ->  find  a          a^3 = Z M / (d NA), then cube root
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 9.1  (type 1 — find the density)
*Copper crystallises in an fcc lattice with edge length 361 pm. Atomic mass of Cu
is 63.5 u. Calculate its density.*

```
  STEP 1   fcc  ->  Z = 4
  STEP 2   a  =  361 pm  =  361 x 10^-10 cm  =  3.61 x 10^-8 cm
  STEP 3   a^3  =  ( 3.61 x 10^-8 )^3
                =  47.05 x 10^-24
                =  4.705 x 10^-23  cm^3

  STEP 4          Z M            4  x  63.5
           d  = ---------  =  ---------------------------------
                 a^3 NA        ( 4.705 x 10^-23 )( 6.022 x 10^23 )

                     254              254
              =  -----------  =   ----------   =   8.97  g/cm^3
                 4.705 x 6.022      28.33

  ANSWER:  8.97 g/cm^3      (the true density of copper is 8.96 - correct)
```

```
  Number of Au atoms in 1 g  =  ( 1 / 197 ) x 6.022 x 10^23
                             =  3.057 x 10^21  atoms

  In fcc, each unit cell contains 4 atoms.

  Number of unit cells  =  3.057 x 10^21 / 4  =  7.64 x 10^20  cells
```

> **TRAP 1:** Convert **pm to cm**, not to metres. Using metres gives an answer
> 10^6 times too small. `1 pm = 10^-10 cm`.
>
> **TRAP 2:** For an ionic compound such as NaCl, Z counts **formula units**, not
> atoms, and M is the **molar mass of the formula unit** (58.5, not 23 or 35.5).
>
> **TRAP 3:** Z must come out as a whole number. If you get 1.99 write **2**;
> if you get 3.96 write **4**. A value like 2.7 means you made an arithmetic slip.

# TOPIC 10 — IMPERFECTIONS (DEFECTS) IN SOLIDS

## Why do defects exist?

```
  A crystal is PERFECT only at 0 K (absolute zero).

  Above 0 K the particles vibrate, and some of them get enough energy
  to leave their places. So EVERY real crystal has defects.

  Slow crystallisation  ->  fewer defects
  Fast crystallisation  ->  more defects

  Rise in TEMPERATURE   ->  MORE defects  (defects are entropy-driven)
```

```
                        DEFECTS
                           |
              +------------+------------+
              |                         |
         POINT DEFECTS            LINE DEFECTS
       (around a single           (whole rows of
        lattice point)             points misplaced)
              |
   +----------+-----------+-------------+
   |          |                         |
 STOICHIO-  NON-STOICHIO-           IMPURITY
 METRIC     METRIC                  DEFECTS
   |          |                     (doping)
   |          +-- metal EXCESS
   |          |     (a) anion vacancy -> F-CENTRE
   |          |     (b) extra cation in interstitial site
   |          |
   |          +-- metal DEFICIENCY
   |
   +-- VACANCY defect     (a particle missing, non-ionic solids)
   +-- INTERSTITIAL defect(an extra particle in a gap)
   +-- SCHOTTKY defect    (ionic solids)
   +-- FRENKEL defect     (ionic solids)
```

## 10.1 SIMPLE DEFECTS IN NON-IONIC SOLIDS

```
  VACANCY DEFECT                      INTERSTITIAL DEFECT

   o  o  o  o  o                       o  o  o  o  o
   o  o     o  o   <- one site         o  o  o  o  o
   o  o  o  o  o      is empty         o  o o o  o  o  <- an extra particle
   o  o  o  o  o                       o  o  o  o  o      squeezed into a gap

   DENSITY DECREASES                   DENSITY INCREASES
```

## 10.2 STOICHIOMETRIC DEFECTS

"Stoichiometric" means the **ratio of cations to anions stays exactly as the
formula says**. The compound is still NaCl, still 1 : 1.

### (a) SCHOTTKY DEFECT

```
  A PAIR of ions - one cation AND one anion - is MISSING from
  the lattice, so electrical neutrality is preserved.

     +  -  +  -  +  -               +  -  +  -  +  -
     -  +  -  +  -  +               -  +     +  -  +      <- Cl- missing
     +  -  +  -  +  -      ---->    +  -  +     +  -      <- Na+ missing
     -  +  -  +  -  +               -  +  -  +  -  +
     +  -  +  -  +  -               +  -  +  -  +  -

     PERFECT CRYSTAL                SCHOTTKY DEFECT
                                    (2 holes, still neutral)

  IT IS BASICALLY A VACANCY DEFECT IN AN IONIC SOLID.

  CONDITIONS THAT FAVOUR IT
     - HIGH coordination number
     - The cation and the anion are of NEARLY THE SAME SIZE
       (small difference in ionic radii)

  CONSEQUENCES
     - DENSITY DECREASES  (same volume, fewer ions -> less mass)
     - Lattice energy and stability decrease
     - Electrical conductivity increases slightly (ions can hop
       into the holes)

  EXAMPLES:   NaCl , KCl , KBr , CsCl , AgBr
```

### (b) FRENKEL DEFECT (also called DISLOCATION DEFECT)

```
  The SMALLER ion (almost always the CATION) leaves its own lattice
  site and squeezes into an INTERSTITIAL space nearby.
  Nothing leaves the crystal.

     +  -  +  -  +  -               +  -  +  -  +  -
     -  +  -  +  -  +               -  +  -  +  -  +
     +  -  +  -  +  -      ---->    +  -     -  +  -   <- cation left this site
     -  +  -  +  -  +               -  + (+) +  -  +   <- and sits in a gap
     +  -  +  -  +  -               +  -  +  -  +  -

     PERFECT CRYSTAL                FRENKEL DEFECT

  CONDITIONS THAT FAVOUR IT
     - LOW coordination number
     - LARGE difference in size between the cation and the anion
       (a small cation and a big anion)

  CONSEQUENCES
     - DENSITY REMAINS UNCHANGED  (no ion has left the crystal)
     - The DIELECTRIC CONSTANT INCREASES (two like charges have come
       close together, creating a dipole)
     - Electrical conductivity increases slightly

  EXAMPLES:   ZnS , AgCl , AgBr , AgI
```

## SCHOTTKY vs FRENKEL — the table they ask for

| Point | SCHOTTKY DEFECT | FRENKEL DEFECT |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | An equal number of cations **and** anions are **missing** | A smaller ion **moves** from its site into an **interstitial** site |
| Also called | Vacancy defect in ionic solids | Dislocation defect |
| Coordination number | **High** | **Low** |
| Size difference of ions | **Small** (ions nearly equal in size) | **Large** (small cation, large anion) |
| Effect on density | **DECREASES** | **NO CHANGE** |
| Effect on dielectric constant | No appreciable change | **Increases** |
| Electrical neutrality | Preserved | Preserved |
| Examples | NaCl, KCl, KBr, CsCl, AgBr | ZnS, AgCl, AgBr, AgI |

```
  AgBr shows BOTH Schottky and Frenkel defects.
  This is a favourite one-mark trap.
```

## 10.3 NON-STOICHIOMETRIC DEFECTS

Here the ratio of cations to anions is **no longer exactly** what the formula says.

### (a) METAL EXCESS DEFECT

**Route 1 — by an ANION VACANCY (this is where F-CENTRES come from)**

```
  An anion is missing from its site. To keep the crystal neutral, an
  ELECTRON occupies the empty anion site.

     Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-               Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-
     Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+       --->    Cl- Na+  e-  Na+    <- an electron sits
     Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-               Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-        in the Cl- vacancy

  That trapped electron is called an  F-CENTRE
  (F from the German "Farbenzentre" = colour centre).

  HOW IT HAPPENS:  heat NaCl in an atmosphere of sodium vapour.

       Na (vapour)  --->  Na+  (on the crystal surface)  +  e-
       the electron diffuses in and fills a Cl- vacancy

  Result:  the crystal now has EXCESS SODIUM  ->  Na(1+x) Cl

  WHY THE COLOUR?
       The trapped electron absorbs energy from visible light and
       gets excited. The colour we see is the light that is left over.

       NaCl  heated in Na vapour   ->   YELLOW
       KCl   heated in K  vapour   ->   VIOLET (lilac)
       LiCl  heated in Li vapour   ->   PINK

  These crystals also conduct electricity (the trapped electron can
  move) - they behave as n-type semiconductors.
```

**Route 2 — by an EXTRA CATION in an interstitial site**

```
  An extra cation squeezes into an interstitial site, and an electron
  goes into another interstitial site to balance the charge.

  CLASSIC EXAMPLE:  ZINC OXIDE

       ZnO is WHITE at room temperature.
       On HEATING it turns YELLOW, and on cooling it becomes white again.

       ZnO  --(heat)-->  Zn2+  +  (1/2) O2  +  2 e-

       The Zn2+ ions go into interstitial sites and the electrons go
       into neighbouring interstitial sites.
       Formula becomes  Zn(1+x) O   - excess zinc.
       The free electrons make it an n-type semiconductor.
```

### (b) METAL DEFICIENCY DEFECT

```
  Some cation sites are EMPTY, and the missing positive charge is made
  up by a NEIGHBOURING cation taking a HIGHER oxidation state.

  CLASSIC EXAMPLE:  FERROUS OXIDE, FeO

       Real FeO is almost always  Fe(0.95) O  -  never exactly FeO.

       For every 3 Fe2+ ions that are removed, 2 Fe3+ ions take their
       place, keeping the charge balanced but leaving one hole:

            3 Fe2+   ->   2 Fe3+   +   1 vacant cation site
            (6+)          (6+)

       The composition can range from Fe(0.93)O to Fe(0.96)O.

       The "holes" allow conduction  ->  p-TYPE semiconductor.
```

## 10.4 IMPURITY DEFECTS (DOPING)

```
  A foreign ion of DIFFERENT charge replaces a host ion. Vacancies
  appear to keep the crystal neutral.

  EXAMPLE:  molten NaCl crystallised with a little SrCl2

       One  Sr2+  replaces  TWO  Na+ ions,
       but it occupies only ONE of those two sites.

       Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-               Na+ Cl- Sr2+ Cl-
       Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+       --->    Cl-  X  Cl- Na+     X = cation vacancy
       Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-               Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-

       RESULT:  the number of CATION VACANCIES created is EQUAL to
                the number of Sr2+ ions added.

  The same happens with CdCl2 added to AgCl.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.1
*NaCl is doped with 10^-3 mol % of SrCl2. What is the concentration of cation
vacancies?*

```
  Each Sr2+ creates ONE cation vacancy.

  10^-3 mol % means:
        in 100 mol of NaCl there are 10^-3 mol of SrCl2
        in   1 mol of NaCl there are 10^-5 mol of SrCl2

  Number of Sr2+ ions in 1 mol NaCl
        =  10^-5  x  6.022 x 10^23
        =  6.022 x 10^18

  So the number of cation vacancies  =  6.022 x 10^18  per mole.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.2
*Which defect lowers the density of a crystal, and why?*

```
  The SCHOTTKY defect.

  Reason:  a cation and an anion actually LEAVE the crystal, so the
  mass of the unit cell falls while the volume stays the same.
        d = mass / volume    ->    mass down, volume same  ->  d falls.

  In the Frenkel defect nothing leaves the crystal, so the mass is
  unchanged and the density does NOT change.
```

> **TRAP:** "Which defect changes the density?" The answer is **Schottky (decreases)**.
> Frenkel = **no change**. Interstitial (extra atom pushed in) = **increases**.

# TOPIC 11 — ELECTRICAL PROPERTIES AND BAND THEORY

## The three classes by conductivity

```
  +----------------+------------------------------+
  |  CONDUCTORS    |  10^4  to  10^7   ohm-1 m-1  |   metals, electrolytes
  |  SEMICONDUCTORS|  10^-6 to  10^4   ohm-1 m-1  |   Si , Ge
  |  INSULATORS    |  10^-20 to 10^-10 ohm-1 m-1  |   wood, rubber, sulphur
  +----------------+------------------------------+
```

## Band theory in one picture

When many atoms come together, their atomic orbitals merge into **bands** of
closely spaced levels.

```
  VALENCE BAND     - the band filled with the valence electrons
  CONDUCTION BAND  - the empty band above it
  FORBIDDEN GAP    - the energy gap between them (also "band gap")

  CONDUCTOR (metal)      SEMICONDUCTOR          INSULATOR

  ///////////////        ///////////////        ///////////////
  // conduction //       // conduction //       // conduction //
  ///////////////        ///////////////        ///////////////
  ===============              |                       |
  OVERLAP or               SMALL GAP                  |
  PARTLY FILLED            ~ 0.5 to 3 eV          LARGE GAP
  ===============              |                  > 3 eV (diamond ~ 6)
  \\\\\\\\\\\\\\\              |                       |
  \\  valence  \\        \\\\\\\\\\\\\\\        \\\\\\\\\\\\\\\
  \\\\\\\\\\\\\\\        \\  valence  \\        \\  valence  \\
                         \\\\\\\\\\\\\\\        \\\\\\\\\\\\\\\

  electrons flow          a few electrons        no electron can
  freely                  jump across when       cross the gap
                          heated                 -> no conduction
```

```
  THE TEMPERATURE TEST - a favourite question

     METAL          :  conductivity DECREASES on heating
                       (kernels vibrate more and block the electrons)

     SEMICONDUCTOR  :  conductivity INCREASES on heating
                       (more electrons get enough energy to cross the gap)
```

## Intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors

```
  INTRINSIC SEMICONDUCTOR
      A PURE substance (Si or Ge) that conducts only slightly because
      a few electrons cross the small gap by thermal excitation.
      Very poor conductivity - not useful on its own.

  EXTRINSIC SEMICONDUCTOR
      An intrinsic semiconductor DOPED with a tiny amount of an
      impurity to raise its conductivity enormously.

  DOPING  =  adding a controlled trace of an impurity element to a
             pure semiconductor.
```

## n-TYPE semiconductor (dope with GROUP 15)

```
  Silicon is in GROUP 14 - it has 4 valence electrons and uses all 4
  in bonds. Now put in a group 15 atom (P, As, Sb) which has 5.

           Si     Si     Si                4 of P's electrons form bonds
             \   /  \   /                  the 5th has NOTHING to bond to
              Si -- P -- Si                -> it is FREE to move
             /   \ *|*  \                  ( * = the free electron )
           Si     Si     Si

  The extra FREE ELECTRON carries the current.
  The current carriers are NEGATIVE  ->  n-TYPE.

  Dopants:  P , As , Sb , Bi   (group 15)
```

## p-TYPE semiconductor (dope with GROUP 13)

```
  Now put in a group 13 atom (B, Al, Ga, In) which has only 3
  valence electrons.

           Si     Si     Si                only 3 bonds can form
             \   /  \   /                  the 4th position is EMPTY
              Si -- B -- Si                -> a HOLE
             /   \  o|   \                 ( o = the electron hole )
           Si     Si     Si

  A neighbouring electron hops into the hole, which moves the hole
  the other way. A moving hole behaves like a POSITIVE charge.
  The current carriers are POSITIVE  ->  p-TYPE.

  Dopants:  B , Al , Ga , In   (group 13)
```

```
  MEMORY:   group 15 = FIVE electrons = one to spare = negative = n-type
            group 13 = THREE electrons = one short  = hole     = p-type

            "15 gives, 13 takes."
```

## 13–15 and 12–16 compound semiconductors

```
  The AVERAGE number of valence electrons must come to 4, like silicon.

  13-15 COMPOUNDS   (a group 13 element + a group 15 element)
        (3 + 5) / 2  =  4                    ->  behaves like Si
        Examples:  InSb , AlP , GaAs , GaP

        GaAs is used in LEDs and in very fast transistors.

  12-16 COMPOUNDS   (a group 12 element + a group 16 element)
        (2 + 6) / 2  =  4
        Examples:  ZnS , CdS , CdSe , HgTe
        These have some IONIC character because of the larger
        electronegativity difference.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.1
*What type of semiconductor is obtained when silicon is doped with arsenic?*

```
  Arsenic (As) is in group 15  ->  5 valence electrons
  ->  one spare electron  ->  n-TYPE semiconductor.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.2
*Explain why ZnO becomes yellow on heating and conducts electricity.*

```
  ZnO --(heat)--> Zn2+ + (1/2) O2 + 2 e-

  Zinc ions enter interstitial sites and the released electrons
  occupy neighbouring interstitial sites. This is a METAL EXCESS
  defect. The trapped electrons absorb visible light, so the solid
  turns YELLOW, and because those electrons are free to move,
  ZnO becomes an n-TYPE semiconductor.
```

> **TRAP:** In n-type the crystal is still **electrically neutral** overall.
> "n" refers to the sign of the **charge carriers**, not to a net charge.

# TOPIC 12 — MAGNETIC PROPERTIES

Every electron is a tiny magnet, because a spinning charge creates a magnetic
moment. What the whole solid does depends on how those tiny magnets line up.

```
  Magnetic moment comes from
      (a) the SPIN of the electron
      (b) the ORBITAL motion of the electron

  Unit:  Bohr magneton, BM   =   9.27 x 10^-24  A m^2
```

## The five types — with the arrow diagrams

```
  1. PARAMAGNETIC
     -----------------------------------------------------------
     Has UNPAIRED electrons.
     WEAKLY ATTRACTED by a magnetic field.
     Magnetism is shown only WHILE the field is applied; it is lost
     as soon as the field is removed.

     alignment - random, until a field is applied:

           ^    >    v    <    ^    >    <    v
           (randomly pointing, no net moment on their own)

     EXAMPLES:  O2 , Cu2+ , Fe3+ , Cr3+ , TiO , VO2 , NO


  2. DIAMAGNETIC
     -----------------------------------------------------------
     ALL electrons are PAIRED.
     WEAKLY REPELLED by a magnetic field.

     alignment - every arrow cancelled by its partner:

           ^v   ^v   ^v   ^v   ^v   ^v        net moment = ZERO

     EXAMPLES:  NaCl , H2O , C6H6 , TiO2 , V2O5 , N2


  3. FERROMAGNETIC
     -----------------------------------------------------------
     STRONGLY ATTRACTED. Can be magnetised PERMANENTLY.
     The unpaired electrons in small regions called DOMAINS all
     point THE SAME WAY.

           ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^
           ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^      LARGE net moment
           ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^    ^

     EXAMPLES:  Fe , Co , Ni , Gd , CrO2

     CrO2 is used in magnetic recording tapes.
     On heating above the CURIE TEMPERATURE a ferromagnetic solid
     becomes PARAMAGNETIC (Fe: 1043 K).


  4. ANTIFERROMAGNETIC
     -----------------------------------------------------------
     The domains point in OPPOSITE directions in EQUAL numbers,
     so they CANCEL EXACTLY.

           ^    v    ^    v    ^    v    ^    v
           v    ^    v    ^    v    ^    v    ^      net moment = ZERO

     EXAMPLES:  MnO , MnO2 , Mn2O3 , FeO , NiO , Cr2O3


  5. FERRIMAGNETIC
     -----------------------------------------------------------
     The domains point in opposite directions but in UNEQUAL numbers
     (or with unequal strength), so they do NOT cancel completely.
     A SMALL net moment survives.

           ^    ^    v    ^    ^    v    ^    ^
           (more up than down)                       SMALL net moment

     Weakly attracted. On heating they become PARAMAGNETIC.

     EXAMPLES:  Fe3O4 (magnetite) , ferrites such as MgFe2O4
                and ZnFe2O4
```

## The comparison table

| Type | Electrons | Behaviour in a magnetic field | Alignment of moments | Net moment | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Paramagnetic** | Unpaired present | Weakly **attracted** | Random until field applied | Small, only in a field | O2, Cu2+, Fe3+, TiO |
| **Diamagnetic** | All paired | Weakly **repelled** | All cancelled in pairs | **Zero** | NaCl, H2O, C6H6, TiO2 |
| **Ferromagnetic** | Unpaired, in domains | **Strongly attracted**, permanent magnet | All parallel, same direction | **Very large** | Fe, Co, Ni, Gd, CrO2 |
| **Antiferromagnetic** | Unpaired, in domains | Almost no effect | Antiparallel and **equal** | **Zero** | MnO, MnO2, FeO, NiO |
| **Ferrimagnetic** | Unpaired, in domains | Weakly attracted | Antiparallel but **unequal** | **Small** | Fe3O4, MgFe2O4, ZnFe2O4 |

### WORKED EXAMPLE 12.1
*Why is Fe3O4 ferrimagnetic while MnO is antiferromagnetic?*

```
  In Fe3O4 the magnetic moments of the Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions are aligned
  in OPPOSITE directions but in UNEQUAL numbers, so they do not
  cancel completely and a SMALL net moment remains  ->  ferrimagnetic.

  In MnO the moments are aligned in opposite directions in EQUAL
  numbers, so they cancel EXACTLY and the net moment is
  ZERO  ->  antiferromagnetic.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 12.2
*Classify: (a) MnO2 (b) H2O (c) Ni (d) Fe3O4 (e) O2.*

```
  (a) MnO2   - ANTIFERROMAGNETIC
  (b) H2O    - DIAMAGNETIC       (all electrons paired)
  (c) Ni     - FERROMAGNETIC
  (d) Fe3O4  - FERRIMAGNETIC
  (e) O2     - PARAMAGNETIC      (two unpaired electrons)
```

> **TRAP:** Fe3O4 is **ferri**magnetic, NOT ferromagnetic. One letter, one mark.
