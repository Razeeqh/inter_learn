# The d and f Block Elements — Concepts Explained Simply

Read one topic. Say the reason out loud. Then move to the next.
Nothing here assumes you remember anything from 1st year except
"electrons sit in shells and orbitals".

**In this chapter, every mark comes from the REASON, not the fact.**
The examiner already knows the fact. He wants to see the word "because".

# TOPIC 1 — Where is the d block, and why "transition"?

Open the periodic table. It has four blocks, named after the orbital that
receives the LAST electron.

```
  +---------+-----------------------------------------------------------+
  | BLOCK   | LAST ELECTRON GOES INTO                                   |
  +---------+-----------------------------------------------------------+
  | s block | the  n s  orbital           (groups 1 and 2)              |
  | p block | the  n p  orbital           (groups 13 to 18)             |
  | d block | the  (n-1) d  orbital       (groups 3 to 12)  <-- THIS    |
  | f block | the  (n-2) f  orbital       (the two bottom rows) <-- THIS|
  +---------+-----------------------------------------------------------+
```

The **d block sits in the MIDDLE** of the table, groups 3 to 12, wedged between
the s block on the left and the p block on the right.

```
    s BLOCK  |          d   B L O C K          |    p BLOCK
   (metals)  |    (the bridge in between)      | (non-metals)
     Na, K   |   Sc Ti V Cr Mn Fe Co Ni Cu Zn  |  C, N, O, Cl
             |                                 |
        properties change GRADUALLY across this bridge
```

Because they form a **bridge, a "transition", from the very metallic s block to
the non-metallic p block**, they are called **TRANSITION ELEMENTS**.

## The proper definition (learn this word for word)

```
  A TRANSITION ELEMENT is an element whose ATOM has a partly filled
  d subshell, OR which can give rise to CATIONS with a partly filled
  d subshell.
```

> **TRAP:** The definition says "atom **OR** its cation". If you drop the
> "or its cation" part you cannot explain why copper counts as a transition
> element (Cu atom is 3d10, but Cu2+ is 3d9 — partly filled). Copper qualifies
> only because of the second half of the definition.

## General electronic configuration

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |     d BLOCK  :     ( n - 1 ) d 1-10      n s 1-2              |
  |                                                               |
  |     n  =  the outermost shell number                          |
  |     (n-1) d  =  the d orbitals of the shell just INSIDE it    |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Read it as: "some electrons in the inner d shell, one or two in the outer s shell."

# TOPIC 2 — The four transition series

```
  +---------+---------------+--------------------+---------------------+
  | SERIES  | ORBITAL BEING | PERIOD             | ELEMENTS            |
  |         | FILLED        |                    |                     |
  +---------+---------------+--------------------+---------------------+
  | FIRST   |     3 d       | 4th period         | Sc (21) to Zn (30)  |
  | (3d)    |               |                    |                     |
  +---------+---------------+--------------------+---------------------+
  | SECOND  |     4 d       | 5th period         | Y (39) to Cd (48)   |
  | (4d)    |               |                    |                     |
  +---------+---------------+--------------------+---------------------+
  | THIRD   |     5 d       | 6th period         | La (57), then       |
  | (5d)    |               |                    | Hf (72) to Hg (80)  |
  +---------+---------------+--------------------+---------------------+
  | FOURTH  |     6 d       | 7th period         | Ac (89), then       |
  | (6d)    |               | INCOMPLETE series  | Rf (104) onwards    |
  +---------+---------------+--------------------+---------------------+
```

> **TRAP:** In the 5d series, after La (57) the 4f orbitals get filled first
> (that is the lanthanoid row), so the series jumps from La straight to Hf (72).
> The same jump happens in the 6d series: Ac (89) then Rf (104).

Each complete series has **10 elements**, because a d subshell holds 10 electrons.

# TOPIC 3 — Electronic configurations of the 3d series (Sc to Zn)

Fill in the order 4s first, then 3d (Aufbau: 4s is lower in energy than 3d
when they are empty).

```
  +----+---------+---------------+------------------+------------------+
  | Z  | ELEMENT | CONFIGURATION | ION M2+          | UNPAIRED e- in   |
  |    |         | (of the atom) | CONFIGURATION    | the M2+ ion      |
  +----+---------+---------------+------------------+------------------+
  | 21 |   Sc    | [Ar] 3d1 4s2  | 3d1 (rare)       |  1               |
  | 22 |   Ti    | [Ar] 3d2 4s2  | 3d2              |  2               |
  | 23 |   V     | [Ar] 3d3 4s2  | 3d3              |  3               |
  | 24 |   Cr    | [Ar] 3d5 4s1  | 3d4              |  4    <-- ODD    |
  | 25 |   Mn    | [Ar] 3d5 4s2  | 3d5              |  5               |
  | 26 |   Fe    | [Ar] 3d6 4s2  | 3d6              |  4               |
  | 27 |   Co    | [Ar] 3d7 4s2  | 3d7              |  3               |
  | 28 |   Ni    | [Ar] 3d8 4s2  | 3d8              |  2               |
  | 29 |   Cu    | [Ar] 3d10 4s1 | 3d9              |  1    <-- ODD    |
  | 30 |   Zn    | [Ar] 3d10 4s2 | 3d10             |  0               |
  +----+---------+---------------+------------------+------------------+
```

> **TRAP — THE BIGGEST ONE IN THE CHAPTER:**
> When a transition metal forms a POSITIVE ion, the **4s electrons are removed
> FIRST**, before the 3d electrons. Fe is 3d6 4s2, so Fe2+ is 3d6 (NOT 3d4 4s2).
> Students lose marks here every single year.

## Why Cr and Cu are exceptions

```
  EXPECTED                       ACTUAL
  --------                       ------
  Cr :  [Ar] 3d4 4s2      ->     [Ar] 3d5 4s1     (one 4s electron shifted in)
  Cu :  [Ar] 3d9 4s2      ->     [Ar] 3d10 4s1    (one 4s electron shifted in)
```

**REASON:** exactly half-filled (d5) and completely filled (d10) subshells are
extra stable. Two things give them this stability:

```
  1. SYMMETRY of the charge cloud
        A half-filled or fully-filled set of orbitals is perfectly
        symmetrical, and a symmetrical arrangement is a lower-energy
        arrangement.

  2. EXCHANGE ENERGY
        Electrons of the same spin in different orbitals can swap places.
        Every swap RELEASES a little energy (exchange energy).
        More parallel-spin electrons  ->  more possible swaps
                                      ->  more energy released
                                      ->  more stable.
        d5 and d10 arrangements allow the MAXIMUM number of exchanges.
```

Draw the orbital boxes and it becomes obvious:

```
   CHROMIUM

   Expected  3d4 4s2                    Actual  3d5 4s1
   ----------------                     ----------------
   3d [ up ][ up ][ up ][ up ][   ]     3d [ up ][ up ][ up ][ up ][ up ]
   4s [ up dn ]                         4s [ up ]

   4 unpaired, not symmetrical          5 unpaired, HALF-FILLED d5
                                        + a half-filled 4s  ->  MORE STABLE


   COPPER

   Expected  3d9 4s2                    Actual  3d10 4s1
   ----------------                     -----------------
   3d [updn][updn][updn][updn][ up ]    3d [updn][updn][updn][updn][updn]
   4s [ up dn ]                         4s [ up ]

   not symmetrical                      COMPLETELY FILLED d10 -> MORE STABLE
```

# TOPIC 4 — Why Zn, Cd and Hg are NOT typical transition elements

This is a guaranteed 2-mark question. The answer is three lines.

```
  Zn :  [Ar] 3d10 4s2      ->   Zn2+  =  3d10
  Cd :  [Kr] 4d10 5s2      ->   Cd2+  =  4d10
  Hg :  [Xe] 4f14 5d10 6s2 ->   Hg2+  =  5d10

  In the ATOM the d subshell is COMPLETELY FILLED (d10),
  and in the common ION (M2+) it is STILL COMPLETELY FILLED (d10).

  The definition demands a PARTLY filled d subshell in the atom
  OR in a cation. Neither is true here.

  THEREFORE Zn, Cd and Hg are NOT regarded as typical transition elements.
  They are placed in the d block only because of their position (group 12).
```

Because they have no partly filled d subshell, they lose all the "transition"
behaviour:

```
  +--------------------------+-------------------+------------------------+
  | PROPERTY                 | TYPICAL TRANSITION| Zn , Cd , Hg           |
  +--------------------------+-------------------+------------------------+
  | Variable oxidation state | YES               | NO (only +2; Hg also +1|
  |                          |                   | as Hg2 2+)             |
  | Coloured ions            | YES               | NO - colourless        |
  | Paramagnetism            | YES               | NO - diamagnetic       |
  | Catalytic activity       | strong            | poor                   |
  | Melting point            | very high         | LOW (Hg is a LIQUID)   |
  | Enthalpy of atomisation  | very high         | low                    |
  +--------------------------+-------------------+------------------------+
```

**REASON for the low melting point:** in a normal transition metal the d
electrons also join in the metallic bonding, making the bond very strong. In
Zn, Cd and Hg the d10 shell is full and stable, so **only the two s electrons
take part in metallic bonding**. Weak metallic bond = low melting point, and in
mercury it is so weak that the metal is liquid at room temperature.

# TOPIC 5 — Metallic character and physical properties

**All transition elements are metals.** They are hard, have high melting and
boiling points, high density, are good conductors of heat and electricity, and
are malleable and ductile.

```
  REASON  ->  They have  (a) a small atomic size
                         (b) a high nuclear charge
                         (c) ONE or TWO s electrons free to move
                         (d) UNPAIRED d ELECTRONS that also take part
                             in metallic bonding

              (d) is the key point. In sodium only one electron holds
              the lattice together. In chromium up to six electrons
              (5 d + 1 s) hold it together. Six ropes are stronger
              than one, so the metal is harder and melts much higher.
```

## Melting point / enthalpy of atomisation trend

```
   Melting point rises from Sc, peaks in the MIDDLE of the series,
   then falls to Zn.

   high  |                 *  Cr  (highest m.p. in the 3d series)
         |            *         *
         |       *                   *
         |   *          Mn (dip)          *
         | *              o                   *
   low   +----------------------------------------*-- Zn (lowest)
         Sc  Ti  V  Cr  Mn  Fe  Co  Ni  Cu  Zn

   REASON  -  maximum number of unpaired d electrons in the middle
              ->  maximum number of covalent-type metallic bonds
              ->  strongest lattice  ->  highest melting point.

   Mn DIPS because its 3d5 4s2 configuration is unusually stable and
   symmetrical, so its electrons are reluctant to join in the bonding.

   Zn is LOWEST because its d10 shell is full and takes no part at all.
```

> **TRAP:** Tungsten (W, 5d series) has the highest melting point of all
> metals — that is why it is used as the filament in a light bulb. Within the
> **3d series only**, chromium has the highest melting point.

## Density

Density **increases** across a series (Sc about 3.0 g/cm3 to Cu about 8.9 g/cm3),
because atomic mass keeps rising while atomic volume keeps shrinking.
Density = mass / volume, so up on top and down on bottom means a big rise.

# TOPIC 6 — Atomic and ionic radii (the "flat middle" question)

```
  METALLIC RADII of the 3d series, in picometres

  Sc   Ti   V    Cr   Mn   Fe   Co   Ni   Cu   Zn
  164  147  135  129  137  126  125  125  128  137

   size |*
        |   *
        |      *  *      *
        |            *  *  *  *  *          *
        |                              *
        +-----------------------------------------
        Sc  Ti  V  Cr  Mn  Fe  Co  Ni  Cu  Zn

        BIG FALL     ALMOST FLAT      SLIGHT RISE
        at the start  in the middle    at the end
```

Three things to explain, each worth a mark:

```
  1. WHY THE SIZE FALLS AT THE START (Sc -> V)
     Going right, the nuclear charge increases by +1 each time, but the
     new electron goes into the SAME inner 3d shell. The stronger nucleus
     pulls the electron cloud in.  ->  size decreases.

  2. WHY THE SIZE STAYS ALMOST CONSTANT IN THE MIDDLE (Cr -> Ni)
     Two opposing effects now cancel each other:

        increasing nuclear charge   ->  pulls electrons IN  (shrinks)
        increasing d-d repulsion    ->  pushes electrons OUT (expands)
        + the added d electron SCREENS the outer 4s electrons from
          the extra nuclear charge

     Pull in = push out, so the net change is almost zero.
     ->  the radius is nearly the SAME for Fe, Co, Ni.

  3. WHY THE SIZE RISES SLIGHTLY AT THE END (Cu, Zn)
     Now the d orbitals are nearly full (d10). Electron-electron
     repulsion in the filled d shell wins over the nuclear pull,
     and the screening is at its maximum.  ->  size increases a little.
```

## The lanthanoid contraction effect on the 4d and 5d series

```
  ATOMIC RADII, picometres

  +---------+--------+--------+--------+
  | GROUP   |   4    |   5    |   6    |
  +---------+--------+--------+--------+
  | 3d      | Ti 147 | V 135  | Cr 129 |
  | 4d      | Zr 160 | Nb 146 | Mo 139 |
  | 5d      | Hf 159 | Ta 146 | W  139 |
  +---------+--------+--------+--------+
             ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
             4d and 5d are ALMOST IDENTICAL
```

Normally going down a group the atom gets bigger, so Hf should be much bigger
than Zr. It is not — it is very slightly SMALLER.

```
  REASON:  between the 4d series and the 5d series lie the 14 LANTHANOIDS.
           Across the lanthanoids the size shrinks steadily (the LANTHANOID
           CONTRACTION), because 4f electrons shield the nucleus very poorly.

           This accumulated shrinkage exactly cancels out the increase in
           size you would expect from adding a whole new shell.

  CONSEQUENCE:  Zr and Hf, Nb and Ta, Mo and W have nearly the same size,
                nearly the same properties, and are found together and are
                very hard to separate. They are called "twin pairs".
```

# TOPIC 7 — Ionisation enthalpies

```
  FIRST IONISATION ENTHALPY of the 3d series, kJ/mol

  Sc   Ti   V    Cr   Mn   Fe   Co   Ni   Cu   Zn
  631  656  650  653  717  762  758  736  745  906
```

Points that carry marks:

```
  1. The values are HIGHER than the s block (because of small size and
     high nuclear charge) but LOWER than the p block.

  2. They increase across the series, but only SLOWLY and IRREGULARLY.
     REASON - the added electron goes into an INNER 3d shell, where it
     screens the 4s electron from the extra nuclear charge. Increased
     nuclear charge and increased screening partly cancel out.

  3. Zn has the HIGHEST value (906).
     REASON - fully filled and very stable 3d10 4s2 configuration; it is
     hard to disturb.

  4. IE(1) < IE(2) < IE(3) always, because removing an electron from an
     already positive ion is harder.

  5. Mn2+ (3d5) and Zn2+ (3d10) are hard to ionise further, so their
     third ionisation enthalpies are abnormally HIGH. That is why Mn3+
     and Zn3+ are not common.
```

# TOPIC 8 — Variable oxidation states

This is the single most characteristic property of transition metals.

```
  WHY DO THEY SHOW VARIABLE OXIDATION STATES?

      The (n-1)d and the ns orbitals have ALMOST THE SAME ENERGY.

      So BOTH the ns electrons AND a variable number of (n-1)d electrons
      can be lost, one at a time, using only a small amount of extra energy.

      Different numbers lost  ->  different oxidation states,
      and they differ by ONE unit (+2, +3, +4, +5 ...).

  CONTRAST with the p block, where the oxidation states usually differ
  by TWO units (Sn: +2 and +4, never +3) because there the s and p
  energies are far apart.
```

```
  OXIDATION STATES OF THE 3d SERIES
  (the most common / most stable one is marked with ***)

  +----+----------------------------------------------+---------------+
  | El | OXIDATION STATES                             | MAXIMUM       |
  +----+----------------------------------------------+---------------+
  | Sc | +3 ***                                       |  +3           |
  | Ti | +2 , +3 , +4 ***                             |  +4           |
  | V  | +2 , +3 , +4 , +5 ***                        |  +5           |
  | Cr | +2 , +3 *** , +4 , +5 , +6                   |  +6           |
  | Mn | +2 *** , +3 , +4 , +5 , +6 , +7              |  +7  HIGHEST  |
  | Fe | +2 , +3 *** , (+4 , +5 , +6)                 |  +6           |
  | Co | +2 *** , +3 , (+4)                           |  +4           |
  | Ni | +2 *** , (+3 , +4)                           |  +4           |
  | Cu | +1 , +2 ***                                  |  +2           |
  | Zn | +2 ***                                       |  +2           |
  +----+----------------------------------------------+---------------+
```

```
  THE SHAPE OF THAT TABLE - two sentences that earn full marks:

  * The number of oxidation states INCREASES from Sc to Mn, then
    DECREASES from Mn to Zn.

  * Mn shows the MAXIMUM number (+2 to +7) because it has the greatest
    number of available electrons (5 d + 2 s = 7) that can be lost.

  * After Mn the d electrons start pairing up. Paired electrons are held
    more tightly and are harder to remove, so the number of oxidation
    states falls again.
```

## Stability of the oxidation states

```
  RULE 1  -  A HALF-FILLED (d5) or FULLY FILLED (d10) ion is extra stable.
             Mn2+ (3d5)  and  Fe3+ (3d5)  and  Zn2+ (3d10)  are very stable.

  RULE 2  -  In the SAME group, the HIGHER oxidation state becomes MORE
             stable as you go DOWN.
             Example:  Cr(VI) as CrO4 2- is a strong oxidiser (unstable,
                       wants to drop to +3), but W(VI) in WO4 2- is stable.

  RULE 3  -  The HIGHEST oxidation states occur only in OXIDES and
             FLUORIDES, because O and F are the most electronegative
             elements and can stabilise a very high positive charge.
             Examples:  MnO4- (+7) , CrO4 2- (+6) , VF5 (+5) , OsF6 (+6)

  RULE 4  -  LOW oxidation states are found in complexes with ligands
             that are good pi acceptors, like CO.
             Example:  Ni(CO)4  -> nickel is in the ZERO oxidation state.
```

> **TRAP:** "Which ion is more stable, Fe2+ or Fe3+?" Answer: **Fe3+**, because
> Fe3+ is 3d5 (half-filled, extra stable), while Fe2+ is 3d6. That is exactly
> why Fe2+ solutions slowly turn to Fe3+ in air.

# TOPIC 9 — Standard electrode potentials and the three anomalies

E° (M2+ / M) tells you how easily the metal turns into its M2+ ion.
**More negative = the metal is a stronger reducing agent = it corrodes/reacts
more readily.**

```
  STANDARD ELECTRODE POTENTIALS  E° (M2+/M) in volts

  +------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
  |  Ti  |   V   |  Cr   |  Mn   |  Fe   |  Co   |  Ni   |  Cu   |  Zn   |
  +------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
  |-1.63 | -1.18 | -0.90 | -1.18 | -0.44 | -0.28 | -0.25 | +0.34 | -0.76 |
  +------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+

     GENERAL TREND: the values become LESS NEGATIVE from Ti to Cu.
     (the metals get less and less reactive as you go right)

     But look at the three marked below - they break the trend:
                       Mn (-1.18)  Ni (-0.25)  Zn (-0.76)
                       MORE NEGATIVE than expected
```

**E° depends on three energy terms:**

```
      M(s)  --sublimation-->  M(g)  --ionisation-->  M2+(g)  --hydration-->  M2+(aq)

      E°  is decided by:
          (1) enthalpy of SUBLIMATION  (breaking the metal lattice)
          (2) IONISATION enthalpy      (removing the 2 electrons)
          (3) enthalpy of HYDRATION    (water surrounding the ion) - releases energy
```

```
  THE THREE ANOMALIES - EXPLAINED

  Mn  E° = -1.18  (much more negative than expected)
      REASON: Mn2+ is 3d5, a HALF-FILLED and extra-stable ion. Because
              the product is so stable, Mn gives up its electrons easily.

  Zn  E° = -0.76  (more negative than expected)
      REASON: Zn2+ is 3d10, a COMPLETELY FILLED and extra-stable ion,
              so again the metal ionises readily.

  Ni  E° = -0.25  (more negative than expected)
      REASON: Ni2+ has a very high (very negative) HYDRATION ENTHALPY.
              A lot of energy is released when water surrounds Ni2+,
              which drives the reaction forward.

  Cu  E° = +0.34  -  THE ONLY POSITIVE VALUE IN THE SERIES
      REASON: copper has a HIGH enthalpy of sublimation AND a HIGH
              ionisation enthalpy, and its hydration enthalpy is not
              large enough to pay for them.
      CONSEQUENCE: Cu does NOT liberate H2 from dilute acids.
                   Copper is below hydrogen in the activity series.
```

> **TRAP:** "Why does copper not displace hydrogen from dilute HCl?" The answer
> is *not* "because it is a noble metal". Say: **because E°(Cu2+/Cu) is
> POSITIVE (+0.34 V), so copper cannot reduce H+ to H2.**

# TOPIC 10 — Magnetic properties and the spin-only formula

## The two words you must get right

```
  PARAMAGNETIC   -  has UNPAIRED electrons
                 -  is ATTRACTED into a magnetic field
                 -  mu is greater than zero
                    examples: Fe2+ , Cu2+ , Mn2+ , Ti3+

  DIAMAGNETIC    -  ALL electrons are PAIRED
                 -  is slightly REPELLED by a magnetic field
                 -  mu = 0
                    examples: Sc3+ , Ti4+ , Zn2+ , Cu+
```

Most transition metal ions have unpaired d electrons, so **most transition metal
compounds are paramagnetic**, and the paramagnetism increases as the number of
unpaired electrons increases.

## The formula

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |        mu  =  sqrt (  n ( n + 2 )  )      Bohr Magneton (BM)    |
  |                                                                 |
  |        n  =  number of UNPAIRED electrons                       |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

It is called the **spin-only** formula because it counts only the spin of the
electrons and ignores their orbital motion.

```
  +-----+---------------------------+-----------+
  |  n  |  sqrt( n(n+2) )           |  mu (BM)  |
  +-----+---------------------------+-----------+
  |  0  |  sqrt(0 x 2) = sqrt 0     |   0.00    |
  |  1  |  sqrt(1 x 3) = sqrt 3     |   1.73    |
  |  2  |  sqrt(2 x 4) = sqrt 8     |   2.83    |
  |  3  |  sqrt(3 x 5) = sqrt 15    |   3.87    |
  |  4  |  sqrt(4 x 6) = sqrt 24    |   4.90    |
  |  5  |  sqrt(5 x 7) = sqrt 35    |   5.92    |
  +-----+---------------------------+-----------+

  MEMORISE THE RIGHT-HAND COLUMN. You will never need a calculator.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — Calculate the magnetic moment of Fe2+

```
  STEP 1  Configuration of the ATOM:      Fe (26) = [Ar] 3d6 4s2
  STEP 2  Remove 2 electrons from 4s FIRST:
                                          Fe2+ = [Ar] 3d6
  STEP 3  Fill the five d boxes by Hund's rule (singly first, then pair):

             3d6  ->  [ up dn ][ up ][ up ][ up ][ up ]
                          ^        ^     ^     ^     ^
                        paired    four UNPAIRED electrons

          n = 4

  STEP 4  mu = sqrt( 4 x 6 ) = sqrt 24 = 4.90 BM

  ANSWER:  4.90 BM , paramagnetic.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — Calculate the magnetic moment of Cu2+

```
  Cu (29) = [Ar] 3d10 4s1
  Remove 4s first, then one 3d:      Cu2+ = [Ar] 3d9

     3d9  ->  [updn][updn][updn][updn][ up ]     n = 1

  mu = sqrt( 1 x 3 ) = 1.73 BM      paramagnetic
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 3 — Going BACKWARDS from mu to n

```
  Q: An ion has a magnetic moment of 3.87 BM. How many unpaired
     electrons does it have?

     3.87 = sqrt( n(n+2) )
     square both sides:   14.98  =  n(n+2)
     try n = 3 :          3 x 5  =  15      MATCH

     ANSWER:  n = 3   (for example Cr3+ = 3d3 , or V2+ = 3d3)
```

# TOPIC 11 — Why transition metal ions are COLOURED

## The observation

Most transition metal compounds and their aqueous solutions are coloured:
CuSO4 solution is blue, KMnO4 is purple, K2Cr2O7 is orange, FeSO4 is pale green.
But Sc3+, Ti4+, Zn2+ and Cu+ solutions are **colourless**.

## The explanation — the d-d TRANSITION (learn this diagram)

In a free gaseous ion the five d orbitals all have the same energy. But when
ligands (water molecules, ions, etc.) come close, the electric field of the
ligands **splits the five d orbitals into two sets** with a small energy gap
between them.

```
        FREE ION                         ION SURROUNDED BY LIGANDS
     (5 d orbitals equal)                 (split into 2 levels)

                                            ---   ---   eg  (higher)
                                             ^
                                             |  gap = delta
     --- --- --- --- ---                     |   (SMALL - matches
     (all the same energy)                   |    visible light)
                                             v
                                        ---  ---  ---   t2g (lower)


   WHAT HAPPENS:
   1. White light falls on the solution.
   2. An electron in the LOWER set absorbs a photon of exactly the
      right energy and JUMPS to the HIGHER set.
      This jump is called a  d - d   T R A N S I T I O N.
   3. That one colour of light is now MISSING from the white light.
   4. What comes out to your eye is the COMPLEMENTARY colour.
      -> the substance LOOKS coloured.
```

```
  THE ESSENTIAL CONDITION - state it clearly:

      For colour, the ion must have a PARTLY FILLED d subshell
      (that is, between d1 and d9).

      d0  ->  there is NO electron to jump      ->  COLOURLESS
      d10 ->  the upper set is already FULL, there is no empty
              place to jump into                ->  COLOURLESS
```

```
  +---------+--------+--------------+       +---------+-------+-----------+
  | COLOUR- | CONFIG | WHY          |       |  ION    | CONFIG| COLOUR    |
  | LESS ION|        |              |       +---------+-------+-----------+
  +---------+--------+--------------+       | Ti3+    | 3d1   | purple /  |
  | Sc3+    |  3d0   | no d electron|       |         |       | violet    |
  | Ti4+    |  3d0   | no d electron|       | V3+     | 3d2   | green     |
  | Cu+     |  3d10  | d shell full |       | V2+     | 3d3   | violet    |
  | Zn2+    |  3d10  | d shell full |       | Cr3+    | 3d3   | violet /  |
  | Cd2+    |  4d10  | d shell full |       |         |       | green     |
  | Hg2+    |  5d10  | d shell full |       | Mn3+    | 3d4   | violet    |
  +---------+--------+--------------+       | Mn2+    | 3d5   | light pink|
                                            | Fe3+    | 3d5   | yellow    |
                                            | Fe2+    | 3d6   | green     |
                                            | Co2+    | 3d7   | pink      |
                                            | Ni2+    | 3d8   | green     |
                                            | Cu2+    | 3d9   | blue      |
                                            +---------+-------+-----------+
```

> **TRAP:** KMnO4 (Mn is +7, so 3d0) and K2Cr2O7 (Cr is +6, so 3d0) are
> intensely coloured even though they have NO d electrons. Their colour is
> **NOT** from a d-d transition — it comes from a **charge transfer**, where an
> electron momentarily jumps from an oxygen to the metal. If an examiner asks
> why KMnO4 is coloured, "d-d transition" is the WRONG answer.

# TOPIC 12 — Catalytic properties

Transition metals and their compounds are the best catalysts we have.

```
  EXAMPLES YOU SHOULD BE ABLE TO QUOTE

  +-----------------+-------------------------------------------------+
  | CATALYST        | PROCESS                                         |
  +-----------------+-------------------------------------------------+
  | Fe              | Haber process   N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3                 |
  | V2O5            | Contact process 2SO2 + O2 -> 2SO3               |
  | Ni              | hydrogenation of oils into vanaspati            |
  | Pt / Rh         | catalytic converter in cars, Ostwald process    |
  | TiCl4 + Al(C2H5)3 | Ziegler-Natta catalyst for polythene          |
  | MnO2            | decomposition of KClO3 to give O2               |
  | Cu / Cu2Cl2     | Sandmeyer and Deacon reactions                  |
  +-----------------+-------------------------------------------------+
```

```
  WHY ARE THEY GOOD CATALYSTS?  -  THREE REASONS

  1. VARIABLE OXIDATION STATES
        The metal can lend an electron to one reactant and take it back
        from another, going up and down between two oxidation states.
        This provides an alternate path with a LOWER activation energy.

        Example - the reaction of I- with S2O8 2- catalysed by Fe3+:
             2 Fe3+  +  2 I-        ->  2 Fe2+  +  I2
             2 Fe2+  +  S2O8 2-     ->  2 Fe3+  +  2 SO4 2-
             ---------------------------------------------------
             The Fe3+ is regenerated - a true catalyst.

  2. LARGE SURFACE AREA + FREE VALENCIES
        Atoms on the surface of the metal have unused (free) valencies
        because of the partly filled d orbitals. Reactant molecules get
        ADSORBED onto these sites. Adsorption
          (a) increases the concentration of reactants at the surface,
          (b) WEAKENS the bonds inside the reactant molecules.
        Both make the reaction faster.

  3. FORMATION OF UNSTABLE INTERMEDIATE COMPOUNDS
        The metal forms a loose intermediate with a reactant, which then
        breaks down easily to give the product and return the catalyst.
```

# TOPIC 13 — Formation of complex compounds

A **complex compound** is one in which a central metal ion is surrounded by a
fixed number of ions or neutral molecules (called **ligands**) attached by
coordinate bonds.

```
  EXAMPLES

     [Fe(CN)6] 4-       hexacyanoferrate(II)
     [Cu(NH3)4] 2+      deep blue, formed when excess NH3 is added to CuSO4
     [Ni(CN)4] 2-
     [Co(NH3)6] 3+
     [Fe(CN)6] 3-
```

```
  WHY DO TRANSITION METALS FORM COMPLEXES SO EASILY?
  THREE REASONS - all three must be written:

  1. SMALL SIZE of the metal ion
  2. HIGH CHARGE (high charge density = high polarising power, so the
     ion can pull ligand electron pairs strongly towards itself)
  3. VACANT d ORBITALS of the right energy that can ACCEPT the lone
     pairs donated by the ligands
```

# TOPIC 14 — Interstitial compounds

```
  WHAT ARE THEY?

     Transition metals have a crystal lattice with small EMPTY SPACES
     between the metal atoms. These spaces are called INTERSTICES
     (or interstitial voids).

     Small atoms - H , C , N , B - can slip into these holes.
     The compound formed is called an INTERSTITIAL COMPOUND.

     Examples:  TiC , Mn4N , Fe3H , VH0.56 , TiH1.7 , steel (Fe + C)
```

```
   THE CRYSTAL LATTICE, drawn crudely:

      M     M     M     M            M = large metal atom
         x     x     x               x = small H / C / N atom
      M     M     M     M                sitting in the HOLE between them
         x     x     x
      M     M     M     M
```

```
  PROPERTIES OF INTERSTITIAL COMPOUNDS  (a favourite 2-mark question)

  1. They are NON-STOICHIOMETRIC - the formula is not a whole-number
     ratio (VH0.56 , TiH1.7). So they cannot be given an ordinary
     chemical formula and the metal has no definite oxidation state.
  2. They are VERY HARD - often harder than the pure metal.
  3. They have VERY HIGH melting points, higher than the pure metal.
  4. They RETAIN metallic conductivity.
  5. They are chemically INERT.

  REASON for 2 and 3: the trapped small atoms form extra bonds with the
  metal atoms and block the metal layers from sliding over one another.
  That is exactly why adding carbon to iron gives STEEL, which is far
  harder than pure iron.
```

```
                              8 Na2CrO4  +  2 Fe2O3  +  8 CO2

      2 Na2CrO4  +  H2SO4   ->   Na2Cr2O7  +  Na2SO4  +  H2O

      Na2Cr2O7  +  2 KCl   ->   K2Cr2O7  +  2 NaCl
```

## Structures of the chromate and dichromate ions

```
   CHROMATE ION   CrO4 2-         -  TETRAHEDRAL
                                  -  all four Cr-O bonds are EQUAL (161 pm)
                                  -  Cr is in the +6 oxidation state

                        O
                        |
                        |
                  Cr <--+
                 /  \
                /    \
               O      O          (the fourth O points backwards)



   DICHROMATE ION   Cr2O7 2-     -  TWO tetrahedra joined by ONE SHARED
                                    (bridging) oxygen atom
                                 -  Cr - O - Cr angle is about 126 degrees
                                 -  terminal Cr-O = 163 pm (shorter)
                                    bridging Cr-O = 179 pm (longer)

              O           O
              |           |
              |           |
        O --- Cr --- O --- Cr --- O
              |     bridging|
              |       O     |
              O             O

                   ^
                   |
             the SHARED oxygen - this is what makes it a DI-chromate
```

## The chromate - dichromate equilibrium (a certain 4-mark question)

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |    2 CrO4 2-   +   2 H+     <-->    Cr2O7 2-   +   H2O           |
  |      YELLOW                           ORANGE                     |
  |                                                                  |
  |    Cr2O7 2-    +   2 OH-    <-->    2 CrO4 2-  +   H2O           |
  |      ORANGE                           YELLOW                     |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+

  IN ONE LINE:

     ADD ACID  (lower the pH)   ->  YELLOW turns ORANGE  (dichromate)
     ADD ALKALI(raise the pH)   ->  ORANGE turns YELLOW  (chromate)

  REASON: the equilibrium is pH dependent. Adding H+ pushes the first
  equilibrium to the right (Le Chatelier); adding OH- removes H+ and
  pulls it back to the left.

  IMPORTANT: the oxidation state of Cr stays +6 in BOTH ions.
  This is NOT a redox change, only a change of ion.
```

> **TRAP:** Students write "chromate is orange". It is **YELLOW**. Remember:
> **"Acid makes it orange"** — A for Acid, and dichromate is the acidic-medium
> species.

## Oxidising action of K2Cr2O7 (only in ACIDIC medium)

```

  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |   Cr2O7 2-  +  14 H+  +  6 e-   ->   2 Cr3+  +  7 H2O           |
  |     ORANGE                              GREEN                   |
  |                                                                 |
  |                E°  =  + 1.33 V                                  |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

  So it accepts 6 electrons. Multiply the other half reaction to match 6.
  The colour change ORANGE -> GREEN is the visible sign of the reaction.
```

```
  THE FIVE REACTIONS TO WRITE

  1. Iodide  to  iodine
     Cr2O7 2- + 14 H+ + 6 I-   ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O + 3 I2

  2. Iron(II) to iron(III)     (used in volumetric analysis)
     Cr2O7 2- + 14 H+ + 6 Fe2+ ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O + 6 Fe3+

  3. Hydrogen sulphide to sulphur
     Cr2O7 2- + 8 H+ + 3 H2S   ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O + 3 S

  4. Sulphite to sulphate
     Cr2O7 2- + 8 H+ + 3 SO3 2- -> 2 Cr3+ + 4 H2O + 3 SO4 2-
```

# TOPIC 18 — The f block: lanthanoids

```
  THE f BLOCK  =  the two rows printed SEPARATELY at the bottom.

  LANTHANOIDS  -  the 14 elements from  Ce (58)  to  Lu (71) ,
                  following lanthanum La (57).
                  The 4f orbitals are being filled.

  ACTINOIDS    -  the 14 elements from  Th (90)  to  Lr (103) ,
                  following actinium Ac (89).
                  The 5f orbitals are being filled.

  They are called INNER TRANSITION ELEMENTS because the last electron
  enters an orbital TWO shells inside the outermost shell.
```

## General electronic configuration

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |   LANTHANOIDS :   [Xe]   4 f 1-14    5 d 0-1    6 s 2            |
  |                                                                  |
  |   ACTINOIDS   :   [Rn]   5 f 1-14    6 d 0-1    7 s 2            |
  |                                                                  |
  |   GENERAL     :   ( n-2 ) f 1-14  ( n-1 ) d 0-1   n s 2          |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+

  Note the "5d 0-1" - most lanthanoids have NO 5d electron, but
  Ce, Gd and Lu do:
        Ce (58) = [Xe] 4f1  5d1 6s2
        Gd (64) = [Xe] 4f7  5d1 6s2     (half-filled 4f7 stability)
        Lu (71) = [Xe] 4f14 5d1 6s2     (fully filled 4f14 stability)
```

## Oxidation states of the lanthanoids

```
  +3 is the COMMON and CHARACTERISTIC oxidation state of ALL lanthanoids.

  REASON: they lose the two 6s electrons and one 4f (or 5d) electron
          fairly easily; removing a fourth electron needs far more energy.

  A FEW show +2 or +4 as well, and always for the same reason -
  the resulting ion reaches an EMPTY, HALF-FILLED or FULL 4f shell:

  +-------+--------------+--------------------------------------------+
  | ION   | 4f CONFIG    | WHY IT IS STABLE                           |
  +-------+--------------+--------------------------------------------+
  | Ce4+  |  4f0         | EMPTY 4f shell  (Ce4+ is a good oxidiser,  |
  |       |              | it wants to go back to Ce3+)               |
  | Eu2+  |  4f7         | HALF-FILLED 4f7 (Eu2+ is a good reducer)   |
  | Tb4+  |  4f7         | HALF-FILLED 4f7                            |
  | Yb2+  |  4f14        | COMPLETELY FILLED 4f14                     |
  +-------+--------------+--------------------------------------------+
```

## General properties

```
  - all are silvery-white, soft METALS that tarnish in air
  - hardness INCREASES with increasing atomic number
  - they are highly ELECTROPOSITIVE and reactive
  - they burn in oxygen to give M2O3, react with water to give hydroxide
    and hydrogen, and dissolve in dilute acids
  - most of their ions are COLOURED and PARAMAGNETIC (because of the
    unpaired 4f electrons); La3+ (4f0) and Lu3+ (4f14) are colourless
    and diamagnetic
  - they show far LESS tendency to form complexes than the d block,
    because the 4f orbitals are buried deep inside and are shielded
    from the ligands
```

# TOPIC 19 — LANTHANOID CONTRACTION  *** guaranteed question ***

## Definition

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |   The STEADY DECREASE in the atomic and ionic radii of the       |
  |   lanthanoid elements with INCREASING atomic number, going       |
  |   from La (57) to Lu (71), is called the LANTHANOID CONTRACTION. |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+

  Ionic radii of M3+ ions, picometres:

     La3+  103        ->        Lu3+   86

     size |*
          |  *
          |     *
          |        *
          |            *
          |                 *
          |                       *
          +-------------------------------
          La  Ce  Pr  Nd ... Er  Tm  Yb  Lu

     A steady slide downhill - about 17 pm over 15 elements.
```

## THE CAUSE (one sentence, and it must contain the word "shielding")

```
  As we move along the series, the nuclear charge increases by +1
  each time and the extra electron enters the SAME inner 4f subshell.

  4f orbitals have a very DIFFUSE, badly shaped charge cloud, so a
  4f electron SHIELDS the outer electrons from the nucleus very POORLY.

  So the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons keeps
  INCREASING, and the electron cloud is pulled in a little more with
  every element.

  RESULT: the size decreases steadily.
```

> **TRAP:** The cause is the **poor shielding by 4f electrons**, not "increasing
> nuclear charge" alone. Nuclear charge increases across every period; what
> makes this case special is that the 4f shielding is unusually bad.

## THE FOUR CONSEQUENCES

```
  1. SIMILARITY OF THE 4d AND 5d SERIES
     The contraction cancels the expected size increase from period 5
     to period 6. So Zr (160 pm) and Hf (159 pm) are almost identical
     in size, and so are Nb/Ta and Mo/W. These "twin pairs" have almost
     identical chemical properties and occur together in nature.

  2. DIFFICULTY IN SEPARATION OF THE LANTHANOIDS
     Because the sizes of the 14 lanthanoids differ only very slightly,
     their chemical properties are almost identical, so separating them
     from one another is extremely difficult. (Ion-exchange is used.)

  3. BASIC STRENGTH OF THE HYDROXIDES DECREASES
     From La(OH)3 to Lu(OH)3 the size falls, so the M-OH bond becomes
     more covalent and holds the OH- more tightly.
        La(OH)3 = MOST basic  ->  Lu(OH)3 = LEAST basic
     (Lu(OH)3 is even slightly amphoteric.)

  4. SLIGHT VARIATION IN PROPERTIES / GRADUAL CHANGE
     Complex-forming ability and the tendency to form stable complexes
     INCREASE slightly from La3+ to Lu3+, because charge density
     (charge / size) increases as the ion gets smaller.
```

## Uses of the lanthanoids — MISCH METAL

```
  MISCH METAL

     composition:  about 95 %  lanthanoid metal
                   about  5 %  iron
                   traces of   S , C , Ca , Al

     USES:  - mixed with magnesium to make ALLOYS used in
              bullets, shells and lighter FLINTS
            - as a "pyrophoric" alloy - it sparks when scratched,
              which is exactly what a cigarette lighter flint does

  OTHER USES OF LANTHANOIDS
     - mixed oxides are used as CATALYSTS in petroleum cracking
     - lanthanoid oxides are used in TV screens, and in glass and
       ceramics as colouring / polishing agents
     - added in small amounts to steel to improve its strength and
       workability
```

# TOPIC 20 — The actinoids

```
  ACTINOIDS  =  Th (90) to Lr (103) , 14 elements , the 5f series.

  GENERAL CONFIGURATION:   [Rn]  5f 1-14   6d 0-1   7s 2
```

```
  KEY POINTS

  1. ALL actinoids are RADIOACTIVE. This is the biggest single difference
     from the lanthanoids.

  2. Elements AFTER uranium (Z greater than 92) are called TRANSURANIC
     elements. They are man-made and do not occur naturally.

  3. They show a MUCH WIDER RANGE of oxidation states:
     +3 , +4 , +5 , +6 and even +7.
     REASON - in the actinoids the 5f, 6d and 7s orbitals have very
     similar energies, so all of them can take part in bonding.
     (In the lanthanoids the 4f orbitals are buried much deeper and
     hardly take part, which is why they are stuck at +3.)
        U shows up to +6 (as in UO2 2+)
        Np shows up to +7

  4. +3 is still the most common oxidation state overall.

  5. They are highly reactive, especially when finely divided.

  6. ACTINOID CONTRACTION - the steady decrease in atomic and ionic
     radii from Ac to Lr. It is caused by the poor shielding of the
     5f electrons, and it is GREATER than the lanthanoid contraction
     because 5f electrons shield even more POORLY than 4f electrons.

  7. Their chemistry is complicated and hard to study because of the
     radioactivity and the many oxidation states.
```
