# PATTERN 1 — Position of hydrogen in the periodic table

### Q1
Hydrogen resembles the halogens in that it

(a) has one electron in its valence shell &nbsp; (b) forms a unipositive ion
(c) forms a uninegative ion and exists as a diatomic molecule
(d) is a good reducing agent

**ANSWER: (c)** — H− (hydride ion) and H2 are the halogen-like features.
Options (a), (b) and (d) are the ALKALI-METAL-like features.

---

### Q2
The ionisation enthalpy of hydrogen (1312 kJ/mol) is closest to that of

(a) Li &nbsp; (b) Na &nbsp; (c) Cl &nbsp; (d) Cs

**ANSWER: (c)**
```
  H   1312 kJ/mol
  Cl  1255 kJ/mol   <- very close
  F   1680 kJ/mol
  Li   520 kJ/mol
  Na   496 kJ/mol
  Cs   376 kJ/mol

  The HIGH ionisation enthalpy is exactly why hydrogen does NOT
  really belong with the alkali metals.
```

---

### Q3
The position of hydrogen in the periodic table is anomalous because

(a) it is the lightest element
(b) it resembles both alkali metals and halogens but belongs fully to neither
(c) it is a gas &nbsp; (d) it has no neutron

**ANSWER: (b)** — that is the definition of the anomaly.

---

### Q4
In which of the following is hydrogen in the −1 oxidation state?

(a) H2O &nbsp; (b) HCl &nbsp; (c) CaH2 &nbsp; (d) NH3

**ANSWER: (c)**
> **SHORTCUT:** hydrogen is −1 **only** when bonded to a metal that is LESS
> electronegative than it — that is, in the IONIC HYDRIDES (NaH, CaH2, LiH,
> BaH2). With any non-metal (O, Cl, N, C, S) hydrogen is +1.

# PATTERN 2 — Isotopes of hydrogen and heavy water

### Q5
The isotope of hydrogen that is radioactive is

(a) protium &nbsp; (b) deuterium &nbsp; (c) tritium &nbsp; (d) all three

**ANSWER: (c)** — tritium (1H3), a beta emitter with half-life 12.33 years.

---

### Q6
The number of neutrons in protium, deuterium and tritium respectively is

(a) 1, 2, 3 &nbsp; (b) 0, 1, 2 &nbsp; (c) 1, 1, 1 &nbsp; (d) 0, 0, 0

**ANSWER: (b)**
> **SHORTCUT:** neutrons = mass number − 1 (there is always exactly 1 proton).
> So 1−1 = 0, 2−1 = 1, 3−1 = 2.

---

### Q7
The natural abundance of deuterium in ordinary hydrogen is about

(a) 0.0156 % &nbsp; (b) 1.56 % &nbsp; (c) 15.6 % &nbsp; (d) 99.985 %

**ANSWER: (a)** — 0.0156%, roughly 1 atom in 6000. 99.985% is protium.

---

### Q8
Heavy water is used in nuclear reactors as a

(a) fuel &nbsp; (b) coolant only &nbsp; (c) moderator &nbsp; (d) control rod

**ANSWER: (c)** — D2O slows down fast neutrons WITHOUT absorbing them, which
is exactly what a moderator must do. (Ordinary H2O absorbs neutrons too much.)

---

### Q9
When D2O reacts with CaC2, the product formed is

(a) C2H2 &nbsp; (b) C2D2 &nbsp; (c) CD4 &nbsp; (d) CaD2

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
  CaC2  +  2 D2O   ->   C2D2   +   Ca(OD)2

  RULE: in every exchange reaction, simply replace every H that
  CAME FROM THE WATER with a D.
```

---

### Q10
Which statement about heavy water is **incorrect**?

(a) its boiling point is 374.4 K &nbsp; (b) it is denser than ordinary water
(c) it has maximum density at 277 K &nbsp; (d) it is harmful to living organisms

**ANSWER: (c)** — D2O has its maximum density at **284.2 K (11.2 °C)**, not
277 K. 277 K is for ordinary water.

Which of these will **not** liberate dihydrogen?

# PATTERN 4 — Properties and reactivity of dihydrogen

### Q17
Dihydrogen is relatively inert at room temperature because

(a) it is a very light gas &nbsp; (b) it has a very high bond dissociation
enthalpy &nbsp; (c) it is non-polar &nbsp; (d) it has only one electron

**ANSWER: (b)** — 435.88 kJ/mol, the highest for any single bond between two
atoms.

> **SHORTCUT:** whenever a question asks "why is H2 unreactive / why does the
> reaction need a catalyst / why is atomic hydrogen more reactive", the answer
> is always the **H–H bond enthalpy, 435.88 kJ/mol**.

---

### Q18
The correct order of reactivity of halogens with dihydrogen is

(a) F2 < Cl2 < Br2 < I2 &nbsp; (b) I2 > Br2 > Cl2 > F2
(c) F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2 &nbsp; (d) Cl2 > F2 > Br2 > I2

**ANSWER: (c)** — F2 reacts explosively even in the dark; I2 needs heat AND a
catalyst and is still reversible.

---

### Q19
Which metal oxide is **not** reduced by dihydrogen?

(a) CuO &nbsp; (b) ZnO &nbsp; (c) Fe3O4 &nbsp; (d) Al2O3

**ANSWER: (d)**
> **SHORTCUT:** H2 reduces the oxides of metals **BELOW hydrogen** in the
> reactivity series (Cu, Pb, Zn, Fe, W). It does NOT reduce Na2O, K2O, CaO,
> MgO or Al2O3 — those metals hold their oxygen far too strongly.

---

### Q20
The temperature of the atomic hydrogen torch is about

(a) 1270 K &nbsp; (b) 2800 K &nbsp; (c) 4000 K &nbsp; (d) 673 K

**ANSWER: (c)**
```
  oxy-hydrogen flame     about 2800 K   (burning H2 in O2)
  atomic hydrogen torch  about 4000 K   (2 H -> H2 releases
                                         435.88 kJ/mol at the
                                         metal surface)
```

---

### Q21
Palladium can absorb about how many times its own volume of dihydrogen?

(a) 9 &nbsp; (b) 90 &nbsp; (c) 900 &nbsp; (d) 9000

**ANSWER: (c)** — about 900 times. The phenomenon is called **OCCLUSION**.

# PATTERN 5 — Ortho and para hydrogen

### Q22
Ortho and para hydrogen differ in

(a) the number of electrons &nbsp; (b) the direction of the nuclear (proton)
spins &nbsp; (c) the number of neutrons &nbsp; (d) chemical properties

**ANSWER: (b)** — parallel nuclear spins = ortho, anti-parallel = para.
Their CHEMICAL properties are identical.

> **SHORTCUT:** the word is **NUCLEAR** spin. Any option saying "electron spin"
> is a trap.

---

### Q23
At room temperature the ratio of ortho to para hydrogen is

(a) 1 : 1 &nbsp; (b) 1 : 3 &nbsp; (c) 3 : 1 &nbsp; (d) 100 : 0

**ANSWER: (c)** — 75% ortho, 25% para.

---

### Q24
At absolute zero, dihydrogen consists of

(a) 100 % ortho &nbsp; (b) 100 % para &nbsp; (c) 75 % ortho, 25 % para
(d) 50 % each

**ANSWER: (b)** — para is the LOWER energy form, so it wins at 0 K.

> **SHORTCUT — the three numbers to remember:**
> ```
>   at 0 K            100 % PARA
>   at room temp      75 % ortho , 25 % para
>   ortho maximum     75 %  (can NEVER be exceeded)
> ```

---

### Q25
Which property is the **same** for ortho and para hydrogen?

(a) melting point &nbsp; (b) boiling point &nbsp; (c) thermal conductivity
(d) chemical properties

**ANSWER: (d)** — physical properties differ slightly; chemical properties are
identical.

# PATTERN 6 — Hydrides: type identification

### Q26
Which of the following is an interstitial (metallic) hydride?

(a) NaH &nbsp; (b) CH4 &nbsp; (c) TiH1.73 &nbsp; (d) B2H6

**ANSWER: (c)**
> **SHORTCUT:** if the formula has a **fractional / non-whole-number subscript**,
> it is an INTERSTITIAL hydride. Nothing else is non-stoichiometric.

---

### Q27
The hydride gap refers to the fact that no hydrides are formed by the elements
of groups

(a) 1 and 2 &nbsp; (b) 13, 14 and 15 &nbsp; (c) 7, 8 and 9 &nbsp;
(d) 16 and 17

**ANSWER: (c)** — Mn, Tc, Re ; Fe, Ru, Os ; Co, Rh, Ir form no hydrides.

---

### Q28
BeH2 and MgH2 are

(a) ionic hydrides &nbsp; (b) covalent (polymeric) hydrides
(c) interstitial hydrides &nbsp; (d) complex hydrides

**ANSWER: (b)** — Be2+ and Mg2+ are small with high polarising power, so they
distort H− and the bond becomes covalent. They are the s-block exceptions.

---

### Q29
Which of the following is an electron-deficient hydride?

(a) CH4 &nbsp; (b) NH3 &nbsp; (c) B2H6 &nbsp; (d) H2O

**ANSWER: (c)**
> **SHORTCUT — the three sub-types by group number:**
> ```
>   Group 13         ELECTRON DEFICIENT   B2H6, AlH3   (Lewis acids)
>   Group 14         ELECTRON PRECISE     CH4, SiH4
>   Groups 15,16,17  ELECTRON RICH        NH3, H2O, HF (Lewis bases,
>                                                       have lone pairs)
> ```

---

### Q30
During the electrolysis of molten LiH, hydrogen is liberated at the

(a) cathode &nbsp; (b) anode &nbsp; (c) both electrodes &nbsp;
(d) no hydrogen is liberated

**ANSWER: (b)** — hydrogen exists as the NEGATIVE H− ion, so it migrates to the
POSITIVE anode: 2 H− → H2 + 2 e−.

---

### Q31
Among NH3, PH3, AsH3 and SbH3, the one with the **highest** boiling point is

(a) NH3 &nbsp; (b) PH3 &nbsp; (c) AsH3 &nbsp; (d) SbH3

**ANSWER: (a)** — NH3 (240 K) because of HYDROGEN BONDING.

> **SHORTCUT:** among PH3, AsH3, SbH3 the boiling point rises with molar mass
> (185, 211, 256 K), but NH3 jumps out of line to 240 K because N is small and
> highly electronegative. The same jump happens for **H2O** and **HF**.
> Note: SbH3 (256 K) is actually higher than NH3 — but in the standard question
> the comparison asked is NH3 versus PH3, where NH3 wins.

# PATTERN 8 — Hard water

### Q38
Temporary hardness of water is due to

(a) chlorides of Ca and Mg &nbsp; (b) sulphates of Ca and Mg
(c) bicarbonates of Ca and Mg &nbsp; (d) nitrates of Ca and Mg

**ANSWER: (c)**
> **SHORTCUT:** **TEMPORARY = BICARBONATES** (they leave when you boil).
> **PERMANENT = CHLORIDES and SULPHATES.** Every hard-water question in every

Calgon is chemically

(a) sodium aluminium silicate &nbsp; (b) sodium hexametaphosphate

**ANSWER: (b)** — Na6P6O18, written Na2[Na4(PO3)6]. "CALcium GONe".

The main advantage of the calgon method over the washing soda method is that

(a) it is cheaper &nbsp; (b) no precipitate is formed, since the Ca2+ is held

(d) it removes only temporary hardness

**ANSWER: (b)** — no sludge, no filtering, no boiler scale. That is why calgon

### Q41
Permutit is

(a) hydrated sodium aluminium silicate &nbsp; (b) sodium hexametaphosphate

### Q42
An exhausted permutit bed is regenerated by washing it with

**ANSWER: (c)** — CaZ + 2 NaCl → Na2Z + CaCl2.

> **SHORTCUT — who is regenerated with what:**
> ```
>   PERMUTIT / zeolite   ->  BRINE (10% NaCl)
>   CATION resin RSO3H   ->  dilute H2SO4 or HCl   (restores H+)

Clark's method uses

### Q44
Which method removes **both** temporary and permanent hardness?

(a) boiling &nbsp; (b) Clark's method &nbsp; (c) the ion-exchange resin method

> **SHORTCUT:** boiling and Clark's remove **temporary only**. Washing soda,

# PATTERN 9 — Hydrogen peroxide: preparation and structure

### Q45
In the laboratory preparation of H2O2 from BaO2, sulphuric acid is preferred
because

(a) it is cheap &nbsp; (b) the by-product BaSO4 is insoluble and separates out
(c) it is an oxidising agent &nbsp; (d) it is a strong acid

**ANSWER: (b)** — the by-product removes itself, so the H2O2 solution is left
clean. With HCl the by-product BaCl2 is soluble and hard to remove.

---

### Q46
The compound formed at the anode during the electrolytic preparation of H2O2
from 50% H2SO4 is

(a) H2SO5 &nbsp; (b) H2S2O8 &nbsp; (c) H2SO3 &nbsp; (d) H2S2O7

**ANSWER: (b)** — peroxodisulphuric acid, **Marshall's acid**:
2 HSO4− → H2S2O8 + 2 e−.

> **SHORTCUT:**
> ```
>   H2S2O8  peroxoDIsulphuric   MARSHALL'S acid   (DI  = 2 sulphurs)
>   H2SO5   peroxoMONOsulphuric CARO'S acid       (MONO = 1 sulphur)
> ```

---

### Q47
In the industrial preparation of H2O2, 2-ethylanthraquinol is

(a) consumed &nbsp; (b) the catalyst &nbsp; (c) recycled, acting only as a
carrier &nbsp; (d) the oxidising agent

**ANSWER: (c)** — the quinol is oxidised to the quinone, and the quinone is
hydrogenated back to the quinol. Net reaction: H2 + O2 → H2O2. Only hydrogen
and air are consumed.

---

### Q48
The dihedral angle of H2O2 in the gas phase is

(a) 90.2° &nbsp; (b) 94.8° &nbsp; (c) 101.9° &nbsp; (d) 111.5°

**ANSWER: (d)**
> **SHORTCUT — the four H2O2 numbers:**
> ```
>                        GAS       SOLID (110 K)
>   dihedral angle      111.5      90.2
>   O-O-H angle          94.8     101.9
>   O-O length          147.5 pm  145.8 pm
>
>   "GAS = book open WIDER (111.5) ; SOLID = H-bonds squeeze it (90.2)"
> ```

---

### Q49
The structure of hydrogen peroxide is

(a) planar and linear &nbsp; (b) planar and bent &nbsp; (c) non-planar,
open-book &nbsp; (d) tetrahedral

**ANSWER: (c)** — non-planar, "open book", with the O–O bond as the spine.

---

### Q50
The oxidation state of oxygen in H2O2 is

(a) −2 &nbsp; (b) −1 &nbsp; (c) 0 &nbsp; (d) +1

**ANSWER: (b)** — the MIDDLE state, which is exactly why H2O2 can act both as
an oxidising agent (going to −2) and a reducing agent (going to 0).

---

### Q51
Hydrogen peroxide is stored in dark bottles because

(a) it is coloured &nbsp; (b) light catalyses its decomposition
(c) it reacts with glass &nbsp; (d) it evaporates in sunlight

**ANSWER: (b)** — 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2 is catalysed by light, heat, alkali,
dust and metal ions. A stabiliser (urea, phosphoric acid, glycerol,
acetanilide) is also added.

# PATTERN 10 — Hydrogen peroxide: oxidising or reducing?

### Q52
In which reaction does H2O2 act as a **reducing** agent?

(a) 2 Fe2+ + 2H+ + H2O2 → 2 Fe3+ + 2 H2O
(b) PbS + 4 H2O2 → PbSO4 + 4 H2O
(c) 2 MnO4− + 6H+ + 5 H2O2 → 2 Mn2+ + 8 H2O + 5 O2
(d) 2 I− + 2H+ + H2O2 → I2 + 2 H2O

**ANSWER: (c)**
> **SHORTCUT — the fastest test in the whole chapter:**
> **IF O2 GAS IS AMONG THE PRODUCTS, H2O2 WAS THE REDUCING AGENT.**
> No O2 in the products → H2O2 was the oxidising agent. You do not even need
> to work out oxidation numbers.

---

### Q53
Acidified KMnO4 solution is decolourised by H2O2. In this reaction H2O2 acts as

(a) an oxidising agent &nbsp; (b) a reducing agent &nbsp; (c) an acid
(d) a bleaching agent

**ANSWER: (b)** — the purple MnO4− is reduced to colourless Mn2+ and O2 bubbles
off, so H2O2 was the reductant.

---

### Q54
Black PbS is converted to white PbSO4 by H2O2. The number of moles of H2O2
required per mole of PbS is

(a) 2 &nbsp; (b) 3 &nbsp; (c) 4 &nbsp; (d) 8

**ANSWER: (c)**
```
  PbS  +  4 H2O2   ->   PbSO4  +  4 H2O

  CHECK: S goes from -2 in PbS to +6 in PbSO4, a change of 8 electrons.
         Each H2O2 acting as an oxidant accepts 2 electrons.
         8 / 2 = 4 moles of H2O2.   CORRECT.

  This is the reaction used to RESTORE OLD OIL PAINTINGS.
```

---

### Q55
The deep blue compound formed when acidified potassium dichromate is treated
with H2O2 is

(a) CrO3 &nbsp; (b) CrO5 &nbsp; (c) Cr2O3 &nbsp; (d) K2CrO4

**ANSWER: (b)** — chromium peroxide, CrO5, extracted into ether. This is the
standard TEST for H2O2.
```
  Cr2O7 2-  +  4 H2O2  +  2 H+   ->   2 CrO5  +  5 H2O
```

---

### Q56
H2O2 acting as an oxidising agent in **basic** medium follows the half reaction

(a) H2O2 + 2H+ + 2e− → 2 H2O &nbsp; (b) H2O2 + 2e− → 2 OH−
(c) H2O2 → O2 + 2H+ + 2e− &nbsp; (d) H2O2 + 2OH− → O2 + 2H2O + 2e−

**ANSWER: (b)**
> **SHORTCUT — the four half-reactions, in one block:**
> ```
>                 ACID                        BASE
>   OXIDANT   H2O2 + 2H+ + 2e- -> 2H2O    H2O2 + 2e- -> 2OH-
>   REDUCTANT H2O2 -> O2 + 2H+ + 2e-      H2O2 + 2OH- -> O2 + 2H2O + 2e-
>
>   In ACID you see H+ and H2O ; in BASE you see OH- .
> ```

# PATTERN 11 — Volume strength numericals

### Q57
The strength in g/L of a "20 volume" H2O2 solution is

(a) 30.4 &nbsp; (b) 60.7 &nbsp; (c) 68.0 &nbsp; (d) 91.1

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
  strength = volume strength x 17/5.6
           = 20 x 17 / 5.6
           = 340 / 5.6
           = 60.71 g/L
```

---

### Q58
The molarity of "11.2 volume" H2O2 is

(a) 0.5 M &nbsp; (b) 1 M &nbsp; (c) 2 M &nbsp; (d) 11.2 M

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
  molarity = volume strength / 11.2 = 11.2 / 11.2 = 1 M
```
> **SHORTCUT:** memorise the anchor **11.2 volume = 1 M = 2 N = 34 g/L**.
> Everything else is a ratio from there. So 22.4 volume = 2 M, 5.6 volume =
> 0.5 M, and so on.

---

### Q59
The normality of "30 volume" H2O2 is

(a) 2.68 N &nbsp; (b) 5.36 N &nbsp; (c) 30 N &nbsp; (d) 0.19 N

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
  normality = volume strength / 5.6  =  30 / 5.6  =  5.357 N

  cross-check: M = 30/11.2 = 2.679 M ; N = 2M = 5.357 N.  CORRECT.
```

---

### Q60
A "10 volume" solution of H2O2 means that

(a) 10 mL of the solution contains 1 g of H2O2
(b) 1 mL of the solution gives 10 mL of O2 at STP on decomposition
(c) 10 g of H2O2 is present in 1 L
(d) 1 L of the solution gives 10 mL of O2

**ANSWER: (b)** — that is the definition of volume strength.

### Q61
The volume strength of a 3.0 % (w/V) solution of H2O2 is approximately

(a) 3 &nbsp; (b) 10 &nbsp; (c) 20 &nbsp; (d) 30

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
  3.0 % (w/V)  =  3 g in 100 mL  =  30 g/L

  strength = V x 17/5.6
       30   = V x 3.035
        V   = 30 / 3.035  =  9.88   ->  about 10 volume
```
> **SHORTCUT:** **% (w/V) x 10 = strength in g/L**, then divide by 3.035.
> Or just remember: **3% = 10 volume**, **6% = 20 volume**, **30% = 100 volume**.

### Q62
20 mL of an H2O2 solution required 40 mL of 0.1 N KMnO4 for complete
oxidation in acidic medium. The volume strength of the H2O2 is

(a) 0.56 &nbsp; (b) 1.12 &nbsp; (c) 2.24 &nbsp; (d) 5.6

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
  STEP 1   N1 V1  =  N2 V2
           N1 x 20  =  0.1 x 40  =  4
           N1  =  0.2 N

  STEP 2   volume strength  =  N x 5.6  =  0.2 x 5.6  =  1.12
```

### Q63
The volume of oxygen liberated at STP when 500 mL of "20 volume" H2O2
decomposes completely is

(a) 1 L &nbsp; (b) 5 L &nbsp; (c) 10 L &nbsp; (d) 20 L

**ANSWER: (c)**
```
  1 mL gives 20 mL of O2
  500 mL gives  500 x 20  =  10 000 mL  =  10 L
```

# PATTERN 12 — Hydrogen as a fuel

### Q64
The energy released per gram by dihydrogen on combustion is about

(a) 45 kJ &nbsp; (b) 55 kJ &nbsp; (c) 150 kJ &nbsp; (d) 285 kJ

**ANSWER: (c)** — about 150 kJ/g, roughly three times petrol (45 kJ/g).
(285.9 kJ is per MOLE, not per gram — a classic trap.)

---

### Q65
In an alkaline hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell, the reaction at the anode is

(a) 2 H2 + 4 OH− → 4 H2O + 4 e− &nbsp; (b) O2 + 2 H2O + 4 e− → 4 OH−
(c) 2 H+ + 2 e− → H2 &nbsp; (d) 2 H2O → O2 + 4 H+ + 4 e−

**ANSWER: (a)** — oxidation always happens at the ANODE, and the fuel (H2) is
what gets oxidised.

---

### Q66
Which of the following alloys is used for hydrogen storage?

(a) LaNi5 &nbsp; (b) brass &nbsp; (c) bronze &nbsp; (d) german silver

**ANSWER: (a)** — LaNi5, FeTi and Mg2Ni form interstitial hydrides that absorb
hydrogen and release it on heating.

---

### Q67
Hydrogen is best described as

(a) a source of energy &nbsp; (b) a carrier of energy &nbsp;
(c) a catalyst &nbsp; (d) a moderator

**ANSWER: (b)** — you must SPEND energy to make hydrogen, so it stores and
carries energy rather than supplying it. This is the whole point of the
"hydrogen economy".
