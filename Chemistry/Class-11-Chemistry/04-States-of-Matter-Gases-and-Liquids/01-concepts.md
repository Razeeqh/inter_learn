# TOPIC 1 — INTERMOLECULAR FORCES

## 1.1 What are they?

**Intermolecular forces** are the forces of attraction and repulsion **BETWEEN**
molecules. They are *not* chemical bonds.

```
   H --- O --- H        H --- O --- H
   \___________/        \___________/
        ^                     ^
        |                     |
   INTRA-molecular       INTRA-molecular
   (covalent BOND,       (covalent BOND)
    very strong)
        <------ this gap ------>
                   ^
                   |
          INTER-molecular force
          (weak attraction, this
           chapter is about THIS)
```

> **TRAP:** A very common 2-mark trap. "Intramolecular" = inside one molecule = a
> chemical bond. "Intermolecular" = between two molecules = the weak attraction.
> Boiling a liquid breaks **inter**molecular forces, NOT the bonds inside the molecule.
> When water boils it stays H2O — it does not turn into H and O.

All attractive intermolecular forces together are called **van der Waals forces**.
There are three kinds of van der Waals force, plus hydrogen bonding which is treated
separately because it is much stronger.

```
                   INTERMOLECULAR FORCES
                            |
        +-------------------+-------------------+
        |                                       |
   VAN DER WAALS FORCES                  HYDROGEN BONDING
        |                                (special, strongest of
        |                                 the intermolecular forces)
   +----+-----------+---------------+
   |                |               |
 DISPERSION    DIPOLE-DIPOLE   DIPOLE-INDUCED
 (LONDON)                        DIPOLE
   |                |               |
 in ALL         only in POLAR   polar + non-polar
 molecules      molecules       together
```

---

## 1.2 Dispersion forces (London forces)

**Present in EVERY molecule — polar or non-polar, atom or molecule.**
They are the ONLY force acting between non-polar molecules like H2, Cl2, CH4 and
the noble gases.

**How they arise:** electrons are always moving. At any instant, by pure chance,
more electrons may sit on one side of an atom than the other. That instant creates a
**temporary (instantaneous) dipole**. That dipole then pushes the electrons of the
neighbouring atom away, creating an **induced dipole** in it. The two dipoles attract.

```
   AT ONE INSTANT (electrons momentarily on the left):

        ATOM A                     ATOM B
    +-----------+              +-----------+
    | e e e     |              |  e   e    |     (still symmetrical)
    |   NUCLEUS |              |  NUCLEUS  |
    +-----------+              +-----------+
       -     +
    instantaneous dipole


   A MOMENT LATER  -  A has INDUCED a dipole in B:

        ATOM A                     ATOM B
    +-----------+              +-----------+
    | e e e     |              | e e e     |
    |   NUCLEUS |              |  NUCLEUS  |
    +-----------+              +-----------+
       -     +          <-->      -     +
                    ATTRACTION
                  (+ of A pulls - of B)
```

**Strength depends on:**

| Factor | Effect | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Size of the molecule / number of electrons** | bigger -> STRONGER | more electrons, easier to distort |
| **Polarisability** | more polarisable -> STRONGER | electron cloud deforms more easily |
| **Shape** | long/linear -> STRONGER than spherical | more surface area to touch |
| **Distance r between molecules** | force is proportional to `1 / r^6` | dies away extremely fast |

```
   Boiling points rise down the halogens and the noble gases because the
   dispersion force grows with size:

      F2   (-188 degC)  <  Cl2 (-34 degC)  <  Br2 (59 degC)  <  I2 (184 degC)
      He   (-269 degC)  <  Ne (-246)  <  Ar (-186)  <  Kr (-152)  <  Xe (-108)

   Shape effect at the SAME molar mass (both C5H12, M = 72):

      n-pentane  (long, sausage-shaped)   b.p. = 36 degC   more contact
      neopentane (compact, ball-shaped)   b.p. =  9 degC   less contact
```

## 1.3 Dipole-dipole forces

Act between **two POLAR molecules** (molecules with a permanent dipole, i.e. a
permanent positive end and a permanent negative end).

```
     H --> Cl            H --> Cl
   d+      d-          d+      d-

   Molecules line up head to tail so that opposite charges are near:

        d+   d-        d+   d-
        H ---- Cl ---- H ---- Cl
                  ^
             ATTRACTION between the d- of one Cl
             and the d+ of the next H
```

- Stronger than dispersion for molecules of comparable size.
- Weaker than a real ionic interaction, because the charges are only partial.
- Interaction energy varies as **`1 / r^3`** for rotating polar molecules in a gas,
  and as `1 / r^6` when the molecules are free to rotate at ordinary temperature.

**Examples:** HCl, HBr, SO2, CH3Cl, acetone (CH3COCH3), NH3 (also H-bonds).

```
   Compare two molecules of almost the SAME molar mass:

      N2   (M = 28, NON-polar)     b.p. = -196 degC   only dispersion
      CO   (M = 28, slightly polar) b.p. = -192 degC   dispersion + dipole
```

---

## 1.4 Dipole-induced dipole forces

Act between **one POLAR molecule and one NON-POLAR molecule.**

The permanent dipole of the polar molecule distorts the electron cloud of the
non-polar molecule and *induces* a dipole in it. Then the two attract.

```
      POLAR                     NON-POLAR
    +--------+                 +----------+
    | d+  d- |     ------->    |   even   |
    | H -- Cl|                 | electron |
    +--------+                 |  cloud   |
                               +----------+
                                    |
                                    v  (cloud is pushed)
    +--------+                 +----------+
    | d+  d- |  <-attracts->   | d+    d- |
    | H -- Cl|                 |          |
    +--------+                 +----------+
                                INDUCED dipole
```

**Strength depends on:** the **dipole moment** of the polar molecule, and the
**polarisability** (size) of the non-polar molecule. Energy varies as `1 / r^6`.

**This is why oxygen and nitrogen dissolve (a little) in water, and why noble gases
can be dissolved in water at all.** Without it, fish could not breathe.

## 1.5 Hydrogen bonding

The **strongest** of the intermolecular forces (about 10-40 kJ/mol, compared to
0.05-40 kJ/mol for other van der Waals forces, but only about 1/10 of a covalent bond).

**Condition:** hydrogen must be bonded directly to a **highly electronegative, small
atom — F, O or N** — and there must be a lone pair on the F, O or N of another molecule.

```
  Remember the three letters:   F , O , N     ->   "FON"

           d+     d-           d+     d-
           H ---- O  . . . . . H ---- O
                  |                   |
                  H                   H
                     ^
                     |
              HYDROGEN BOND (drawn as a DOTTED line)
```

**Water is the classic case:**

```
                H            H
                 \          /
                  O        O
                  |  .    .|
             H    |   .  . |    H
              \   H     ..  H   /
               O . . . . . . . O
              /                 \
             H                   H

   Each water molecule can form up to FOUR hydrogen bonds
   (2 through its own H atoms, 2 through its 2 lone pairs).
```

**Consequences of hydrogen bonding (memorise this list, it earns marks):**

| Observation | Because |
|---|---|
| H2O (100 degC) boils much higher than H2S (-60 degC) | H2O has H-bonds, H2S does not |
| HF boils higher than HCl | H-bonding in HF |
| NH3 boils higher than PH3 | H-bonding in NH3 |
| Ice is LESS dense than water and floats | open cage-like H-bonded structure |
| Alcohols and acids are liquids, alkanes of same M are gases | H-bonding |
| Water has a very high surface tension and viscosity | H-bonding |

```
   THE FAMOUS ANOMALY GRAPH

   b.p.
   (degC)
    120 |
        |  H2O
    100 |  *
        |   \
     60 |    \                                         H2Te
        | HF  \                                        *
     20 |  *   \                              H2Se  ../
        |   \   \                    HCl      *  ../
    -20 |    \   \        NH3        *    ..'   ./
        |     \   \       *      ..'    ./     /
    -60 |      \   \  ..'      ./      /    H2S
        |       \  .\'        /       /     *
   -100 |        \/  \       /   PH3 /
        |            *      *    *  *
        |          H2S    HCl  PH3
        +----+------+------+------+------> period (2, 3, 4, 5)
             2      3      4      5

   H2O, HF and NH3 stick out ABOVE the trend line. That "bump" is
   hydrogen bonding. CH4 shows NO bump because carbon is not
   electronegative enough.
```

## 1.6 Comparing the strengths

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                    |
  |  WEAKEST  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  ->  STRONGEST    |
  |                                                                    |
  |  dispersion  <  dipole-induced  <  dipole-dipole  <  hydrogen      |
  |  (London)       dipole                               bonding       |
  |                                                                    |
  |  ... all of these are far weaker than a COVALENT or IONIC bond.    |
  |                                                                    |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+

  BUT: for very large molecules, dispersion forces can add up and beat
  everything else. That is why iodine (I2, non-polar) is a SOLID while
  HCl (polar) is a GAS.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 1.1

**Question:** Name the type(s) of intermolecular force present in
(a) HCl and HCl (b) He and He (c) H2O and H2O (d) HCl and Ar.

**ANSWER:**
```
  (a)  HCl ... HCl      both polar
                        ->  DISPERSION  +  DIPOLE-DIPOLE

  (b)  He ... He        both non-polar (atoms)
                        ->  DISPERSION only (London forces)

  (c)  H2O ... H2O      polar, H bonded to O
                        ->  DISPERSION + DIPOLE-DIPOLE + HYDROGEN BONDING

  (d)  HCl ... Ar       one polar, one non-polar
                        ->  DISPERSION + DIPOLE-INDUCED DIPOLE
```

> **TRAP:** Dispersion forces are present in **every** case. Never write
> "only dipole-dipole" — always include dispersion as well.

# TOPIC 2 — THERMAL ENERGY AND THE THREE STATES

## 2.1 Thermal energy

**Thermal energy** is the energy of a body arising from the motion of its particles.
It is directly proportional to the **absolute temperature**. It is the measure of the
**average kinetic energy** of the particles.

```
   Thermal energy  =  the "KICKING" that tries to scatter the molecules
   Intermolecular  =  the "GLUE" that tries to hold them together
    force
```

## 2.2 The balance decides the state

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                       |
  |   GLUE  >>  KICKING       ->      S O L I D                           |
  |   molecules locked in place, only vibrate                             |
  |                                                                       |
  |            heat it up (kicking increases)                             |
  |                       |                                               |
  |                       v                                               |
  |                                                                       |
  |   GLUE  ~=  KICKING       ->      L I Q U I D                         |
  |   molecules stay close but slide past each other                      |
  |                                                                       |
  |            heat it up more                                            |
  |                       |                                               |
  |                       v                                               |
  |                                                                       |
  |   KICKING  >>  GLUE       ->      G A S                               |
  |   molecules fly apart and fill the whole container                    |
  |                                                                       |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------------+

   Going the other way:  COOL a gas  (less kicking)  ->  liquid  ->  solid
                         COMPRESS a gas (molecules forced close, glue
                         becomes effective)  ->  liquid
```

**This one idea is the whole reason the chapter exists.** Any question that asks
"why does a substance exist as a solid / liquid / gas?" is answered with this balance.

## 2.3 The comparison table (learn to reproduce it)

```
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |   PROPERTY         |      SOLID       |      LIQUID      |       GAS        |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Shape             | DEFINITE         | takes the shape  | fills the whole  |
  |                    |                  | of the container | container        |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Volume            | DEFINITE         | DEFINITE         | NOT definite     |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Intermolecular    | very STRONG      | moderate         | negligible /     |
  |   forces           |                  |                  | very weak        |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Thermal energy    | lowest           | moderate         | highest          |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Distance between  | very small       | small            | very large       |
  |   particles        |                  |                  |                  |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Type of motion    | vibration about  | vibration +      | free, rapid,     |
  |                    | a fixed position | translation +    | random in all    |
  |                    |                  | rotation         | directions       |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Density           | very high        | high             | very low         |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Compressibility   | negligible       | very small       | very LARGE       |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Diffusion         | negligible       | slow             | very fast        |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
  |  Fluidity (flow)   | does not flow    | flows            | flows            |
  +--------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
```

## 2.4 The measurable properties of a gas

Any sample of gas is described by exactly **four** measurable quantities:

```
   P  =  pressure       (atm, bar, Pa, mm Hg, torr)
   V  =  volume         (L, mL, m^3, dm^3)
   T  =  temperature    (KELVIN, always)
   n  =  amount         (moles)

   The gas laws are simply the statements of HOW these four are related.
```

```
  UNIT CONVERSIONS (write these on the top of your answer sheet)

  1 atm   =  760 mm Hg  =  760 torr  =  101325 Pa  =  101.325 kPa
          =  1.01325 bar
  1 bar   =  10^5 Pa  =  100 kPa  =  0.9869 atm
  1 Pa    =  1 N m^-2
  1 L     =  1 dm^3  =  1000 cm^3  =  10^-3 m^3
  T (K)   =  t (degC) + 273.15    (in most problems just use + 273)
```

# TOPIC 3 — BOYLE'S LAW

## 3.1 Statement

> **At constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of a gas is inversely
> proportional to its pressure.**

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |         1                                                       |
  |   V  ~  ---        (at constant T and constant n)               |
  |         P                                                       |
  |                                                                 |
  |   P V  =  k1       (a constant)                                 |
  |                                                                 |
  |   Therefore for two states of the SAME gas sample:              |
  |                                                                 |
  |            P1 V1   =   P2 V2                                    |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Everyday picture:** squeeze a sealed syringe with your thumb on the nozzle. The
plunger moves in. You increased P, so V went down.

## 3.2 The graphs (both are asked)

```
   GRAPH 1:  P  vs  V     ->   a RECTANGULAR HYPERBOLA
                               (called an ISOTHERM, "same temperature")

    P |
      | *
      |  *
      |   *
      |    **
      |      ***                     T3   (higher T = curve further out)
      |         *****
      |             ********         T2
      |                     ******** T1
      +--------------------------------> V

    Each curve is drawn at ONE fixed temperature -> ISOTHERM.
    Higher temperature curves lie FURTHER from the origin.


   GRAPH 2:  P  vs  1/V   ->   a STRAIGHT LINE through the origin
                               (this is the graph examiners love)

    P |                    *
      |                 *
      |              *
      |           *          slope = k1 = P V  (a constant)
      |        *
      |     *
      |  *
      +-------------------------> 1/V
     0


   GRAPH 3:  P V  vs  P     ->   a HORIZONTAL STRAIGHT LINE

   PV |
      |
      |------------------------  T2
      |
      |------------------------  T1
      |
      +-------------------------> P
     0
      (PV does not change with P at a fixed T. This is the test for
       ideal behaviour - a real gas curve BENDS here.)


   GRAPH 4:  V  vs  1/P    ->   a STRAIGHT LINE through the origin
```

> **TRAP:** "P vs V" is a **curve** (hyperbola). "P vs 1/V" is a **straight line**.
> Students mix these two up constantly and lose the whole 2 marks.

## 3.3 Density form

Since density `d = mass / V` and mass is fixed:

```
              m            m
    V  =  --------  ->  ------- = constant  ->   d  ~  P
              d           d P

    At constant temperature, DENSITY is directly proportional to PRESSURE.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 3.1

**Question:** A gas occupies 2.9 L at a pressure of 760 mm Hg. What pressure is
needed to compress it into 4.0 L at the same temperature?

**ANSWER:**
```
  Given:   P1 = 760 mm Hg     V1 = 2.9 L
           P2 = ?             V2 = 4.0 L        T constant  -> Boyle's law

  Boyle:   P1 V1  =  P2 V2

                    P1 V1        760  x  2.9        2204
           P2  =  ---------  =  --------------  =  --------
                     V2              4.0             4.0

           P2  =  551  mm Hg

  SENSE CHECK: the volume got BIGGER, so the pressure must get SMALLER.
               551 < 760.  Correct.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 3.2

**Question:** A balloon contains 500 mL of helium at 1 atm. It rises to a height
where the pressure is 0.4 atm. What is its new volume? (Temperature unchanged.)

**ANSWER:**
```
  P1 V1 = P2 V2

                 P1 V1        1  x  500        500
        V2  =  ---------  =  -----------  =  -------  =  1250 mL  =  1.25 L
                   P2            0.4           0.4

  The balloon expands to 2.5 times its size. That is why weather balloons
  are only partly filled at the ground - otherwise they burst.
```

# TOPIC 4 — CHARLES'S LAW

## 4.1 Statement

> **At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of a gas is directly
> proportional to its absolute (kelvin) temperature.**

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |   V  ~  T          (at constant P and constant n)               |
  |                                                                 |
  |    V                                                            |
  |   ---  =  k2       (a constant)                                 |
  |    T                                                            |
  |                                                                 |
  |          V1        V2                                           |
  |         ----   =  ----                                          |
  |          T1        T2                                           |
  |                                                                 |
  |   T MUST BE IN KELVIN.                                          |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Original form:** for every 1 degC rise in temperature, a gas expands by
**1/273.15** of its volume at 0 degC.

```
                    +-           t     -+
        V(t)  =  V0 |  1  +  ---------  |
                    +-        273.15   -+
```

**Everyday picture:** a half-flat football left in the sun puffs up. Heat -> more
volume at the same (atmospheric) pressure.

## 4.2 The graph — isobars and ABSOLUTE ZERO

```
   V  vs  T (in kelvin)  ->  straight line THROUGH the origin
   Each line is at one fixed pressure  ->  called an ISOBAR

    V |                                *   P1 (low pressure, steep)
      |                          *   *
      |                    *   *   *
      |              *   *   *          *   P2
      |        *   *   *         *
      |   * *  *   *      *          *      P3 (high pressure, shallow)
      |  * *  *  *   *
      +--------------------------------------> T (K)
      0

   Lower pressure -> larger volume -> STEEPER line.


   V  vs  t (in degrees CELSIUS)  ->  straight lines that all meet at
                                      ONE point on the temperature axis

    V |                                       *
      |                                  *  *
      |                             * *  *
      |                        * *   *
      |                   * *  *
      |              * *  *
      |         * *  *
      |   (extrapolated, dotted)
      | . . . . . . . .
      +---*----------+-----------+-----------+------> t (degC)
     -273.15         0          100         200
        ^
        |
   ABSOLUTE ZERO - the temperature at which the volume of an ideal gas
   would become ZERO. All the isobars, extended backwards, meet here.
   No substance can actually be cooled to it - the gas liquefies first.
   This point is the ZERO of the KELVIN scale:  0 K = -273.15 degC.
```

**Absolute zero (definition to write in the exam):** *the lowest conceivable
temperature, -273.15 degC, at which the volume of an ideal gas would theoretically
become zero. It is taken as the zero of the kelvin (absolute) scale.*

## WORKED EXAMPLE 4.1

**Question:** A gas occupies 600 mL at 27 degC. What volume will it occupy at
127 degC if the pressure stays the same?

**ANSWER:**
```
  STEP 1  Convert to KELVIN (never skip this)
          T1 = 27  + 273  =  300 K
          T2 = 127 + 273  =  400 K

  STEP 2  Charles's law
              V1        V2
             ----  =   ----
              T1        T2

                   V1 T2        600  x  400        240000
          V2  =  ---------  =  -------------  =  ----------  =  800 mL
                    T1              300              300

  V2  =  800 mL

  SENSE CHECK: heated the gas, so it must EXPAND.  800 > 600.  Correct.
```

> **TRAP:** If you had used degrees Celsius, you would have got
> `600 x 127 / 27 = 2822 mL` — completely wrong. **KELVIN. ALWAYS.**

## WORKED EXAMPLE 4.2

**Question:** At what temperature (in degC) will 2.5 L of a gas at 27 degC shrink
to 2.0 L at constant pressure?

**ANSWER:**
```
  T1 = 27 + 273 = 300 K ,   V1 = 2.5 L ,   V2 = 2.0 L

              V1 T2
      V2  =  --------      ->      T2  =  T1 x V2 / V1
                T1

                     2.0            600
      T2  =  300 x  -----   =    --------   =  240 K
                     2.5            2.5

      t2  =  240 - 273  =   -33 degC
```

# TOPIC 5 — GAY-LUSSAC'S LAW (the pressure-temperature law)

## 5.1 Statement

> **At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of a gas is directly
> proportional to its absolute temperature.**

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |   P  ~  T          (at constant V and constant n)               |
  |                                                                 |
  |    P                              P1        P2                  |
  |   ---  =  k3                     ----  =   ----                 |
  |    T                              T1        T2                  |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Everyday picture:** a spray-can or an LPG cylinder warns "do not heat". Heating it
at fixed volume raises the pressure until it bursts. Also why car tyre pressure
rises after a long drive.

## 5.2 The graph — ISOCHORES

```
   P vs T (kelvin)   ->  straight line through the origin.
   Each line is at ONE fixed volume  ->  called an ISOCHORE
                                         (or isometric / isovolumetric line)

    P |                              *   V1 (small volume, steep)
      |                        *   *
      |                  *   *  *
      |            *   *  *        *     V2
      |      *   *  *        *
      |  * *  *  *      *         *      V3 (large volume, shallow)
      +----------------------------------> T (K)
      0

   In degrees Celsius the lines again extrapolate back to -273.15 degC.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 5.1

**Question:** A steel cylinder of fixed volume contains a gas at 2.0 atm and
27 degC. It is heated to 127 degC. What is the new pressure?

**ANSWER:**
```
  T1 = 300 K ,  T2 = 400 K ,  P1 = 2.0 atm ,  V constant

       P1        P2                        P1 T2
      ----  =   ----     ->      P2  =  ----------
       T1        T2                         T1

                 2.0  x  400          800
      P2  =   ---------------  =   --------   =   2.67 atm
                    300               300

  The pressure rises by a third. This is exactly why an aerosol can
  thrown into a fire explodes.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 5.2

**Question:** A tyre is inflated to 3.0 bar at 20 degC. After driving, the air
inside reaches 50 degC. What is the pressure now? (Assume the volume is unchanged.)

**ANSWER:**
```
  T1 = 20 + 273 = 293 K
  T2 = 50 + 273 = 323 K

                P1 T2        3.0 x 323          969
      P2  =  ----------  =  -------------  =  --------  =  3.31 bar
                 T1             293              293

  About a 10% rise. Check tyre pressure only when the tyres are COLD.
```

# TOPIC 6 — AVOGADRO'S LAW, THE AVOGADRO CONSTANT AND MOLAR VOLUME

## 6.1 Statement

> **Equal volumes of all gases, under the same conditions of temperature and
> pressure, contain an equal number of molecules.**

Equivalently: **at constant temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is
directly proportional to the number of moles.**

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |   V  ~  n          (at constant T and P)                        |
  |                                                                 |
  |    V                              V1        V2                  |
  |   ---  =  k4                     ----  =   ----                 |
  |    n                              n1        n2                  |
  |                                                                 |
  |                        m                                        |
  |   And since   n  =  --------  ,     V  ~  m / M                 |
  |                        M                                        |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

     m = mass of the gas in grams
     M = molar mass in g/mol
```

## 6.2 The Avogadro constant

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |   NA  =  6.022 x 10^23  particles per mole                     |
  |                                                                |
  |   1 mole of ANY substance contains 6.022 x 10^23 particles.    |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 6.3 Molar volume

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |   At STP (0 degC = 273.15 K  and  1 atm = 760 mm Hg):          |
  |                                                                |
  |          1 mole of ANY gas occupies   22.4  litres             |
  |                                                                |
  |   (IUPAC's newer STP uses 1 bar instead of 1 atm and gives     |
  |    22.7 litres. AP Inter uses 22.4 L unless told otherwise.)   |
  |                                                                |
  |   At 25 degC and 1 atm  ->  molar volume = 24.4 L              |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+

   So at STP:
        22.4 L of H2   =  1 mol  =  2 g    =  6.022 x 10^23 molecules
        22.4 L of O2   =  1 mol  =  32 g   =  6.022 x 10^23 molecules
        22.4 L of CO2  =  1 mol  =  44 g   =  6.022 x 10^23 molecules

   SAME volume, SAME number of molecules, DIFFERENT masses.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 6.1

**Question:** How many molecules are present in 5.6 L of nitrogen gas at STP?
What is its mass?

**ANSWER:**
```
  STEP 1  moles
                  volume at STP        5.6
           n  =  ----------------  =  -------  =  0.25 mol
                      22.4             22.4

  STEP 2  molecules
           N  =  n x NA  =  0.25  x  6.022 x 10^23
              =  1.5055 x 10^23   ~   1.51 x 10^23  molecules

  STEP 3  mass  (M of N2 = 28 g/mol)
           m  =  n x M  =  0.25 x 28  =  7 g
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 6.2

**Question:** 2 L of a gas contains 3.0 x 10^22 molecules at a certain T and P.
How many molecules will 5 L of ANOTHER gas contain at the same T and P?

**ANSWER:**
```
  Avogadro's law: equal volumes -> equal number of molecules,
  no matter WHICH gas it is.

  molecules per litre  =  3.0 x 10^22 / 2  =  1.5 x 10^22

  In 5 L:   5  x  1.5 x 10^22   =   7.5 x 10^22  molecules
```

# TOPIC 7 — THE IDEAL GAS EQUATION

## 7.1 Putting the four laws together

```
   Boyle      V ~ 1/P     (T, n constant)
   Charles    V ~ T       (P, n constant)
   Avogadro   V ~ n       (P, T constant)

   Combine all three:

                    n T                          n R T
          V   ~   -------          ->     V  =  -------
                     P                             P

  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |                    P  V   =   n  R  T                           |
  |                                                                 |
  |          THE IDEAL GAS EQUATION                                 |
  |                                                                 |
  |   R = the UNIVERSAL GAS CONSTANT (same for every gas)           |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

  A gas that obeys P V = n R T under ALL conditions of T and P is called
  an IDEAL GAS. No real gas is ideal - it is a model.
```

## 7.2 The values of R (a favourite 2-mark question)

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                    |
  |    R  =  0.0821   L atm K^-1 mol^-1     (P in atm, V in litres)    |
  |    R  =  0.0831   L bar K^-1 mol^-1     (P in bar, V in litres)    |
  |    R  =  8.314    J K^-1 mol^-1         (SI: P in Pa, V in m^3)    |
  |    R  =  8.314    Pa m^3 K^-1 mol^-1                               |
  |    R  =  2        cal K^-1 mol^-1       (1.987 cal, use 2)         |
  |    R  =  62.4     L mm Hg K^-1 mol^-1                              |
  |                                                                    |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+

  HOW R IS CALCULATED (show this if asked "derive the value of R"):

      For 1 mole of gas at STP:
          P = 1 atm ,  V = 22.4 L ,  T = 273.15 K ,  n = 1

               P V         1  x  22.4         22.4
       R  =  --------  =  -------------  =  ---------
               n T         1 x 273.15         273.15

          =  0.0821   L atm K^-1 mol^-1

      In SI units:
          P = 101325 Pa , V = 22.4 x 10^-3 m^3 , T = 273.15 K

               101325  x  22.4 x 10^-3            2269.7
       R  =  ----------------------------  =   ------------  =  8.31 J/K/mol
                    1  x  273.15                  273.15
```

> **TRAP:** Match R to the units in the question.
> P in **atm** and V in **litres** -> use **0.0821**.
> Anything in **SI / joules / m^3 / kg** -> use **8.314**.
> Mixing them is the most common numerical mistake in this chapter.

## 7.3 The combined gas law

If the **amount of gas n does not change** (a sealed sample), then `nR` is a
constant, so `PV / T` is constant:

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |            P1 V1            P2 V2                               |
  |          ---------   =   ---------                              |
  |             T1              T2                                  |
  |                                                                 |
  |     THE COMBINED GAS LAW                                        |
  |     Use it whenever a gas goes from one set of                  |
  |     conditions to another and NO gas escapes.                   |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

  Each individual gas law falls out of this:
     T constant  ->  P1 V1 = P2 V2                    (Boyle)
     P constant  ->  V1/T1 = V2/T2                    (Charles)
     V constant  ->  P1/T1 = P2/T2                    (Gay-Lussac)
```

## 7.4 The density and molar mass forms

```
   Start from   P V  =  n R T   and put   n  =  m / M :

                     m                             m R T
          P V  =  ------- R T      ->     M  =  -----------
                     M                             P V

   Now put   m / V  =  d   (density):

  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |          d R T                          P M                     |
  |   M  =  --------              d  =  -----------                 |
  |            P                            R T                     |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

   Read them out loud:
     "Density of a gas is DIRECTLY proportional to pressure and to
      molar mass, and INVERSELY proportional to temperature."

   Shortcut at STP:      M  =  d(in g/L)  x  22.4
```

# TOPIC 8 — DALTON'S LAW OF PARTIAL PRESSURES

## 8.1 Statement

> **At constant temperature, the total pressure exerted by a mixture of two or more
> non-reacting gases enclosed in a definite volume is equal to the sum of the
> partial pressures of the individual gases.**

**Partial pressure** = the pressure a gas would exert if it alone occupied the
whole container at the same temperature.

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |     P(total)  =  p1  +  p2  +  p3  +  ...                       |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+


   +-------------+   +-------------+       +-----------------+
   |  o   o   o  |   |  x    x     |       | o x  o   x  o   |
   |    o    o   | + |    x    x   |  ->   |   x o  x   o    |
   |  o    o     |   |  x     x    |       | o   x  o  x   x |
   +-------------+   +-------------+       +-----------------+
      GAS A only        GAS B only          MIXTURE, same V, same T
      p(A) = 2 atm      p(B) = 3 atm        P(total) = 5 atm
```

## 8.2 Partial pressure in terms of mole fraction

```
   For gas 1 in a mixture at total pressure P:

        p1 V  =  n1 R T          and         P V  =  n(total) R T

   Divide one by the other:

        p1          n1
       ----  =  -----------   =   x1     (the MOLE FRACTION of gas 1)
         P        n(total)

  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |        p(i)   =   x(i)   x   P(total)                           |
  |                                                                 |
  |        partial     mole        total                            |
  |        pressure    fraction    pressure                         |
  |                                                                 |
  |        and         x1 + x2 + x3 + ...  =  1                     |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 8.3 Aqueous tension

When a gas is collected **over water** (by the downward displacement of water), the
gas comes out mixed with water vapour. The pressure of that water vapour is called
the **aqueous tension**.

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |   AQUEOUS TENSION  =  the partial pressure of water vapour      |
  |                       in a gas collected over water.            |
  |                       It equals the VAPOUR PRESSURE of water    |
  |                       at that temperature.                      |
  |                                                                 |
  |   P(dry gas)  =  P(total, measured)  -  aqueous tension         |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

        |        |
        |  gas   |  <-- what you measure is  gas + water vapour
        |________|
        | ~~~~~~ |
        | water  |
        +--------+

   Aqueous tension INCREASES with temperature (more evaporation).
   At 25 degC it is 23.8 mm Hg ; at 100 degC it is 760 mm Hg
   (which is exactly why water boils at 100 degC at 1 atm).
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 8.1

**Question:** A 10 L vessel contains 4 g of H2 and 16 g of O2 at 27 degC.
Find the partial pressure of each gas and the total pressure.

**ANSWER:**
```
  STEP 1  moles
           n(H2)  =  4 / 2    =  2.0 mol
           n(O2)  =  16 / 32  =  0.5 mol
           n(total)           =  2.5 mol

  STEP 2  total pressure  (T = 300 K, V = 10 L, R = 0.0821)

              n R T        2.5  x  0.0821  x  300         61.575
      P  =  ---------  =  -------------------------  =  ----------  = 6.16 atm
                V                   10                      10

           2.5 x 0.0821 = 0.20525 ;  0.20525 x 300 = 61.575

  STEP 3  mole fractions
           x(H2)  =  2.0 / 2.5  =  0.8
           x(O2)  =  0.5 / 2.5  =  0.2         (check: 0.8 + 0.2 = 1  OK)

  STEP 4  partial pressures
           p(H2)  =  0.8  x  6.16  =  4.93 atm
           p(O2)  =  0.2  x  6.16  =  1.23 atm

  CHECK:   4.93 + 1.23  =  6.16 atm  =  P(total)   OK
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 8.2 (aqueous tension)

**Question:** 250 mL of oxygen is collected over water at 25 degC and a total
pressure of 750 mm Hg. The aqueous tension at 25 degC is 23.8 mm Hg. What volume
would the DRY oxygen occupy at STP?

**ANSWER:**
```

           P1 = 750  -  23.8  =  726.2 mm Hg
           V1 = 250 mL ,  T1 = 298 K

  STEP 2  bring it to STP  (P2 = 760 mm Hg , T2 = 273 K)

           P1 V1        P2 V2
          -------  =   -------
            T1           T2

                   P1 V1 T2          726.2  x  250  x  273
          V2  =  ------------  =  ---------------------------
                   T1 P2               298   x   760

           Numerator :  726.2 x 250   = 181550
                        181550 x 273  = 49 563 150
           Denominator: 298 x 760     = 226 480

                    49 563 150
          V2  =   -------------- =  218.9  mL
                     226 480

  V2  ~  219 mL of dry oxygen at STP.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 8.3

**Question:** A mixture contains 2 mol of N2, 3 mol of O2 and 5 mol of He at a
total pressure of 5 atm. Calculate the partial pressure of each gas.

**ANSWER:**
```
  n(total)  =  2 + 3 + 5  =  10 mol

  x(N2) = 2/10 = 0.2      ->   p(N2) = 0.2 x 5  =  1.0 atm

```

# TOPIC 9 — KINETIC MOLECULAR THEORY OF GASES

This is the model that **explains** why the gas laws are true. It is a standard
8-mark question. Learn the postulates as a numbered list.

## 9.1 The postulates

```
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                         |
  |  1. A gas consists of a very large number of tiny particles             |
  |     (atoms or molecules).                                               |
  |                                                                         |
  |  2. The ACTUAL VOLUME of the molecules is NEGLIGIBLE compared with      |
  |     the volume of the container. (Molecules are treated as points.)     |
  |                                                                         |
  |  3. There are NO forces of attraction or repulsion between the          |
  |     molecules.                                                          |
  |                                                                         |
  |  4. The molecules are in CONSTANT, RAPID, RANDOM motion in all          |
  |     directions.                                                         |
  |                                                                         |
  |  5. Collisions between molecules, and with the walls, are PERFECTLY     |
  |     ELASTIC - no kinetic energy is lost.                                |
  |                                                                         |
  |  6. PRESSURE is caused by the molecules bombarding the walls of the     |
  |     container.                                                          |
  |                                                                         |
  |  7. The AVERAGE KINETIC ENERGY of the molecules is DIRECTLY             |
  |     PROPORTIONAL to the ABSOLUTE TEMPERATURE.                           |
  |                                                                         |
  |  8. At a given temperature, different molecules have different          |
  |     speeds, but the AVERAGE kinetic energy is the same for all gases.   |
  |                                                                         |
  |  9. Gravity has no effect on the motion of the molecules.               |
  |                                                                         |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Memory hook:** *"Tiny, empty, free, moving, bouncing, banging, hotter-is-faster."*

```
                  A GAS ACCORDING TO KMT

     +-------------------------------------------+
     |    o->                    <-o             |
     |               ^                      o    |
     |          o    |         o->          |    |
     |               o                      v    |
     |   <-o                    ^                |
     |            o->           |     o->        |
     |                          o                |
     +-------------------------------------------+
       Tiny points, huge empty space between them,
       flying in every direction, bouncing off the
       walls (that bouncing IS the pressure).
```

## 9.2 How the postulates explain the gas laws

| Gas law | The KMT explanation (write this exactly) |
|---|---|
| **Boyle's law** | Reduce the volume at constant T. The molecules have less room, so they hit the walls **more often per second**. More collisions per unit area = higher pressure. Hence `P ~ 1/V`. |
| **Charles's law** | Raise T at constant P. The average KE and hence the speed of the molecules rises, so they hit the wall harder and more often. To keep P the same, the volume must expand. Hence `V ~ T`. |
| **Gay-Lussac's law** | Raise T at constant V. Molecules move faster and strike the walls harder and more often, but the wall area is fixed, so the pressure must rise. Hence `P ~ T`. |
| **Avogadro's law** | Equal volumes at the same P and T must have equal numbers of molecules, because P depends on the number of molecules hitting the wall and their average KE (which is fixed by T alone). |
| **Dalton's law** | Since molecules do not attract one another (postulate 3), each gas bangs on the wall completely independently. The pressures therefore simply add. |
| **Graham's law** | At the same T, all gases have the same average KE, so `(1/2) m u^2` is the same for all. A heavier molecule must therefore move slower, so it diffuses slower. |

# TOPIC 10 — MOLECULAR SPEEDS

At any instant molecules in a gas have a huge spread of speeds. So we quote
**three** kinds of average.

## 10.1 The three speeds

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |  MOST PROBABLE SPEED   u(mp)  - the speed possessed by the LARGEST  |
  |                                 number of molecules (the PEAK of    |
  |                                 the distribution curve)             |
  |                                                                     |
  |                          +--------------                            |
  |                         /   2 R T                                   |
  |              u(mp)  =  /   ---------                                |
  |                      \/        M                                    |
  |                                                                     |
  |                                                                     |
  |  AVERAGE SPEED         u(av)  - the ordinary arithmetic mean of     |
  |                                 all the individual speeds           |
  |                                                                     |
  |                          +--------------                            |
  |                         /   8 R T                                   |
  |              u(av)  =  /   ---------                                |
  |                      \/      pi  M                                  |
  |                                                                     |
  |                                                                     |
  |  ROOT MEAN SQUARE      u(rms) - the square root of the mean of the  |
  |   SPEED                         SQUARES of the speeds. This is the  |
  |                                 one linked to kinetic energy.       |
  |                                                                     |
  |                          +--------------                            |
  |                         /   3 R T             +-------------        |
  |             u(rms)  =  /   ---------   =     / 3 P V   or  3 P / d  |
  |                      \/        M           \/     M                 |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+

   T  in KELVIN
   M  in kg/mol  if you use R = 8.314 J/K/mol   (the answer is then m/s)
       e.g. O2 -> M = 0.032 kg/mol , NOT 32
```

> **TRAP:** With `R = 8.314`, the molar mass must be in **kilograms** per mole.
> Using 32 instead of 0.032 makes your answer come out about 31 times too small.

## 10.2 The ratio (learn this number)

```
   u(mp) : u(av) : u(rms)   =   sqrt(2)  :  sqrt(8/pi)  :  sqrt(3)

                            =   1.414  :  1.596  :  1.732

   Divide everything by 1.414:

  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |     u(mp)  :  u(av)  :  u(rms)   =   1  :  1.128  :  1.224     |
  |                                                                |
  |     So always:      u(mp)   <   u(av)   <   u(rms)             |
  |                                                                |
  |     Handy conversions:                                         |
  |         u(av)   =  0.9213  x  u(rms)                           |
  |         u(mp)   =  0.8165  x  u(rms)                           |
  |         u(av)   =  1.128   x  u(mp)                            |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 10.3 The Maxwell distribution of molecular speeds

```
      FRACTION OF MOLECULES
      with a given speed
        |
        |          ***
        |        *     *
        |       *        *
        |      *           *
        |     *              *
        |    *                  *
        |   *                       *
        |  *                             * *
        | *                                    * * *
        |*                                            * * * *
        +----+----+----+-----------------------------------------> SPEED
             |    |    |
             |    |    +--  u(rms)   (largest)
             |    +-------  u(av)
             +------------  u(mp)  = the PEAK

   FEATURES you must state:
     - the curve starts at the origin (no molecule has zero speed... almost none)
     - it rises to a MAXIMUM at u(mp)
     - it falls off gradually with a LONG TAIL to the right
     - it is NOT symmetrical (it is skewed to the right)
     - the AREA under the curve = total number of molecules = constant
     - order on the axis:  u(mp)  <  u(av)  <  u(rms)
```

### Effect of TEMPERATURE (same gas)

```
        |
        |      ****
        |     *    *                    T1  (LOW temperature)
        |    *      *                       tall, narrow peak
        |   *        *
        |  *          *  * * *
        | *        *        *  *  *        T2  (HIGH temperature)
        |*      *                *  *  *   short, broad peak,
        |    *                        * * *  shifted RIGHT
        +--------------------------------------> SPEED
             ^        ^
             |        |
          u(mp) at T1 |
                   u(mp) at T2

   ON HEATING:
     - the peak SHIFTS to the RIGHT (molecules move faster)
     - the peak becomes LOWER and BROADER (bigger spread of speeds)
     - the AREA under both curves is the SAME (same number of molecules)
     - the fraction of molecules with very HIGH speed increases sharply
       (this is why reaction rates jump with temperature)
```

### Effect of MOLAR MASS (same temperature)

```
        |
        |          ****
        |         *    *                 HEAVY gas (large M),
        |        *      *                e.g. O2 - slow, narrow
        |       *        *
        |      *          *
        |  * * *           * *
        | *                    * * *     LIGHT gas (small M),
        |*                          * *  e.g. H2 - fast, broad
        +-----------------------------------> SPEED
            ^              ^
            |              |
       O2 peak         H2 peak (much further right)

   At the SAME temperature, a LIGHTER gas has a HIGHER most probable
   speed and a BROADER, FLATTER curve.
   (Both have the SAME average kinetic energy - the light one is
    just faster because it is lighter.)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 10.1

**Question:** Calculate the root mean square speed of oxygen molecules at 27 degC.
(R = 8.314 J K^-1 mol^-1, M of O2 = 32 g/mol)

**ANSWER:**
```
  STEP 1  Units.   T = 27 + 273  = 300 K
                   M = 32 g/mol  = 32 / 1000  =  0.032 kg/mol

  STEP 2                  +-------------
                         /   3 R T
             u(rms)  =  /  -----------
                      \/        M

                         +--------------------------
                        /   3  x  8.314  x  300
                    =  /   -------------------------
                     \/            0.032

  STEP 3  numerator:   3 x 8.314   =  24.942
                       24.942 x 300 = 7482.6

  STEP 4  divide:      7482.6 / 0.032   =  233 831.25

  STEP 5  square root: sqrt(233831.25)   =  483.6

             u(rms)  =  483.6  m/s     (about 1740 km/h!)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 10.2

**Question:** For the same oxygen at 27 degC, find the average speed and the most
probable speed.

**ANSWER:**

# TOPIC 11 — THE KINETIC GAS EQUATION AND KINETIC ENERGY

## 11.1 The kinetic gas equation

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |                    1                                            |
  |          P V  =   ---   m  N  c^2                               |
  |                    3                                            |
  |                                                                 |
  |    P  =  pressure of the gas                                    |
  |    V  =  volume of the gas                                      |
  |    m  =  mass of ONE molecule                                   |
  |    N  =  total number of molecules                              |
  |    c^2 = MEAN SQUARE speed  (so c = u(rms))                     |
  |                                                                 |
  |    Note:  m x N  =  total mass of the gas                       |
  |           For 1 mole:  m x NA  =  M  (the molar mass)           |
  |                                                                 |
  |                    1                                            |
  |    So for 1 mole:  P V  =  ---  M  c^2                          |
  |                    3                                            |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Sketch of where it comes from** (you rarely need the full derivation for AP Inter,
but you should be able to describe it):

```
  1. Take a cube of side l containing N molecules.
  2. A molecule of mass m moving with velocity component ux along x
     hits a wall and bounces back:
          change of momentum per collision  =  m ux - (- m ux) = 2 m ux
  3. Time between two hits on the SAME wall = 2 l / ux
          so   force from one molecule  =  2 m ux / (2 l / ux)
                                        =  m ux^2 / l
  4. Add over all N molecules and all three directions x, y, z.
     Because the motion is RANDOM,  ux^2 = uy^2 = uz^2 = c^2 / 3
  5. Pressure = total force / area = force / (6 l^2)... collecting terms:

                 1   m N c^2                                1
          P  =  --- ----------      i.e.        P V  =     --- m N c^2
                 3      V                                   3
```

## 11.2 Kinetic energy and temperature

```
   Total kinetic energy of N molecules  =  N  x  (1/2) m c^2

                                        =  (1/2) m N c^2

   From the kinetic gas equation:   m N c^2  =  3 P V

                                            3
   So      Kinetic energy   KE   =   (1/2) x  ---  P V  ... rearranged:
                                            1
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |                       3                                         |
  |           K E   =    ---   P V                                  |
  |                       2                                         |
  |                                                                 |
  |   And for 1 mole, P V = R T, so                                 |
  |                                                                 |
  |                       3                                         |
  |           K E   =    ---   R T       per MOLE                   |
  |                       2                                         |
  |                                                                 |
  |                       3                                         |
  |           K E   =    ---   k T       per MOLECULE               |
  |                       2                                         |
  |                                                                 |
  |   where  k  =  R / NA  =  1.38 x 10^-23  J/K                    |
  |                (the BOLTZMANN constant)                         |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

  THE BIG CONCLUSION (this sentence earns marks):

     "The average kinetic energy of the molecules of a gas depends ONLY on
      the absolute temperature and is INDEPENDENT of the nature of the gas."

     So at the same temperature H2, O2 and CO2 all have the SAME average
     kinetic energy - but very DIFFERENT speeds.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 11.1

**Question:** Calculate the total kinetic energy of 2 moles of an ideal gas at
27 degC. (R = 8.314 J/K/mol)

**ANSWER:**
```
                 3
      KE   =   ---  n R T
                 2

           =  1.5  x  2  x  8.314  x  300

      1.5 x 2          =  3
      3 x 8.314        =  24.942
      24.942 x 300     =  7482.6

      KE  =  7482.6  J   =   7.48  kJ
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 11.2

**Question:** Calculate the average kinetic energy of ONE molecule of nitrogen at
27 degC. (k = 1.38 x 10^-23 J/K)

**ANSWER:**
```
                 3
      KE   =   ---  k T   =  1.5  x  1.38 x 10^-23  x  300
                 2

      1.5 x 1.38  =  2.07     ->   2.07 x 10^-23
      2.07 x 10^-23  x  300   =   621 x 10^-23   =  6.21 x 10^-21  J

      KE per molecule  =  6.21 x 10^-21  J

  NOTE: the answer would be EXACTLY THE SAME for O2, He or CO2 at 27 degC.
        Average KE depends only on T.
```

# TOPIC 12 — GRAHAM'S LAW OF DIFFUSION

## 12.1 Two words to keep apart

```
   DIFFUSION  -  the spontaneous mixing of two gases by their own motion
                 (a perfume spreading across a room)

   EFFUSION   -  the escape of a gas through a TINY HOLE into a vacuum
                 (a balloon slowly going flat)
```

## 12.2 Statement

> **At constant temperature and pressure, the rate of diffusion (or effusion) of a
> gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its density (or molar mass).**

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |                 1                        1                      |
  |     r    ~   --------      or    r  ~  --------                 |
  |               sqrt(d)                   sqrt(M)                 |
  |                                                                 |
  |     For two gases:                                              |
  |                                                                 |
  |         r1          +---------            +---------            |
  |        ----   =    /   M2       =        /   d2                 |
  |         r2       \/    M1              \/    d1                 |
  |                                                                 |
  |     Since  M = 2 x vapour density,  M2/M1 = VD2/VD1 too.        |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

   If the SAME volume takes different TIMES,   rate  =  volume / time,
   so                r1 / r2  =  t2 / t1

        r1        V / t1        t2
       ----  =  ----------  =  ----
        r2        V / t2        t1
```

**Uses:** separating isotopes (U-235 from U-238 as UF6 in the atom-bomb project),
and detecting marsh gas in coal mines.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 12.1

**Question:** Compare the rates of diffusion of hydrogen and oxygen.

**ANSWER:**
```
   M(H2) = 2 ,   M(O2) = 32

    r(H2)          +----------          +--------
   -------  =     /   M(O2)      =     /   32       =   sqrt(16)   =   4
    r(O2)       \/    M(H2)          \/    2

   Hydrogen diffuses 4 times FASTER than oxygen.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 12.2

**Question:** A gas diffuses 1.5 times as fast as SO2 under the same conditions.
Calculate its molar mass. (M of SO2 = 64)

**ANSWER:**
```
    r(X)             +-------------
   --------  = 1.5 = /   M(SO2)
    r(SO2)         \/    M(X)

   Square both sides:

                     64
      (1.5)^2  =  --------           ->     2.25  M(X)  =  64
                    M(X)

                  64
      M(X)  =  --------  =  28.44   ~   28  g/mol
                 2.25

   The gas is nitrogen (N2) or carbon monoxide (CO).
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 12.3

**Question:** 50 mL of gas A effuses through a pinhole in 146 s. The same volume
of CO2 takes 115 s. Find the molar mass of A. (M of CO2 = 44)

**ANSWER:**
```
    r(A)         t(CO2)         115
   -------  =  ----------  =  -------  =  0.7877
    r(CO2)       t(A)           146

   And        r(A)          +-------------
             -------   =   /  M(CO2) / M(A)
              r(CO2)     \/

   Square both sides:

                          44
      (0.7877)^2   =   --------
                         M(A)

      0.6205  =  44 / M(A)

                  44
      M(A)  =  ----------   =   70.9   ~   71  g/mol
                0.6205

   (Close to Cl2, M = 71.)
```

# TOPIC 13 — BEHAVIOUR OF REAL GASES

## 13.1 Real gases do NOT obey PV = nRT

An ideal gas would obey `PV = nRT` at all T and P. Real gases obey it only at
**low pressure and high temperature**. Everywhere else there are deviations.

**Two of the KMT postulates are simply false for a real gas:**

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                    |
  |  FALSE POSTULATE 1 :  "the volume of the molecules is negligible"  |
  |     At high pressure the molecules are squeezed close together     |
  |     and their own volume is NO LONGER negligible compared          |
  |     with the container.                                            |
  |                                                                    |
  |  FALSE POSTULATE 2 :  "there are no attractive forces"             |
  |     Real molecules DO attract each other. If they did not,         |
  |     gases could never be liquefied - but they can.                 |
  |                                                                    |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 13.2 The compressibility factor Z

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |                  P V                    P V(m)                  |
  |         Z   =  ---------      =      ------------               |
  |                  n R T                   R T                    |
  |                                                                 |
  |    V(m) = molar volume = V / n                                  |
  |                                                                 |
  |    Z is DIMENSIONLESS (no units).                               |
  |                                                                 |
  |    Z can also be read as                                        |
  |                                                                 |
  |             V(real, observed)                                   |
  |      Z  =  ---------------------                                |
  |             V(ideal, calculated)                                |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+

  +---------+---------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Z = 1  |  the gas is behaving IDEALLY                            |
  +---------+---------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Z < 1  |  the gas is MORE compressible than ideal.               |
  |         |  ATTRACTIVE forces dominate. (called NEGATIVE deviation)|
  |         |  Happens at LOW-to-moderate pressures.                  |
  +---------+---------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Z > 1  |  the gas is LESS compressible than ideal.               |
  |         |  REPULSION / molecular volume dominates.                |
  |         |  (positive deviation). Happens at HIGH pressures.       |
  +---------+---------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 13.3 The Z-versus-P plots (draw these)

```
   PLOT 1:   Z  vs  P   for different gases at 0 degC

    Z |                                                    H2
      |                                              * * *
    2 |                                        * * *
      |                                  * * *                 N2
      |                            * * *              . . . .
      |                      * * *          . . . . .
    1 |------*-*-*-*-*-*-*----------------------------------------
      |  *          . .    . .                            CH4
      |    .  .  .        .    .              _ . _ . _
      |      _ _                 . _ . _ . _
      |         _  _                                       CO2
      |            _  _  _  _  _  _  _  _
    0 +---------------------------------------------------> P (atm)
      0        200      400       600       800     1000

   READ IT LIKE THIS:
     - H2 and He are ABOVE the line at ALL pressures (Z > 1 always).
       They are so small and weakly attracting that only the size
       effect shows.
     - N2, CH4, CO2 first DIP BELOW 1 (attraction wins), reach a
       minimum, then rise ABOVE 1 (molecular volume wins).
     - The dip is DEEPEST for the most easily liquefied gas (CO2),
       because its attractive forces are the strongest.
     - At LOW pressure all of them approach Z = 1  ->  IDEAL.


   PLOT 2:   Z  vs  P  for ONE gas at different temperatures

    Z |                                                   T4 (highest)
      |                                          * * * *
      |                                   * * *
      |                             * *              T3 = BOYLE TEMP
    1 |----*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*------------------------------
      |  *      . . .   . .   . .    . . .
      |   .  .        .            .            T2
      |     _ _     _              _  _  _ _
      |        _ _ _                            T1 (lowest)
      |
    0 +---------------------------------------------------> P
      0

    As the temperature RISES, the dip becomes SHALLOWER.
    At one particular temperature - the BOYLE TEMPERATURE - the gas
    behaves ideally over a WIDE range of pressure (Z stays 1 for a
    long stretch).


   PLOT 3:   P V  vs  P  (the classic "deviation" plot)

    PV |
       |                                            H2, He
       |                                    * * * *
       |                              * * *
       |----*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-*-------------------- IDEAL GAS
       |  *  .                                      (horizontal line)
       |     .  .                    . . .
       |        .  .          . . .              N2
       |           . . .  . .
       |              _  _  _
       |                     _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _     CO2
       +----------------------------------------------> P
```

## 13.4 Why gases deviate — the reasons in words

```
  1. AT HIGH PRESSURE
       The molecules are pushed very close together. Their OWN volume
       becomes a significant part of the total volume, so the free space
       is LESS than V. The measured PV is LARGER than expected -> Z > 1.

  2. AT LOW / MODERATE PRESSURE
       Molecules are close enough to attract each other. A molecule about
       to hit the wall is pulled back by its neighbours, so it hits the
       wall LESS hard. The observed pressure is LOWER than ideal -> Z < 1.

  3. AT LOW TEMPERATURE
       Thermal energy is small, so the attractive forces have a bigger
       relative effect. Deviation is LARGE.

  4. AT HIGH TEMPERATURE AND LOW PRESSURE
       Molecules are far apart (attraction negligible) and moving fast
       (attraction cannot hold them). The gas behaves almost IDEALLY.
```

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |   A REAL GAS BEHAVES MOST LIKE AN IDEAL GAS AT                  |
  |            LOW PRESSURE  and  HIGH TEMPERATURE                  |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Which gases deviate most?** The ones that are easiest to liquefy — i.e. those
with the strongest intermolecular forces and the largest size.

```
   LEAST deviation  ->  H2 , He , Ne         (tiny, weak forces)
   MOST  deviation  ->  CO2 , SO2 , NH3      (large, strong forces,
                                              easily liquefied)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 13.1

**Question:** One mole of a gas occupies 0.1846 L at 100 atm and 273 K. Calculate
its compressibility factor and say what it means.

**ANSWER:**
```
                P V(m)          100  x  0.1846
      Z   =   ----------  =  ---------------------
                 R T           0.0821  x  273

   Numerator :   100 x 0.1846   =   18.46
   Denominator:  0.0821 x 273   =   22.41

                18.46
      Z   =   ---------   =   0.824
                22.41

   Z = 0.82  which is LESS than 1
   ->  the gas is MORE compressible than an ideal gas
   ->  ATTRACTIVE forces are dominating (negative deviation).
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 13.2

**Question:** For a gas Z = 1.5 at 300 K and 50 atm. What is its molar volume?
Compare with the ideal molar volume.

**ANSWER:**
```
                P V(m)                          Z R T
      Z   =   ----------      ->     V(m)  =  ----------
                 R T                              P

                1.5  x  0.0821  x  300
      V(m) = --------------------------
                        50

           1.5 x 0.0821 = 0.12315
           0.12315 x 300 = 36.945
           36.945 / 50   = 0.7389

      V(m, real)  =  0.739 L

      IDEAL:      V(m) = R T / P = (0.0821 x 300) / 50 = 24.63/50 = 0.4926 L

      The real gas takes up MORE room than the ideal calculation
      predicts (Z > 1), because the molecules' own volume matters.
```

# TOPIC 14 — THE VAN DER WAALS EQUATION

Johannes van der Waals **repaired** `PV = nRT` by putting the two false postulates
right — one correction for volume, one for pressure.

## 14.1 The two corrections

```
  CORRECTION 1 : VOLUME
  ---------------------
     The molecules themselves take up space, so the free space
     available is LESS than the container volume.

               V(free)  =  V  -  n b

     b = the EXCLUDED VOLUME or "co-volume" per mole
         (roughly four times the actual volume of one mole of molecules)


  CORRECTION 2 : PRESSURE
  -----------------------
     A molecule about to strike the wall is pulled BACK by its
     neighbours, so it hits less hard. The observed pressure is
     therefore LOWER than the ideal pressure.

                                a n^2
               P(ideal)  =  P + -------
                                 V^2

     a = a measure of the strength of the intermolecular ATTRACTION
```

```
        THE WALL-PULL PICTURE

        inside the gas              near the wall
                                                  |
         o     o                          o       | W
          \   /                            \      | A
       o -- o -- o                       o -- o   | L    -> about to hit
          /   \                            /   \  | L
         o     o                          o     o |
                                             ^
      pulled EQUALLY in all              pulled BACKWARDS only
      directions -> no net force         -> hits the wall SOFTER
                                         -> measured P is LOW
```

## 14.2 The equation

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                      |
  |          +-             a n^2  -+                                    |
  |          |   P    +   --------  |   (  V  -  n b  )   =   n R T      |
  |          +-              V^2   -+                                    |
  |                                                                      |
  |                    THE VAN DER WAALS EQUATION                        |
  |                                                                      |
  |                                                                      |
  |    For ONE mole (n = 1):                                             |
  |                                                                      |
  |          +-           a   -+                                         |
  |          |   P   +  ------ |   (  V  -  b  )   =   R T               |
  |          +-          V^2  -+                                         |
  |                                                                      |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 14.3 The constants a and b

```
  +---------+---------------------------+---------------------------------+
  |         |          a                |             b                   |
  +---------+---------------------------+---------------------------------+
  | Meaning | measures the strength of  | measures the effective SIZE     |
  |         | the INTERMOLECULAR        | (volume) of the molecules;      |
  |         | ATTRACTION                | the excluded / co-volume        |
  +---------+---------------------------+---------------------------------+
  | Units   | atm L^2 mol^-2            | L mol^-1                        |
  |         | (or Pa m^6 mol^-2)        | (or m^3 mol^-1)                 |
  +---------+---------------------------+---------------------------------+
  | Large   | strong attraction         | large molecules                 |
  | value   | -> gas EASILY LIQUEFIED   | -> hard to compress             |
  |  means  | -> high critical temp     |                                 |
  +---------+---------------------------+---------------------------------+

  TYPICAL VALUES  (a in atm L^2 mol^-2 , b in L mol^-1)

     He     a = 0.034    b = 0.0237       weakest attraction
     H2     a = 0.244    b = 0.0266
     N2     a = 1.39     b = 0.0391
     O2     a = 1.36     b = 0.0318
     CO2    a = 3.59     b = 0.0427
     NH3    a = 4.17     b = 0.0371       strongest attraction here
     SO2    a = 6.71     b = 0.0564

  READ IT: NH3 and SO2 have large 'a', so they are easily liquefied.
           He has a tiny 'a', so it is the hardest gas to liquefy.
```

> **TRAP:** A 2-mark question often just asks for the **units of a and b**.
> `a  ->  atm L^2 mol^-2` and `b  ->  L mol^-1`. Memorise them.

## 14.4 Where the equation reduces to the ideal gas law

```
   AT LOW PRESSURE (V is very large):
        a/V^2  is negligible   and   b  is negligible next to V
        ->  P V  =  R T          (ideal)

   AT HIGH TEMPERATURE:
        molecules move too fast for attraction to matter
        ->  behaves ideally

   Therefore an ideal gas is one for which  a = 0  and  b = 0 .
```

## 14.5 Boyle temperature

```
   BOYLE TEMPERATURE  T(B) - the temperature at which a real gas obeys
   the ideal gas law over an appreciable range of pressure
   (the attractive and volume effects exactly cancel).

                        a
           T(B)  =  ---------
                       R b

   Above T(B) : Z > 1 at all pressures (gas is harder to compress).
   Below T(B) : Z dips below 1 first, then rises.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 14.1

**Question:** Calculate the pressure of 2 moles of CO2 confined in 5 L at 300 K
using (i) the ideal gas equation and (ii) the van der Waals equation.
Take `a = 3.59 atm L^2 mol^-2` and `b = 0.0427 L mol^-1`.

**ANSWER:**
```
  (i)  IDEAL GAS

               n R T        2  x  0.0821  x  300         49.26
        P  =  --------  =  -----------------------  =  ---------  =  9.85 atm
                 V                   5                     5


  (ii) VAN DER WAALS

               n R T           a n^2
        P  =  ---------   -   -------
               V - n b          V^2

     TERM 1 : the volume-corrected part
        n b        =  2  x  0.0427   =  0.0854 L
        V - n b    =  5  -  0.0854   =  4.9146 L

        n R T      =  49.26            (from part (i))

           49.26
        ----------  =  10.02  atm
          4.9146

     TERM 2 : the attraction correction
        n^2        =  2^2  =  4
        a n^2      =  3.59  x  4  =  14.36
        V^2        =  5^2  =  25

           14.36
        ----------   =   0.574  atm
            25

     PUT TOGETHER
        P  =  10.02  -  0.574   =   9.45  atm


  COMPARE:   ideal  =  9.85 atm
             real   =  9.45 atm     (LOWER, because attraction pulls
                                     the molecules back from the wall)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 14.2

**Question:** Arrange He, CO2 and NH3 in order of increasing ease of liquefaction,
using their van der Waals `a` values (0.034, 3.59 and 4.17).

**ANSWER:**
```
   Bigger 'a'  =  stronger attraction  =  easier to liquefy.

        He (0.034)   <   CO2 (3.59)   <   NH3 (4.17)

   So the order of INCREASING ease of liquefaction is

        He   <   CO2   <   NH3

   Helium is the hardest gas in the universe to liquefy (b.p. 4 K).
```

# TOPIC 15 — LIQUEFACTION OF GASES AND CRITICAL CONSTANTS

## 15.1 How a gas is liquefied

```
   To liquefy a gas you must make the ATTRACTIVE forces win:

       1. COOL it        -> reduces thermal energy (the kicking)
       2. COMPRESS it    -> brings molecules close so attraction acts

   BUT: no amount of pressure will liquefy a gas that is above its
        CRITICAL TEMPERATURE. You MUST cool it first.
```

## 15.2 The critical constants

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |  CRITICAL TEMPERATURE  T(c)                                         |
  |     The temperature ABOVE which a gas CANNOT be liquefied,          |
  |     however much pressure is applied.                               |
  |                                                                     |
  |  CRITICAL PRESSURE  P(c)                                            |
  |     The minimum pressure needed to liquefy a gas AT its             |
  |     critical temperature.                                           |
  |                                                                     |
  |  CRITICAL VOLUME  V(c)                                              |
  |     The volume occupied by ONE MOLE of the gas at the critical      |
  |     temperature and critical pressure.                              |
  |                                                                     |
  |  Together T(c), P(c), V(c) are the CRITICAL CONSTANTS.              |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+


  RELATION TO THE VAN DER WAALS CONSTANTS

  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |                    8 a                     a                        |
  |       T(c)  =   ----------      P(c)  =  --------      V(c) = 3 b   |
  |                   27 R b                  27 b^2                    |
  |                                                                     |
  |       And the "critical compressibility factor":                    |
  |                                                                     |
  |            P(c) V(c)         3                                      |
  |           -----------   =   ---   =   0.375   for EVERY gas        |
  |            R  T(c)           8                                      |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

```
  SOME CRITICAL TEMPERATURES (learn the trend, not every number)

     He      5.3  K   (-268 degC)      hardest to liquefy
     H2     33.2  K
     N2    126.0  K
     O2    154.3  K
     CO2   304.1  K   (31.1 degC)      easy: just squeeze on a warm day
     NH3   405.5  K   (132.4 degC)     easiest of these

  A gas with a HIGH critical temperature has STRONG intermolecular
  forces and is EASY to liquefy.

  ROOM TEMPERATURE (~300 K) is ABOVE the T(c) of N2 and O2 - which is
  why the air around you never turns into a liquid, no matter what
  the pressure.
```

```
  TWO WORDS THE EXAMINER LIKES:

     VAPOUR  -  a gaseous substance BELOW its critical temperature
                (it CAN be liquefied by pressure alone)
     GAS     -  a gaseous substance ABOVE its critical temperature
                (pressure alone will NOT liquefy it)
```

## 15.3 Andrews' isotherms of carbon dioxide

Thomas Andrews compressed CO2 at a series of fixed temperatures and plotted P
against V. This is the single most important diagram in the topic.

```
     P
     ^
  P  |                                                 T = 50 degC
     |  \                                              (above Tc, no
     |   \                                              liquefaction -
     |    \                                             smooth curve like
     |     \                                            a Boyle hyperbola)
     |      \  \
     |       \   \                                     T = 40 degC
     |        \    \  \
     |         \     \   \
     |          \      \    \                          T = 31.1 degC = Tc
     |           \       \     \                       (CRITICAL ISOTHERM -
 Pc  |............\.........\.....* C                   has a POINT OF
     |             \    ______\____\__                  INFLEXION at C)
     |              \  /       \     \  \
     |               \/         \      \   \           T = 21.5 degC
     |          _____/\_________ \       \    \        (FLAT PORTION:
     |         /      | \        \\        \     \      gas and liquid
     |        /       |  \________\\_________\___ \     coexist)
     |       /        |            \\            \  \
     |      /         |             \\             \   \  T = 13.1 degC
     |     /          |              \\ __________  \    \
     |    /           |               \/           \_ \    \
     |   /            |                                \     \
     |  LIQUID        |     LIQUID + VAPOUR              VAPOUR / GAS
     |  only          |     (two phases together)
     +----------------+------------------------------------------> V
                     Vc

    HOW TO READ THIS DIAGRAM (say this in the exam):

    1. At a LOW temperature (13.1 degC), start on the right with a gas
       and compress it. P rises along the curve.
    2. Suddenly the curve becomes FLAT (horizontal). Here the gas starts
       CONDENSING. Volume falls a lot while the pressure stays CONSTANT.
       Liquid and vapour EXIST TOGETHER along this flat part.
    3. Once all the gas has become liquid, the curve rises almost
       VERTICALLY - because liquids are nearly incompressible.
    4. As the temperature is raised the FLAT PORTION gets SHORTER.
    5. At 31.1 degC (the CRITICAL TEMPERATURE of CO2) the flat portion
       shrinks to a single POINT, C - the CRITICAL POINT.
       Here liquid and vapour become indistinguishable; the meniscus
       between them DISAPPEARS.
    6. ABOVE 31.1 degC there is NO flat portion at all. No matter how
       hard you press, you get NO liquid. The curves look like ordinary
       Boyle's law hyperbolas.

    For CO2:   T(c) = 31.1 degC (304.1 K)
               P(c) = 73.0 atm
               V(c) = 95.6 cm^3 per mole
```

> **TRAP:** The flat horizontal portion is often mislabelled. Along it, **the
> pressure does not change while the volume decreases** — the gas is condensing.
> That constant pressure is the **vapour pressure of liquid CO2 at that temperature.**

# TOPIC 16 — THE LIQUID STATE: VAPOUR PRESSURE

## 16.1 What it is

In an open dish, fast molecules escape and never come back — the liquid evaporates
away. In a **closed** vessel they come back, and eventually the rate of evaporation
equals the rate of condensation. That is a dynamic **equilibrium**.

```
    +-----------------------+
    |    ^    ^    ^        |     evaporation (molecules leaving)
    |    |    |    |        |           |
    |    v    v    v        |     condensation (molecules returning)
    | ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ |
    |      L I Q U I D      |
    +-----------------------+

      rate of evaporation  =  rate of condensation
      -> EQUILIBRIUM
      -> the pressure of the vapour above the liquid is now CONSTANT
      -> that constant pressure is the VAPOUR PRESSURE
```

> **VAPOUR PRESSURE** — *the pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid when it is
> in equilibrium with its own liquid at a given temperature.*

## 16.2 Factors affecting vapour pressure

```
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
  |  FACTOR                   |  EFFECT                                  |
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
  |  TEMPERATURE              |  vapour pressure INCREASES with          |
  |                           |  temperature (more molecules have        |
  |                           |  enough energy to escape).               |
  |                           |  The rise is EXPONENTIAL, not linear.    |
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
  |  NATURE OF THE LIQUID     |  WEAK intermolecular forces  ->  HIGH    |
  |  (intermolecular forces)  |  vapour pressure (VOLATILE liquid).      |
  |                           |  Ether > acetone > ethanol > water       |
  |                           |  (water is low because of H-bonding)     |
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
  |  SURFACE AREA             |  NO EFFECT. Vapour pressure is an        |
  |                           |  intensive property.                     |
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
  |  VOLUME OF LIQUID         |  NO EFFECT.                              |
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
  |  ADDING A NON-VOLATILE    |  vapour pressure DECREASES               |
  |  SOLUTE                   |  (Raoult's law - next year's chapter)    |
  +---------------------------+------------------------------------------+
```

```
   VAPOUR PRESSURE  vs  TEMPERATURE

    VP |                                                    /
  (mm  |                                                   /
   Hg) |                                                  / ether
   760 |- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -/- - -/- -
       |                                          /     /
       |                                         /    /
       |                                       /    /  ethanol
       |                                     /   /
       |                                  /   /
       |                              /    /
       |                          /     /       water
       |                    /      /
       |            /        /
       |  ____/______/
       +---------------------------------------------------> T (degC)
       0                                    78    100

   Where each curve CUTS the 760 mm Hg line is that liquid's
   NORMAL BOILING POINT.
```

# TOPIC 17 — BOILING POINT

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |  BOILING POINT - the temperature at which the VAPOUR PRESSURE of    |
  |  a liquid becomes EQUAL to the EXTERNAL (atmospheric) PRESSURE.     |
  |                                                                     |
  |  NORMAL BOILING POINT - the boiling point when the external         |
  |  pressure is exactly 1 atm (760 mm Hg).                             |
  |    Water: 100 degC                                                  |
  |                                                                     |
  |  STANDARD BOILING POINT - when the external pressure is 1 bar.      |
  |    Water: 99.6 degC                                                 |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 17.1 The effect of external pressure

```
   EXTERNAL PRESSURE  UP    ->   BOILING POINT  UP
   EXTERNAL PRESSURE  DOWN  ->   BOILING POINT  DOWN

   PRESSURE COOKER
   ---------------
      The lid traps steam, so the pressure inside rises to about 2 atm.
      The vapour pressure of water must now reach 2 atm before it boils,
      which happens at about 120 degC.
      Food cooks much faster at 120 degC than at 100 degC.

   ON A HIGH MOUNTAIN
   ------------------
      Atmospheric pressure is LOW (say 0.5 atm at 5500 m).
      Water's vapour pressure reaches 0.5 atm at only about 82 degC,
      so water BOILS at 82 degC.
      The food does not cook properly, because 82 degC is not hot enough -
      not because it "does not boil".

   TRAP: boiling water on a mountain is COOLER, not hotter.
```

> **TRAP:** Boiling is *not* the same as evaporation. **Evaporation** happens at
> **any** temperature and **only from the surface**. **Boiling** happens at **one
> fixed temperature** (for a given pressure) and **throughout the bulk** of the liquid.

# TOPIC 18 — SURFACE TENSION

## 18.1 Why it exists

```
        AIR
   ------------------------------------------------
        o <--- a molecule AT THE SURFACE
       /|\        pulled sideways and DOWNWARDS only.
      / | \       NO molecules above it to pull it up.
     /  |  \      -> NET INWARD FORCE
    o   o   o
   / \ / \ / \
  o - o - o - o   <--- a molecule INSIDE the liquid
   \ / \ / \ /         pulled EQUALLY in every direction
    o   o   o          -> NET FORCE = ZERO
        LIQUID
```

Because surface molecules are pulled inwards, the liquid tries to make its
**surface area as small as possible**. The surface behaves like a stretched
elastic skin.

## 18.2 Definition and units

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |  SURFACE TENSION (gamma) - the FORCE acting per unit LENGTH at      |
  |  right angles to an imaginary line drawn on the surface of a        |
  |  liquid.                                                            |
  |                                                                     |
  |  Equivalently: the WORK (energy) required to increase the surface   |
  |  area of a liquid by ONE unit  -> called SURFACE ENERGY.            |
  |                                                                     |
  |     SI units      :  N m^-1     (= J m^-2)                          |
  |     CGS units     :  dyne cm^-1 (= erg cm^-2)                       |
  |     1 N m^-1  =  1000 dyne cm^-1                                    |
  |                                                                     |
  |     Dimensions    :  M T^-2                                         |
  |                                                                     |
  |  Surface tension of WATER at 20 degC  =  72.8 dyne/cm               |
  |                                       =  0.0728 N/m                 |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 18.3 Why drops are spherical

```
   FOR A GIVEN VOLUME, THE SPHERE HAS THE SMALLEST SURFACE AREA
   OF ANY SHAPE.

   Surface tension always tries to MINIMISE the surface area,
   because a smaller surface means LOWER surface energy, and
   every system wants to be at its lowest energy.

   Therefore a free liquid drop pulls itself into a SPHERE.

               forces all point inwards
                        |
                    \   |   /
                     \  v  /
                  --> ( o ) <--
                     /  ^  \
                    /   |   \
                        |
                    a SPHERE
```

## 18.4 Effect of temperature and other factors

```
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  FACTOR                 |  EFFECT ON SURFACE TENSION                |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  TEMPERATURE increases  |  surface tension DECREASES.               |
  |                         |  Reason: higher thermal energy weakens    |
  |                         |  the intermolecular attraction.           |
  |                         |  At the CRITICAL TEMPERATURE it becomes   |
  |                         |  ZERO (no surface left).                  |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  Stronger intermolecular|  surface tension INCREASES.               |
  |  forces                 |  (water > ethanol > ether)                |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  Adding soap/detergent  |  surface tension DECREASES sharply.       |
  |  (a surfactant)         |  That is how detergents wet the cloth.    |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  Adding a salt like NaCl|  surface tension slightly INCREASES.      |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
```

**Everyday consequences (good 2-mark answers):**

```
   - Mercury does not wet glass and forms round beads.
   - A needle or a water-strider insect can rest on water.
   - Rain drops and soap bubbles are spherical.
   - Hot water cleans better than cold (lower surface tension -> wets more).
   - Capillary rise of water in a narrow tube.
```

# TOPIC 19 — VISCOSITY

## 19.1 What it is

**Viscosity** is a liquid's **resistance to flow**. Honey is viscous; petrol is not.

```
   A liquid flows in layers ("laminar flow").
   Each layer slides over the one below and is DRAGGED BACK by it.

   SPEED
     ^
     |    ============================>  fastest (top layer)
     |    ========================>
     |    ====================>            velocity gradient
     |    ================>                   du / dz
     |    ============>
     |    ========>
     |    ====>
     |    =>                             slowest (bottom, at rest)
     +---------------------------------------------->
     FIXED SURFACE
```

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                     |
  |  The force of friction F between two layers of area A with a        |
  |  velocity gradient du/dz is                                         |
  |                                                                     |
  |                              du                                     |
  |            F   =   eta  A  ------                                   |
  |                              dz                                     |
  |                                                                     |
  |  eta  =  COEFFICIENT OF VISCOSITY                                   |
  |                                                                     |
  |  DEFINITION: the force per unit area needed to maintain a unit      |
  |  difference of velocity between two parallel layers of liquid       |
  |  that are a unit distance apart.                                    |
  |                                                                     |
  |     SI unit    :  N s m^-2   =   Pa s   (pascal second)             |
  |                   also written kg m^-1 s^-1                         |
  |     CGS unit   :  POISE  =  g cm^-1 s^-1                            |
  |                                                                     |
  |     1 poise  =  0.1  Pa s  =  10^-1  kg m^-1 s^-1                   |
  |     1 Pa s   =  10 poise                                            |
  |                                                                     |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 19.2 Factors affecting viscosity

```
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  FACTOR                 |  EFFECT ON VISCOSITY                      |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  TEMPERATURE increases  |  viscosity DECREASES (strongly).          |
  |                         |  Molecules gain energy, slip past each    |
  |                         |  other more easily.                       |
  |                         |  Hot oil pours easily; cold honey crawls. |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  Stronger intermolecular|  viscosity INCREASES.                     |
  |  forces / H-bonding     |  Glycerol has 3 -OH groups -> very        |
  |                         |  viscous. Water is more viscous than      |
  |                         |  ether.                                   |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  Larger molecular size /|  viscosity INCREASES (long chains tangle) |
  |  longer chains          |  Heavy engine oil > petrol.               |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |  PRESSURE increases     |  viscosity increases slightly.            |
  +-------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
```

> **TRAP:** Surface tension and viscosity **both DECREASE when you heat a liquid**.
> Vapour pressure **INCREASES** when you heat a liquid. Students routinely get this
> backwards. Say it out loud three times.

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |   HEATING A LIQUID:                                             |
  |        VAPOUR PRESSURE      goes  UP                            |
  |        SURFACE TENSION      goes  DOWN                          |
  |        VISCOSITY            goes  DOWN                          |
  |        BOILING POINT        (unchanged - it is a property)      |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```
