## Topic: Intermolecular forces

### Q A1
What are London (dispersion) forces? In which type of molecules do they exist?

**ANSWER:**
```
  LONDON / DISPERSION FORCES - the weak attractive forces that arise
  between molecules due to the formation of INSTANTANEOUS (temporary)
  dipoles caused by the momentary uneven distribution of electrons,
  which then INDUCE dipoles in neighbouring molecules.

  They exist in ALL molecules - polar and non-polar - and are the ONLY
  intermolecular force present in NON-POLAR molecules and in the noble
  gases.       Example:  He...He , Cl2...Cl2 , CH4...CH4

  Strength increases with the SIZE (number of electrons) of the molecule.
```

---

### Q A2
Why is the boiling point of H2O much higher than that of H2S, even though
sulphur is heavier than oxygen?

**ANSWER:**
```
  In H2O, hydrogen is bonded to OXYGEN, which is small and highly
  electronegative. So water molecules are joined by strong HYDROGEN BONDS.

  In H2S, sulphur is much less electronegative and much bigger, so H2S
  has NO hydrogen bonding - only weak dipole-dipole and dispersion forces.

  More energy is needed to break the hydrogen bonds in water.

        b.p. of H2O = 100 degC        b.p. of H2S = -60 degC
```

---

### Q A3
What is the difference between intermolecular and intramolecular forces?

**ANSWER:**
```
  INTRAmolecular force  -  the force holding the ATOMS together INSIDE
                           one molecule. It is a CHEMICAL BOND
                           (covalent / ionic). Very STRONG.
                           Example: the O-H bond in H2O.

  INTERmolecular force  -  the force of attraction BETWEEN two separate
                           molecules. Much WEAKER.
                           Example: the hydrogen bond between two H2O
                           molecules.

  Boiling a liquid breaks INTERmolecular forces only - the molecule
  itself survives.
```

### Q A5
State Charles's law. What is absolute zero?

**ANSWER:**
```
  CHARLES'S LAW: At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of a
  gas is DIRECTLY proportional to its absolute (kelvin) temperature.

              V             V1        V2
      V ~ T ; --- = k ;    ----  =   ----
              T             T1        T2

  ABSOLUTE ZERO: -273.15 degC (= 0 K), the theoretical temperature at
  which the volume of an ideal gas would become ZERO. It is the lowest
  possible temperature and the zero of the kelvin scale.
```

### Q A6
State Gay-Lussac's law. What is an isochore?

**ANSWER:**
```
  GAY-LUSSAC'S LAW: At constant VOLUME, the pressure of a fixed mass of
  a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.

               P             P1        P2
      P ~ T ; --- = k ;     ----  =   ----
               T             T1        T2

  ISOCHORE - the straight-line graph of P against T drawn at CONSTANT
  VOLUME. It passes through the origin when T is in kelvin.
```

  AVOGADRO'S LAW: Equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions
  of temperature and pressure contain an EQUAL NUMBER OF MOLECULES.
  (Equivalently, at constant T and P,  V ~ n .)

  (At 0 degC and 1 bar, the IUPAC value is 22.7 L per mole.)
```

### Q A9
Define an ideal gas. Why does no real gas behave ideally?

**ANSWER:**
```
  IDEAL GAS - a gas that obeys the equation P V = n R T under ALL
  conditions of temperature and pressure.

  No real gas is ideal because:
     1. the molecules of a real gas DO occupy a definite volume, and
     2. real molecules DO attract each other.

  Both of these are assumed to be zero in the ideal model. Real gases
  approach ideal behaviour only at LOW PRESSURE and HIGH TEMPERATURE.
```

## Topic: Dalton's law

### Q A15
State Dalton's law of partial pressures.

**ANSWER:**
```
  At constant temperature, the TOTAL pressure exerted by a mixture of
  two or more NON-REACTING gases enclosed in a definite volume is equal
  to the SUM of the partial pressures of the individual gases.

      P(total)  =  p1  +  p2  +  p3  +  ...

  PARTIAL PRESSURE - the pressure a gas would exert if it alone occupied
  the whole container at the same temperature.
```

## Topic: Kinetic theory and molecular speeds

### Q A18
Write any four postulates of the kinetic molecular theory of gases.

**ANSWER:**
```
  1. A gas consists of a very large number of tiny particles, and the
     ACTUAL VOLUME of these particles is negligible compared with the
     volume of the container.
  2. There are NO forces of attraction or repulsion between the molecules.
  3. The molecules are in constant, rapid, RANDOM motion and their
     collisions are PERFECTLY ELASTIC.
  4. The AVERAGE KINETIC ENERGY of the molecules is directly proportional
     to the ABSOLUTE TEMPERATURE.
```

### Q A19
Define root mean square speed, average speed and most probable speed.
Write the ratio between them.

**ANSWER:**
```
  u(rms) - the square root of the mean of the SQUARES of the speeds of
           all the molecules.        u(rms) = sqrt( 3 R T / M )

  u(av)  - the ordinary arithmetic mean of all the molecular speeds.
                                      u(av)  = sqrt( 8 R T / pi M )

  u(mp)  - the speed possessed by the LARGEST number of molecules
           (the peak of the Maxwell curve).
                                      u(mp)  = sqrt( 2 R T / M )

  RATIO:    u(mp)  :  u(av)  :  u(rms)   =   1  :  1.128  :  1.224
```

## Topic: Real gases

### Q A23
Define the compressibility factor Z. What does Z < 1 mean?

**ANSWER:**
```
                P V          P V(m)
      Z   =   --------  =  ----------          (dimensionless)
                n R T          R T

  For an IDEAL gas  Z = 1  at all temperatures and pressures.

  Z < 1  ->  the gas is MORE compressible than an ideal gas;
             the ATTRACTIVE forces between molecules are dominating
             (negative deviation).

  Z > 1  ->  the gas is LESS compressible than ideal; the molecular
             volume / repulsion dominates (positive deviation).
```

## Topic: The liquid state

### Q A27
Define vapour pressure. How does it change with temperature?

**ANSWER:**
```
  VAPOUR PRESSURE - the pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid when
  it is in EQUILIBRIUM with its own liquid at a given temperature
  (rate of evaporation = rate of condensation).

  It INCREASES with rise in temperature, because more molecules acquire
  enough energy to escape from the surface.

  It does NOT depend on the surface area or the amount of liquid taken.
```

---

### Q A28
Define boiling point. What happens to it when the external pressure is lowered?

**ANSWER:**
```
  BOILING POINT - the temperature at which the VAPOUR PRESSURE of a
  liquid becomes EQUAL to the external (atmospheric) pressure.

  NORMAL boiling point = boiling point at 1 atm (water: 100 degC).

  If the external pressure is LOWERED, the vapour pressure needs to
  reach only that lower value, so the liquid BOILS AT A LOWER
  TEMPERATURE. That is why water boils below 100 degC on a hill and
  why food does not cook properly there.
```

---

### Q A29
Define surface tension and give its units. Why are liquid drops spherical?

**ANSWER:**
```
  SURFACE TENSION (gamma) - the force acting per unit LENGTH at right
  angles to an imaginary line drawn on the surface of a liquid.
  It also equals the work needed to increase the surface area by one unit.

      SI unit  :  N m^-1  (= J m^-2)
      CGS unit :  dyne cm^-1

  WHY DROPS ARE SPHERICAL: surface tension makes a liquid minimise its
  surface area, and for a given VOLUME a SPHERE has the SMALLEST
  surface area (and therefore the lowest surface energy).
```

Define the coefficient of viscosity. Give its SI and CGS units.
How does viscosity change with temperature?

**ANSWER:**
```
  COEFFICIENT OF VISCOSITY (eta) - the force per unit area required to
  maintain a unit difference of velocity between two parallel layers of
  a liquid that are a unit distance apart.

                          du
      F  =  eta  x  A  x -----
                          dz

      SI unit  :  N s m^-2  =  Pa s  =  kg m^-1 s^-1

```

### Q B1
Explain the different types of intermolecular forces with one example of each.

**ANSWER:**
```
  1. DISPERSION (LONDON) FORCES
     Momentary uneven electron distribution creates an INSTANTANEOUS
     dipole, which induces a dipole in the neighbour. Present in ALL
     molecules; the only force in non-polar molecules.
     Strength increases with molecular size / number of electrons.
     Example:  He...He , Cl2...Cl2 , CH4...CH4

  2. DIPOLE-DIPOLE FORCES
     Act between two POLAR molecules; the d+ end of one is attracted to
     the d- end of the other. Stronger than dispersion for molecules
     of comparable size.
     Example:  HCl...HCl , SO2...SO2

  3. DIPOLE-INDUCED DIPOLE FORCES
     A polar molecule distorts the electron cloud of a nearby NON-POLAR
     molecule and induces a dipole in it; the two then attract.
     Depends on the dipole moment of the polar molecule and the
     polarisability of the non-polar one.
     Example:  HCl...Ar ;  O2 dissolved in H2O

  4. HYDROGEN BONDING
     A special, strong dipole-dipole attraction when H is bonded to a
     small, highly electronegative atom - F, O or N - and points at a
     lone pair on another such atom.
     Example:  H2O...H2O , HF...HF , NH3...NH3

  ORDER:  dispersion < dipole-induced dipole < dipole-dipole < H-bond
```

### Q B2
Explain how the balance between intermolecular forces and thermal energy
decides the state of matter. Give the characteristics of the three states.

**ANSWER:**
```
  THERMAL ENERGY is the energy of motion of the particles; it is
  proportional to the absolute temperature and tries to SCATTER them.
  INTERMOLECULAR FORCES try to HOLD them together.
  The state of matter is decided by which one wins.

      forces  >>  thermal energy    ->    SOLID
      forces  ~=  thermal energy    ->    LIQUID
      forces  <<  thermal energy    ->    GAS

  +--------------+-------------+--------------+---------------+
  |  PROPERTY    |   SOLID     |   LIQUID     |     GAS       |
  +--------------+-------------+--------------+---------------+
  | Shape        | definite    | of container | of container  |
  | Volume       | definite    | definite     | not definite  |
  | Forces       | very strong | moderate     | negligible    |
  | Distance     | very small  | small        | very large    |
  | Motion       | vibration   | sliding      | free random   |
  | Density      | very high   | high         | very low      |
  | Compress-    | negligible  | very small   | very large    |
  |  ibility     |             |              |               |
  | Diffusion    | negligible  | slow         | very fast     |
  +--------------+-------------+--------------+---------------+
```

### Q B3
State Boyle's law. Draw the graphs of (a) P vs V and (b) P vs 1/V, and explain them.

**ANSWER:**
```

  (b)  P  vs  1/V  ->  STRAIGHT LINE THROUGH THE ORIGIN

       |               *

       |      *
       |   *
       +-------------------------> 1/V
      0

     Because P = (constant) x (1/V), which is the equation of a straight
     line y = mx passing through the origin.

  Also, since density d = m/V and m is fixed,  d ~ P  at constant T.
```

### Q B4
State Charles's law. Explain how the graph of V against temperature leads to
the idea of absolute zero and the kelvin scale.

**ANSWER:**
```
  CHARLES'S LAW: At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of a
  gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.
        V ~ T   ;   V/T = constant   ;   V1/T1 = V2/T2
        V(t) = V0 ( 1 + t / 273.15 )

  GRAPH OF V vs t (degrees Celsius) - a set of ISOBARS

     V |                                      *
       |                                 *  *
       |                            * *  *
       |                       * *  *
       |                  * *  *              (each line = one pressure)
       |             * *  *
       |        * *  *
       | . . . .
       +---*----------+----------+-----------> t (degC)
       -273.15        0         100

  Every isobar, when EXTRAPOLATED backwards (dotted part), meets the
  temperature axis at the SAME point,  -273.15 degC.

  At that temperature the volume of an ideal gas would become ZERO.
  A volume cannot be negative, so no lower temperature is possible.
  This temperature is called ABSOLUTE ZERO and is taken as the zero of
  the KELVIN (absolute) scale:

        T (K)  =  t (degC)  +  273.15

  On the kelvin scale the graph of V against T is a straight line
  passing THROUGH THE ORIGIN.

  NOTE: absolute zero can never actually be reached - every real gas
  liquefies and then solidifies long before it.
```

### Q B5
Derive the ideal gas equation PV = nRT from the gas laws.

**ANSWER:**
```
  STEP 1  Boyle's law    (T and n constant)      V  ~  1 / P

  STEP 2  Charles's law  (P and n constant)      V  ~  T

  STEP 3  Avogadro's law (P and T constant)      V  ~  n

  STEP 4  If a quantity is proportional to several others separately,
          it is proportional to their product when all vary together:

                            n T
                  V   ~   -------
                             P

  STEP 5  Remove the proportionality by inserting a constant R:

                          n R T
                  V   =  -------            i.e.     P V  =  n R T
                            P

  R is the UNIVERSAL GAS CONSTANT (the same for every gas).

  VALUE OF R : for 1 mole at STP,  P = 1 atm, V = 22.4 L, T = 273.15 K

              P V         1  x  22.4
      R  =  --------  =  -------------  =  0.0821  L atm K^-1 mol^-1
              n T         1 x 273.15

      In SI units, R = 8.314 J K^-1 mol^-1.

  This equation is also called the EQUATION OF STATE of an ideal gas,
  because it links all four state variables P, V, n and T.
```

### Q B8
State Dalton's law of partial pressures and derive the relation
p(i) = x(i) x P(total).

**ANSWER:**
```
  STATEMENT: At constant temperature, the total pressure exerted by a
  mixture of non-reacting gases in a definite volume is equal to the
  sum of their partial pressures.

        P(total)  =  p1  +  p2  +  p3  +  ...

  DERIVATION
  Let the mixture occupy volume V at temperature T. Each gas behaves
  independently, so for gas 1:

        p1 V  =  n1 R T                     ... (i)

  For the whole mixture:

        P V   =  n(total) R T               ... (ii)

  Divide (i) by (ii):

        p1            n1
       ------  =  -----------   =   x1     (the MOLE FRACTION of gas 1)
          P         n(total)

        So          p1  =  x1  x  P(total)

  In general      p(i)  =  x(i)  x  P(total)     and   sum of x(i) = 1

### Q B11
Write the postulates of the kinetic molecular theory of gases and show how it
explains Boyle's law and Charles's law.

**ANSWER:**
```
  POSTULATES
   1. A gas consists of a very large number of tiny particles.
   2. The actual VOLUME of the molecules is NEGLIGIBLE compared with
      the volume of the container.
   3. There are NO forces of attraction or repulsion between molecules.
   4. The molecules are in constant, rapid, RANDOM motion.
   5. Collisions between molecules and with the walls are PERFECTLY
      ELASTIC - no kinetic energy is lost.
   6. Pressure arises from the BOMBARDMENT of the walls by the molecules.
   7. The AVERAGE KINETIC ENERGY is directly proportional to the
      ABSOLUTE TEMPERATURE.
   8. At a given temperature all gases have the same average kinetic
      energy, although the individual speeds differ.

  EXPLANATION OF BOYLE'S LAW
      Reduce the volume at constant T. The molecules now travel a
      shorter distance between wall collisions, so they strike the walls
      MORE OFTEN per second per unit area. More collisions = higher
      pressure. Hence  P ~ 1/V.

  EXPLANATION OF CHARLES'S LAW
      Raise the temperature at constant P. The average kinetic energy
      and hence the molecular speed increases, so the molecules hit the
      walls harder and more often. To keep the pressure UNCHANGED the
      gas must expand so that the collisions per unit area come back
      down. Hence  V ~ T.
```

### Q B14
Write the van der Waals equation. Explain the significance and units of the
constants a and b.

**ANSWER:**
```
      +-             a n^2  -+
      |   P    +   --------- |   (  V  -  n b  )   =   n R T
      +-              V^2   -+

  WHY THE TWO CORRECTIONS ARE NEEDED

  VOLUME CORRECTION (V - nb)
      The molecules themselves occupy space, so the free volume
      available for motion is LESS than the container volume V.
      b is the EXCLUDED (co-) volume per mole, roughly four times the
      actual volume of one mole of molecules.

  PRESSURE CORRECTION (P + an^2/V^2)
      A molecule about to strike the wall is pulled BACK by the
      attraction of its neighbours, so it hits the wall less hard.
      The measured pressure is therefore LOWER than the ideal pressure,
      and the term an^2/V^2 must be ADDED back.

  |       |  SIGNIFICANCE                  |  UNITS               |
  +-------+--------------------------------+----------------------+
  |   a   |  measure of the strength of    |  atm L^2 mol^-2      |
  |       |  intermolecular ATTRACTION.    |  (Pa m^6 mol^-2)     |
  |       |  Large a -> gas easily         |                      |
  |       |  liquefied, high T(c).         |                      |
  +-------+--------------------------------+----------------------+
  |   b   |  measure of the effective SIZE |  L mol^-1            |
  |       |  (volume) of the molecules.    |  (m^3 mol^-1)        |
  |       |  Large b -> hard to compress.  |                      |
  +-------+--------------------------------+----------------------+

  For an ideal gas  a = 0  and  b = 0 , and the equation reduces to

### Q B15
Explain the liquefaction of gases and define the critical constants.

**ANSWER:**
```
  A gas is liquefied by (i) COOLING it, which lowers the thermal energy,

  thermal motion, the gas condenses.

### Q B16
Explain vapour pressure and boiling point. How does external pressure affect
the boiling point?

**ANSWER:**
```
  VAPOUR PRESSURE
    In a closed vessel a liquid evaporates until the rate of evaporation
    equals the rate of condensation. The pressure exerted by the vapour
    at this EQUILIBRIUM is the vapour pressure.
    It INCREASES with temperature, and is HIGHER for liquids with WEAK
    intermolecular forces (volatile liquids such as ether).
    It does NOT depend on the surface area or the quantity of liquid.

  BOILING POINT
    The temperature at which the vapour pressure of the liquid becomes
    EQUAL to the external pressure. At this point bubbles of vapour can
    form throughout the bulk of the liquid, not just at the surface.
    NORMAL boiling point   -> at 1 atm  (water = 100 degC)
    STANDARD boiling point -> at 1 bar  (water = 99.6 degC)

  EFFECT OF EXTERNAL PRESSURE
    external pressure INCREASED -> the vapour pressure must rise higher
        before boiling starts -> BOILING POINT RISES.
        PRESSURE COOKER: pressure inside ~2 atm, water boils near
        120 degC, so food cooks much faster.

    external pressure DECREASED -> BOILING POINT FALLS.
        ON A HILL at 0.5 atm water boils near 82 degC, so food is not
        cooked properly - the water is simply not hot enough.

  DIFFERENCE FROM EVAPORATION
    Evaporation occurs at ANY temperature and ONLY at the surface.
    Boiling occurs at ONE temperature and THROUGHOUT the liquid.
```

### Q B17
Explain surface tension and viscosity. Give their units and the effect of
temperature on each.

**ANSWER:**
```
  SURFACE TENSION (gamma)
    A molecule inside a liquid is pulled EQUALLY in all directions, so
    the net force on it is zero. A molecule AT THE SURFACE has no
    molecules above it, so it experiences a NET INWARD PULL. The liquid
    therefore tries to make its surface area as SMALL as possible and
    the surface behaves like a stretched skin.

    DEFINITION: the force per unit length acting at right angles to an
    imaginary line on the surface of a liquid (= the work required to
    increase the surface area by one unit).

    UNITS:  N m^-1 (SI)  =  J m^-2  ;  dyne cm^-1 (CGS)
    EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE: surface tension DECREASES as temperature
    rises, because the intermolecular attraction weakens; it becomes
    ZERO at the critical temperature.

    CONSEQUENCES: spherical drops (a sphere has the least surface area
    for a given volume), insects walking on water, capillary rise,
    mercury forming beads on glass.

  VISCOSITY (eta)
    The resistance offered by a liquid to its own flow, caused by the
    internal friction between the layers sliding over one another.

                          du
        F  =  eta  x  A  x ----
                          dz

    COEFFICIENT OF VISCOSITY: the force per unit area needed to keep a
    unit velocity difference between two parallel layers unit distance
    apart.

    UNITS:  N s m^-2 = Pa s = kg m^-1 s^-1 (SI) ; POISE = g cm^-1 s^-1
            1 poise = 0.1 Pa s
    EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE: viscosity DECREASES sharply as temperature
    rises, because the molecules gain energy and slip past each other
    more easily. Hot oil pours easily, cold honey crawls.

    Viscosity INCREASES with stronger hydrogen bonding (glycerol is very
    viscous) and with longer molecular chains.

  ON HEATING:  vapour pressure UP , surface tension DOWN , viscosity DOWN.
```

     |             \/       \       \   \             T = 21.5 degC
     |        _____/\_______ \        \    \          (FLAT portion:
     |       /     | \       \\         \     \        liquid + vapour
     |      /      |  \_______\\__________\____ \      together)
     |     /       |           \\              \  \
     |    /        |            \\               \  \  T = 13.1 degC
     |   /         |             \\ ___________    \   \
     |  /          |              \/            \__ \    \
     | LIQUID      |    LIQUID + VAPOUR             VAPOUR / GAS
     | only        |
     +-------------+---------------------------------------------> V
                  Vc

  HOW TO READ IT
   1. At 13.1 degC, start on the RIGHT with gaseous CO2 and compress it.
      The pressure rises smoothly (like Boyle's law).
   2. At a certain point the curve becomes FLAT (horizontal). Here the
      gas begins to CONDENSE: the volume falls a great deal while the
      pressure stays CONSTANT. Liquid and vapour coexist along this
      flat line, and the constant pressure is the VAPOUR PRESSURE of
      liquid CO2 at that temperature.
   3. When all the gas has liquefied the curve rises almost VERTICALLY,
      because a liquid is nearly incompressible.
   4. As the temperature is raised the flat portion becomes SHORTER.
   5. At 31.1 degC the flat portion shrinks to a single POINT C -
      the CRITICAL POINT. Here liquid and vapour become identical in
      density and the MENISCUS between them DISAPPEARS.
   6. ABOVE 31.1 degC there is NO flat portion at all - no liquid forms
      however great the pressure. These curves are smooth hyperbolas.


  PART 2 - CRITICAL CONSTANTS                                 [3 marks]
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   CRITICAL TEMPERATURE  T(c) - the temperature above which a gas cannot
        be liquefied, however great the pressure applied.
   CRITICAL PRESSURE     P(c) - the minimum pressure required to liquefy
        the gas AT its critical temperature.
   CRITICAL VOLUME       V(c) - the volume of ONE MOLE of the gas at
        T(c) and P(c).

   For CO2:   T(c) = 31.1 degC (304.1 K) ,  P(c) = 73 atm ,
              V(c) = 95.6 cm^3 per mole

   RELATION TO THE VAN DER WAALS CONSTANTS

              8 a                  a
    T(c)  = ---------    P(c) = --------     V(c)  =  3 b
             27 R b              27 b^2

              P(c) V(c)          3
   and       ------------  =   -----  =  0.375   for every gas
              R  T(c)            8

   Inverse forms:      a  =  3 P(c) V(c)^2       b  =  V(c) / 3

   A gas with a large 'a' (strong attraction) has a HIGH critical
   temperature and is EASY to liquefy.

        He  5.3 K  <  H2 33.2 K  <  N2 126 K  <  O2 154 K
            <  CO2 304 K  <  NH3 405 K

   Because room temperature (~300 K) is ABOVE the critical temperature
   of N2 and O2, air can never be liquefied by pressure alone - it must
   first be cooled.


  PART 3 - VAPOUR versus GAS                                  [1 mark]
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   VAPOUR - a gaseous substance BELOW its critical temperature; it CAN
            be liquefied by applying pressure alone.
   GAS    - a gaseous substance ABOVE its critical temperature; pressure
            alone will NOT liquefy it. It must be cooled first.

### Q C5
Discuss the intermolecular forces present in matter and explain how they,
together with thermal energy, determine the physical state. Then describe the
properties of the liquid state — vapour pressure, boiling point, surface
tension and viscosity.

**MODEL ANSWER (mark split shown):**

```
  PART 1 - THE FOUR INTERMOLECULAR FORCES                     [3 marks]
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   1. DISPERSION (LONDON) FORCES
      Caused by instantaneous dipoles arising from the momentary uneven
      distribution of electrons, which induce dipoles in neighbours.
      Present in ALL molecules; the only force in non-polar molecules.
      Strength INCREASES with molecular size, number of electrons,
      polarisability and elongated shape. Varies as 1/r^6.
      Example: F2 < Cl2 < Br2 < I2 (boiling points rise down the group)

   2. DIPOLE-DIPOLE FORCES
      Between two POLAR molecules; the d+ end of one attracts the d- end
      of the other. Example: HCl, SO2, CH3Cl.

   3. DIPOLE-INDUCED DIPOLE FORCES
      A permanent dipole induces a dipole in a nearby non-polar molecule.
      Depends on the dipole moment of the first and the polarisability
      of the second. Example: HCl and Ar; O2 dissolving in water.

   4. HYDROGEN BONDING
      Strongest of the four (10-40 kJ/mol). Needs H bonded to a small,
      highly electronegative atom - F, O or N - and a lone pair on
      another such atom. Example: H2O, HF, NH3, alcohols, carboxylic
      acids. Explains the high boiling point of water, the lower density
      of ice, and water's high surface tension and viscosity.

   ORDER: dispersion < dipole-induced dipole < dipole-dipole < H-bond


  PART 2 - THERMAL ENERGY AND THE STATE OF MATTER             [2 marks]
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   THERMAL ENERGY is the energy of motion of the particles and is
   proportional to the ABSOLUTE TEMPERATURE. It tries to scatter the
   particles; the intermolecular forces try to hold them together.

        forces  >>  thermal energy   ->   SOLID
        forces  ~=  thermal energy   ->   LIQUID
        forces  <<  thermal energy   ->   GAS

   Heating supplies thermal energy: solid -> liquid -> gas.
   Cooling and compressing let the forces win: gas -> liquid -> solid.

  +--------------+-------------+--------------+---------------+
  |  PROPERTY    |   SOLID     |   LIQUID     |     GAS       |
  +--------------+-------------+--------------+---------------+
  | Shape        | definite    | of container | of container  |
  | Volume       | definite    | definite     | not definite  |
  | Forces       | very strong | moderate     | negligible    |
  | Motion       | vibration   | sliding      | free, random  |
  | Density      | very high   | high         | very low      |
  | Compressib.  | negligible  | very small   | very large    |
  +--------------+-------------+--------------+---------------+


  PART 3 - PROPERTIES OF THE LIQUID STATE                     [3 marks]
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
   VAPOUR PRESSURE
     The pressure of the vapour in equilibrium with its own liquid at a
     given temperature. INCREASES with temperature; higher for liquids
     with weak intermolecular forces; independent of surface area and
     of the amount of liquid.

   BOILING POINT
     The temperature at which the vapour pressure equals the external
     pressure. Normal b.p. is measured at 1 atm (water 100 degC).
     Higher external pressure -> higher boiling point (pressure cooker).
     Lower external pressure -> lower boiling point (hill station).

   SURFACE TENSION (gamma)
     Surface molecules experience a NET INWARD pull because there are no
     molecules above them, so the liquid minimises its surface area and
     the surface behaves like a stretched skin.
     Definition: force per unit length acting at right angles to a line
     on the surface (= work to increase area by one unit).
     Units: N m^-1 (SI) or dyne cm^-1 (CGS).
     DECREASES with rise in temperature (zero at T(c)).
     Explains spherical drops (a sphere has the least surface area for a
     given volume), capillary rise, and insects resting on water.

   VISCOSITY (eta)
     Resistance of a liquid to flow, caused by friction between layers.
        F = eta A (du/dz)
     Coefficient of viscosity = force per unit area needed to maintain
     unit velocity difference between layers unit distance apart.
     Units: N s m^-2 = Pa s (SI) ; poise = g cm^-1 s^-1 (CGS);
     1 poise = 0.1 Pa s.
     DECREASES with rise in temperature; INCREASES with hydrogen
     bonding and with longer molecular chains (glycerol, heavy oils).

   SUMMARY:  on heating a liquid -> vapour pressure UP,
             surface tension DOWN, viscosity DOWN.
```
