# PATTERN 1 — Configuration, position and occurrence

### Q1
The general electronic configuration of group 13 elements is

(a) ns2 np1 &nbsp; (b) ns2 np2 &nbsp; (c) ns1 np2 &nbsp; (d) ns2 np3

**ANSWER: (a)** — `ns2 np1`. Group 14 is ns2 np2, group 15 is ns2 np3.

---

### Q2
The most abundant metal in the earth's crust is

(a) iron &nbsp; (b) aluminium &nbsp; (c) calcium &nbsp; (d) sodium

**ANSWER: (b)** — Aluminium, **8.3 % by mass**. It is the **third most
abundant element** overall, after oxygen (46 %) and silicon (27.7 %).

> **SHORTCUT:** Remember the top four of the crust: **O, Si, Al, Fe**.
> Aluminium is the first METAL on that list.

---

### Q3
Which of the following is the only non-metal in group 13?

(a) B &nbsp; (b) Al &nbsp; (c) Ga &nbsp; (d) Tl

**ANSWER: (a)** — Boron. Al, Ga, In and Tl are all metals. Group 13 has **no
metalloids** in the Inter syllabus (unlike group 14, which has Si and Ge).

---

### Q4
The chief ore of aluminium is

(a) cryolite &nbsp; (b) corundum &nbsp; (c) bauxite &nbsp; (d) feldspar

**ANSWER: (c)** — Bauxite, `Al2O3.2H2O`. Cryolite is `Na3AlF6`, corundum is
`Al2O3` and feldspar is `KAlSi3O8` — all contain aluminium, but bauxite is the
ore actually used.

---

### Q5
Which of the following is an ore of boron?

(a) galena &nbsp; (b) colemanite &nbsp; (c) cassiterite &nbsp; (d) malachite

**ANSWER: (b)** — Colemanite, `Ca2B6O11.5H2O`. The others are boron ores too:
borax `Na2B4O7.10H2O` and kernite `Na2B4O7.4H2O`.

---

### Q6
The isotope of boron used in nuclear reactor control rods is

(a) 9B &nbsp; (b) 10B &nbsp; (c) 11B &nbsp; (d) 12B

**ANSWER: (b)** — `10B` (19 % abundance) is an excellent **neutron absorber**.
`11B` is the more abundant isotope (81 %) but is not the useful one here.

---

### Q7
The electronic configuration of thallium is

(a) [Xe] 6s2 6p1
(b) [Xe] 4f14 5d10 6s2 6p1
(c) [Xe] 5d10 6s2 6p1
(d) [Kr] 4d10 5s2 5p1

**ANSWER: (b)** — `[Xe] 4f14 5d10 6s2 6p1`. Those 4f and 5d electrons are the
reason for **every** thallium anomaly (high IE, inert pair effect).

---

# PATTERN 2 — Atomic radius and the gallium anomaly

### Q8
The correct order of atomic radii in group 13 is

(a) B < Al < Ga < In < Tl
(b) B < Ga < Al < In < Tl
(c) B < Al < Ga < Tl < In
(d) Tl < In < Ga < Al < B

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
   B 85  <  Ga 135  <  Al 143  <  In 167  <  Tl 170   pm

   GALLIUM JUMPS IN FRONT OF ALUMINIUM.
```

> **SHORTCUT:** Whenever an option shows the plain increasing order
> B < Al < Ga < In < Tl, look for the option where **Ga and Al are swapped** —
> that is usually the correct one.

---

### Q9
The atomic radius of gallium is less than that of aluminium because of

(a) the lanthanoid contraction
(b) poor shielding by the 3d electrons
(c) the higher electronegativity of gallium
(d) the inert pair effect

**ANSWER: (b)** — Gallium has ten `3d` electrons that aluminium does not.
d orbitals are **diffuse and shield the nuclear charge poorly**, so gallium's
outer electrons feel a larger effective nuclear charge and are pulled in
(the **d-block contraction**).

> **SHORTCUT:** "Ga smaller than Al" → **3d**. "Tl anomaly" → **4f and 5d**.
> The lanthanoid contraction belongs to the In → Tl step, not Al → Ga.

---

### Q10
The increase in atomic radius from In to Tl is very small (167 → 170 pm)
because of

(a) the inert pair effect
(b) the lanthanoid contraction and poor d-shielding
(c) the small size of thallium's nucleus
(d) back bonding

**ANSWER: (b)** — Fourteen `4f` electrons plus ten `5d` electrons intervene,
and f orbitals shield even worse than d orbitals.

---

# PATTERN 3 — Ionisation enthalpy

### Q11
The correct order of first ionisation enthalpy in group 13 is

(a) B > Al > Ga > In > Tl
(b) B > Tl > Ga > Al > In
(c) B > Ga > Al > Tl > In
(d) Tl > In > Ga > Al > B

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
   B 801  >  Tl 589  >  Ga 579  >  Al 577  >  In 558   kJ/mol

   TWO tricks in one order: Ga beats Al, and Tl beats In.
```

> **SHORTCUT:** In group 13 the ionisation enthalpy order is **NOT** a smooth
> fall. Two elements break the pattern — **Ga** (3d shielding) and **Tl**
> (4f + 5d shielding). Look for the option that puts them higher.

---

### Q12
The first ionisation enthalpy of gallium is slightly higher than that of
aluminium. The reason is

(a) gallium is larger
(b) the ten 3d electrons of gallium shield the nucleus poorly
(c) gallium has a lower electronegativity
(d) gallium exists as Ga2 molecules

**ANSWER: (b)** — Poor shielding by `3d10` raises the effective nuclear charge
on gallium's outer electron, so it is held more tightly.

---

### Q13
Boron does not form B3+ ions because

(a) it has no d orbitals
(b) the sum of its first three ionisation enthalpies is very high
(c) it is a non-metal
(d) it has an inert pair

**ANSWER: (b)** — `801 + 2427 + 3659 = 6887 kJ/mol`, far too much to be repaid
by lattice or hydration energy. All boron compounds are therefore **covalent**.

---

### Q14
Which of the following has a higher first ionisation enthalpy than the group
13 element in the same period?

(a) the group 1 element
(b) the group 2 element
(c) the group 14 element only
(d) none of them

**ANSWER: (b)** — Be (899) > B (801); Mg (737) > Al (577). Group 2 has a
**completely filled ns2 sub-shell**, which is extra stable and harder to break
into.

---

# PATTERN 4 — Melting point, boiling point and physical properties

### Q15
Which element of group 13 melts in the palm of the hand?

(a) B &nbsp; (b) Al &nbsp; (c) Ga &nbsp; (d) Tl

**ANSWER: (c)** — Gallium, m.p. **303 K** (about 30 °C).

---

### Q16
The abnormally low melting point of gallium is due to

(a) its large atomic size
(b) the existence of Ga2 diatomic molecules in the solid state
(c) the inert pair effect
(d) poor shielding by 3d electrons

**ANSWER: (b)** — Solid gallium is built from **Ga2 diatomic molecules** held
together only by weak intermolecular forces, so melting is easy.

> **SHORTCUT:** Low **melting** point of Ga → **Ga2 molecules**.
> Small **size** of Ga → **3d shielding**. Two different answers — don't mix
> them up.

---

### Q17
Gallium is used in high-temperature thermometers because

(a) it is a good conductor
(b) it has the widest liquid range of any element
(c) it is non-toxic
(d) it expands uniformly

**ANSWER: (b)** — m.p. 303 K and b.p. 2676 K, a liquid range of about 2373 K.
Melting breaks only the weak forces between Ga2 units; boiling must break the
strong Ga-Ga covalent bond.

---

### Q18
The correct order of melting points in group 13 is

(a) B > Al > Ga > In > Tl
(b) B > Al > Tl > In > Ga
(c) B > Tl > In > Al > Ga
(d) Tl > In > Ga > Al > B

**ANSWER: (b)**
```
   B 2453  >  Al 933  >  Tl 576  >  In 430  >  Ga 303   K

   It FALLS from B to Ga and then RISES again for In and Tl,
   because gallium's value is anomalously low.
```

---

### Q19
Which of the following increases regularly down group 13?

(a) melting point &nbsp; (b) electronegativity
(c) density &nbsp; (d) ionisation enthalpy

**ANSWER: (c)** — Density: 2.35 → 2.70 → 5.90 → 7.31 → 11.85 g cm-3. The
atomic mass rises faster than the atomic volume. The other three are all
irregular in this group.

---

### Q20
The variation of electronegativity down group 13 is

(a) a steady decrease
(b) a steady increase
(c) a decrease from B to Al and then a slight increase
(d) constant

**ANSWER: (c)** — B 2.0 → Al 1.5 → Ga 1.6 → In 1.7 → Tl 1.8. Boron is tiny so
it pulls hardest; after Al, poor d and f shielding raises Zeff again.

---

# PATTERN 5 — Oxidation states and the inert pair effect

### Q21
The inert pair effect is due to

(a) the small size of the atom
(b) poor shielding of the nuclear charge by intervening d and f electrons
(c) the absence of d orbitals
(d) back bonding

**ANSWER: (b)** — Filled d and f subshells shield badly, so the `ns2` pair is
gripped tightly and refuses to bond.

---

### Q22
The correct order of stability of the +1 oxidation state is

(a) Al > Ga > In > Tl
(b) Al < Ga < In < Tl
(c) Tl < In < Ga < Al
(d) Ga > Al > Tl > In

**ANSWER: (b)** — `Al < Ga < In < Tl`. The `+1` state gets **more** stable
down the group; the `+3` state gets **less** stable.

---

### Q23
Which of the following is the most stable oxidation state of thallium?

(a) +1 &nbsp; (b) +2 &nbsp; (c) +3 &nbsp; (d) +4

**ANSWER: (a)** — `+1`, because of the inert pair effect. Tl(III) compounds
are therefore strong **oxidising agents**.

---

### Q24
TlI3 is best described as

(a) thallium(III) iodide
(b) thallium(I) tri-iodide, `Tl+ (I3)-`
(c) a mixture of TlI and I2
(d) a complex of Tl(II)

**ANSWER: (b)** — `Tl+(I3)-`. Tl3+ is such a strong oxidiser that it would
immediately oxidise I- to I2, so the compound settles for Tl(I).

> **SHORTCUT:** A **bottom-of-the-group high oxidation state** paired with a
> **good reducing anion** (I-, Br-) usually means the compound does not exist
> in that form. Same logic as `PbI4` in group 14.

---

### Q25
Which of the following reactions shows Tl(III) acting as an oxidising agent?

(a) `TlCl3 --(heat)--> TlCl + Cl2`
(b) `2 Tl + 3 Cl2 --> 2 TlCl3`
(c) `Tl2O + H2O --> 2 TlOH`
(d) `3 Ga+ --> 2 Ga + Ga3+`

**ANSWER: (a)** — Tl(III) drops to the more stable Tl(I), taking electrons
from the chloride, which is oxidised to Cl2.

---

### Q26
`3 Ga+ --> 2 Ga + Ga3+` is an example of

(a) oxidation &nbsp; (b) reduction
(c) disproportionation &nbsp; (d) hydrolysis

**ANSWER: (c)** — **Disproportionation**: the same species is both oxidised
(to Ga3+) and reduced (to Ga). It happens because in the middle of the group
neither +1 nor +3 is comfortable.

---

### Q27
Boron shows only the +3 oxidation state because

(a) it is a non-metal
(b) it has no intervening d or f electrons, so there is no inert pair effect
(c) it is very electronegative
(d) it has only four orbitals

**ANSWER: (b)** — Nothing shields the `2s2` pair badly, so it takes part in
bonding normally and boron always uses all three valence electrons.

---

# PATTERN 6 — Electron deficiency, Lewis acids and back bonding

### Q28
The number of electrons around boron in BF3 is

(a) 4 &nbsp; (b) 6 &nbsp; (c) 8 &nbsp; (d) 10

**ANSWER: (b)** — Six: three of boron's own plus one from each of three
fluorines. A **sextet**, not an octet, plus one **empty 2p orbital**. That is
why BF3 is electron deficient and a Lewis acid.

---

### Q29
The shape of BF3 is

(a) pyramidal &nbsp; (b) tetrahedral
(c) trigonal planar &nbsp; (d) bent

**ANSWER: (c)** — sp2 hybridised, **trigonal planar**, bond angles 120°,
molecule **non-polar** (the three bond dipoles cancel).

---

### Q30
The correct order of Lewis acid strength is

(a) BF3 > BCl3 > BBr3 > BI3
(b) BI3 > BBr3 > BCl3 > BF3
(c) BCl3 > BF3 > BI3 > BBr3
(d) BF3 > BI3 > BBr3 > BCl3

**ANSWER: (b)** — `BI3 > BBr3 > BCl3 > BF3`, i.e.
`BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3 < BI3`.
```
   REASON: p(pi)-p(pi) BACK BONDING.
   F uses 2p - SAME SIZE as B's 2p - BEST overlap - strongest back
   bonding - boron least deficient - WEAKEST Lewis acid.
   I uses 5p - far too big - almost NO back bonding - boron fully
   deficient - STRONGEST Lewis acid.
```

> **SHORTCUT:** This is the most-asked question of the chapter and the
> intuitive answer is **wrong**. Memorise: **the acid strength follows the
> size of the halogen, not its electronegativity.**

---

### Q31
The B-F bond length in BF3 (130 pm) is shorter than expected for a single
bond because of

(a) sp2 hybridisation
(b) p(pi)-p(pi) back bonding giving partial double-bond character
(c) the high electronegativity of fluorine
(d) hydrogen bonding

**ANSWER: (b)** — Back donation of a fluorine lone pair into boron's empty
2p orbital gives the bond partial double-bond character, shortening it.

---

### Q32
Which of the following does NOT exist?

(a) `[BF4]-` &nbsp; (b) `[AlF6]3-` &nbsp; (c) `[BF6]3-` &nbsp; (d) `[Al(H2O)6]3+`

**ANSWER: (c)** — `[BF6]3-`. Boron's valence shell has only one 2s and three
2p orbitals and **no d orbitals**, so its maximum covalency is **4**.
Aluminium has vacant 3d orbitals and reaches a covalency of **6**.

---

### Q33
`BF3 + NH3 --> F3B <- NH3`. In this reaction BF3 acts as

(a) a Lewis base &nbsp; (b) a Lewis acid
(c) a Bronsted acid &nbsp; (d) a reducing agent

**ANSWER: (b)** — It **accepts** a lone pair from nitrogen, which is the
definition of a Lewis acid. Boron becomes sp3 and the adduct is tetrahedral
about boron.

---

# PATTERN 7 — Anomalous boron and the diagonal relationship

### Q34
Boron differs from the rest of group 13 mainly because it

(a) is the largest atom
(b) has no d orbitals and only four orbitals available
(c) has a low ionisation enthalpy
(d) shows the inert pair effect

**ANSWER: (b)** — Small size, high IE, high EN, only four orbitals and
**no d orbitals**.

---

### Q35
Boron shows a diagonal relationship with

(a) carbon &nbsp; (b) beryllium &nbsp; (c) silicon &nbsp; (d) magnesium

**ANSWER: (c)** — **Silicon**. The three diagonal pairs are **Li-Mg, Be-Al and
B-Si** — one step across, one step down.

---

### Q36
Which pair of statements is true of both boron and silicon?

(a) both form ionic compounds and basic oxides
(b) both form covalent compounds and acidic oxides
(c) both are metals with amphoteric oxides
(d) both show a +1 oxidation state

**ANSWER: (b)** — Both form only **covalent** compounds and their oxides
`B2O3` and `SiO2` are both **acidic**, dissolving in alkali to give borates
and silicates.

---

### Q37
Which of the following is hydrolysed completely by water?

(a) `CCl4` &nbsp; (b) `BCl3` &nbsp; (c) `CF4` &nbsp; (d) `SF6`

**ANSWER: (b)** — `BCl3 + 3 H2O -> H3BO3 + 3 HCl`. Boron accepts a lone pair
from water into its empty 2p orbital. `CCl4` is not hydrolysed (carbon has no
d orbitals **and** no empty orbital available).

---

### Q38
Which oxide is acidic?

(a) `Al2O3` &nbsp; (b) `Tl2O3` &nbsp; (c) `B2O3` &nbsp; (d) `In2O3`

**ANSWER: (c)** — `B2O3` is **acidic**; `Al2O3` and `Ga2O3` are amphoteric;
`In2O3` and `Tl2O3` are basic. Acidic → amphoteric → basic going down.

---

# PATTERN 8 — Reactions with air, acids, alkalis and halogens

### Q39
Aluminium does not corrode in air because

(a) it is unreactive
(b) it forms a thin impervious layer of Al2O3
(c) it forms AlN
(d) it is amphoteric

**ANSWER: (b)** — A thin, hard, continuous, **impervious oxide layer** seals
the surface and makes the metal **passive**. Thickening it deliberately is
called **anodising**.

---

### Q40
Concentrated nitric acid can be stored in aluminium containers because

(a) it does not react with aluminium at all
(b) it renders aluminium passive by forming an oxide layer
(c) aluminium is a noble metal
(d) it dissolves the aluminium slowly

**ANSWER: (b)** — Conc. HNO3 is a strong oxidiser and instantly builds a thick
protective `Al2O3` layer, so the reaction stops.

---

### Q41
Aluminium dissolves in NaOH solution evolving

(a) O2 &nbsp; (b) H2 &nbsp; (c) NH3 &nbsp; (d) no gas

**ANSWER: (b)** — Hydrogen.
```
   2 Al  +  2 NaOH  +  6 H2O   -->   2 Na[Al(OH)4]  +  3 H2
```

---

### Q42
Which of these shows that aluminium is amphoteric?

(a) it reacts with oxygen
(b) it reacts with both HCl and NaOH giving H2
(c) it forms a dimeric chloride
(d) it forms alums

**ANSWER: (b)** — Reacting with **both** an acid and a base is the definition
of amphoteric.

---

### Q43
The product formed when boron is heated with dinitrogen is

(a) `B2O3` &nbsp; (b) `BN` &nbsp; (c) `B2H6` &nbsp; (d) `B4C`

**ANSWER: (b)** — Boron nitride, `2 B + N2 -> 2 BN`. It is called
**"inorganic graphite"** because it has graphite's layer structure with B and
N alternating. Its cubic form (**borazon**) is one of the hardest substances.

---

### Q44
Boron reacts with concentrated nitric acid to give

(a) `B2O3` &nbsp; (b) `H3BO3` &nbsp; (c) `BN` &nbsp; (d) no reaction

**ANSWER: (b)** — `B + 3 HNO3 (conc.) -> H3BO3 + 3 NO2`. Boron does **not**
react with dilute acids at all.

---

# PATTERN 9 — Borax

### Q45
The correct formula of borax is

(a) `Na2B4O7.5H2O` &nbsp; (b) `Na2B4O7.10H2O`
(c) `NaBO2.4H2O` &nbsp; (d) `Na3BO3.10H2O`

**ANSWER: (b)** — `Na2B4O7.10H2O`; the real structural formula is
`Na2[B4O5(OH)4].8H2O`.

---

### Q46
In the borax anion `[B4O5(OH)4]2-`, the number of sp3 hybridised boron atoms
is

(a) 0 &nbsp; (b) 1 &nbsp; (c) 2 &nbsp; (d) 4

**ANSWER: (c)** — **Two** borons are sp3 (tetrahedral BO4 units, carrying the
2- charge) and **two** are sp2 (trigonal planar BO3 units).

> **SHORTCUT:** Borax anion = **2 triangles + 2 tetrahedra**. Any option
> claiming all four are the same is wrong.

---

### Q47
An aqueous solution of borax is

(a) acidic &nbsp; (b) neutral &nbsp; (c) alkaline &nbsp; (d) amphoteric

**ANSWER: (c)** — Alkaline. It is the salt of a **strong base and a weak
acid**, so it hydrolyses:
`Na2B4O7 + 7 H2O -> 2 NaOH + 4 H3BO3`.

---

### Q48
The transparent glassy borax bead consists of

(a) `Na2B4O7` only &nbsp; (b) `NaBO2 + B2O3`
(c) `H3BO3 + B2O3` &nbsp; (d) `Na3BO3`

**ANSWER: (b)** — `Na2B4O7 --(red heat)--> 2 NaBO2 + B2O3`. Sodium metaborate
plus boric anhydride.

---

### Q49
The blue colour of the borax bead in a cobalt test is due to

(a) `CoO` &nbsp; (b) `Co(BO2)2` &nbsp; (c) `Co2O3` &nbsp; (d) `CoB`

**ANSWER: (b)** — Cobalt **metaborate**, from
`CoO + B2O3 --(heat)--> Co(BO2)2`.

---

### Q50
In the borax bead test the colour given by a chromium salt is

(a) blue &nbsp; (b) green &nbsp; (c) violet &nbsp; (d) brown

**ANSWER: (b)** — **Green** in both the oxidising and the reducing flame.

```
   THE FOUR TO MEMORISE
      Cu  blue-green (ox)   /  red opaque (red)
      Co  deep blue         /  deep blue
      Cr  green             /  green
      Fe  yellowish-brown   /  bottle green
```

---

### Q51
A platinum wire is used in the borax bead test because platinum

(a) is a good conductor
(b) is inert, adds no colour of its own and has a high melting point
(c) reacts with borax
(d) is cheap

**ANSWER: (b)**

# PATTERN 10 — Orthoboric acid

### Q52
Orthoboric acid is

(a) tribasic and strong &nbsp; (b) monobasic and weak
(c) dibasic and weak &nbsp; (d) tribasic and weak

**ANSWER: (b)** — **Monobasic and very weak** (Ka ≈ `5.8 x 10^-10`).

---

### Q53
H3BO3 acts as an acid by

(a) donating one proton
(b) donating three protons
(c) accepting a hydroxide ion from water
(d) accepting a proton

**ANSWER: (c)** — It is a **Lewis acid**:
`B(OH)3 + 2 H2O -> [B(OH)4]- + H3O+`. One `H3O+` per molecule → monobasic.

> **SHORTCUT:** If a question says "H3BO3 has three H atoms but is monobasic",
> the answer always contains the phrase **"accepts OH- from water"**.

---

### Q54
Boric acid feels soapy to the touch because

(a) it is a strong base
(b) its hydrogen-bonded layers slide over one another
(c) it dissolves in water
(d) it forms a gel

**ANSWER: (b)** — Planar BO3 units are linked by **hydrogen bonds** into
sheets, and the sheets are held only by weak van der Waals forces, so they
slide — exactly like graphite.

---

### Q55
On heating to about 370 K, H3BO3 gives

(a) `B2O3` &nbsp; (b) `HBO2` &nbsp; (c) `H2B4O7` &nbsp; (d) `Na2B4O7`

**ANSWER: (b)** — Metaboric acid.
```
   H3BO3 --370K--> HBO2 --430K--> H2B4O7 --red heat--> B2O3
```

---

### Q56
Boric acid can be titrated sharply against NaOH after adding

(a) glycerol or mannitol &nbsp; (b) methanol
(c) sulphuric acid &nbsp; (d) sodium chloride

**ANSWER: (a)** — A polyhydroxy **cis-diol** such as glycerol or mannitol
chelates the boron and makes the acid much stronger.

---

# PATTERN 11 — Diborane and the boranes

### Q57
The number of valence electrons available in B2H6 is

(a) 10 &nbsp; (b) 12 &nbsp; (c) 14 &nbsp; (d) 16

**ANSWER: (b)** — `2 x 3 + 6 x 1 = 12`. Fourteen would be needed for seven
normal two-electron bonds, so diborane is **two electrons short** — it is
**electron deficient**.

---

### Q58
In diborane, the number of three-centre two-electron bonds is

(a) 1 &nbsp; (b) 2 &nbsp; (c) 4 &nbsp; (d) 6

**ANSWER: (b)** — **Two** — one for each bridging hydrogen. The four terminal
hydrogens use ordinary 2c-2e bonds.

> **SHORTCUT:** In `B2H6` remember **4 terminal + 2 bridging**. Any question
> about "banana bonds", "3c-2e bonds" or "bridging hydrogens" has the answer
> **2**.

---

### Q59
In B2H6, the hybridisation of boron is

(a) sp &nbsp; (b) sp2 &nbsp; (c) sp3 &nbsp; (d) sp3d

**ANSWER: (c)** — `sp3`. (In `BF3`, by contrast, boron is `sp2`.)

---

### Q60
Which statement about diborane is INCORRECT?

(a) the four terminal hydrogens and the two borons lie in one plane
(b) the two bridging hydrogens lie perpendicular to that plane
(c) there is a direct B-B bond
(d) the B-Hb bond is longer than the B-Ht bond

**ANSWER: (c)** — **There is NO direct B-B bond** in diborane. The borons are
held together only through the two hydrogen bridges. (B-Ht = 119 pm,
B-Hb = 133 pm.)

---

### Q61
Diborane on hydrolysis gives

(a) `B2O3 + H2` &nbsp; (b) `H3BO3 + H2`
(c) `HBO2 + O2` &nbsp; (d) `BN + H2`

**ANSWER: (b)** — `B2H6 + 6 H2O -> 2 H3BO3 + 6 H2`.

---

### Q62
When diborane reacts with excess ammonia at low temperature and the product
is heated to 473 K, the compound formed is

(a) `BN` &nbsp; (b) `B3N3H6` &nbsp; (c) `NH4BH4` &nbsp; (d) `B2O3`

**ANSWER: (b)** — **Borazine**, `B3N3H6`, called **"inorganic benzene"** — a
flat six-membered ring of alternating B and N atoms.

---

### Q63
Diborane is prepared in the laboratory by

(a) `2 NaBH4 + I2 -> B2H6 + 2 NaI + H2`
(b) `B2O3 + 3 H2 -> B2H6 + 3 O2`
(c) `2 B + 3 H2 -> B2H6`
(d) `BN + 3 H2 -> B2H6 + N2`

**ANSWER: (a)** — Oxidation of sodium borohydride with iodine. The industrial
route is `2 BF3 + 6 NaH --(450 K)--> B2H6 + 6 NaF`.

---

### Q64
`B2H6 + 2 NMe3 -> 2 BH3.NMe3` shows that diborane is

(a) a Lewis base &nbsp; (b) an electron-deficient Lewis acid
(c) a reducing agent only &nbsp; (d) an ionic compound

**ANSWER: (b)** — A Lewis base **splits the bridge** into two BH3 units,
proving diborane's electron deficiency.

# PATTERN 12 — Aluminium compounds and alums

### Q65
Anhydrous aluminium chloride exists as

(a) `AlCl3` monomer only
(b) `Al2Cl6` dimer in the vapour and in non-polar solvents
(c) an ionic lattice
(d) `Al3Cl9`

**ANSWER: (b)** — `2 AlCl3 == Al2Cl6`. Above about 1073 K it splits into
planar monomers.

---

### Q66
The bridging bond in `Al2Cl6` is

(a) a three-centre two-electron bond
(b) an ordinary coordinate (dative) bond from a chlorine lone pair
(c) an ionic bond
(d) a hydrogen bond

**ANSWER: (b)** — Chlorine **has** lone pairs, so it can donate one in the
normal way. Diborane needs a 3c-2e bond only because hydrogen has **no** lone
pair to give.

> **SHORTCUT:** `Al2Cl6` = **normal coordinate bond**. `B2H6` = **3c-2e banana
> bond**. This exact contrast is a favourite MCQ.

---

### Q67
Which aluminium halide does NOT form a dimer?

(a) `AlCl3` &nbsp; (b) `AlBr3` &nbsp; (c) `AlI3` &nbsp; (d) `AlF3`

**ANSWER: (d)** — `AlF3` is **ionic** and high melting; fluorine is too small
to bridge.

---

### Q68
Anhydrous AlCl3 cannot be prepared by evaporating an aqueous solution because

(a) it sublimes
(b) it is hydrolysed to Al(OH)3
(c) it decomposes to aluminium
(d) it is insoluble

**ANSWER: (b)** — `AlCl3 + 3 H2O -> Al(OH)3 + 3 HCl`. Only **dry** methods
work.

---

### Q69
Anhydrous AlCl3 is used as a catalyst in

(a) the Haber process &nbsp; (b) Friedel-Crafts reactions
(c) the contact process &nbsp; (d) hydrogenation of oils

**ANSWER: (b)** — Friedel-Crafts alkylation and acylation, because AlCl3 is a
powerful **Lewis acid**.

---

### Q70
The general formula of an alum is

(a) `M2SO4 . M'2(SO4)3 . 24 H2O`
(b) `M2SO4 . M'SO4 . 12 H2O`
(c) `M2SO4 . M'2(SO4)3 . 12 H2O`
(d) `MSO4 . M'2(SO4)3 . 24 H2O`

**ANSWER: (a)** — with M = monovalent (Na+, K+, NH4+) and M' = trivalent
(Al3+, Cr3+, Fe3+). Equivalently `M+M'3+(SO4)2 . 12 H2O`.

---

### Q71
Potash alum is used to purify water because

(a) it kills bacteria
(b) Al3+ hydrolyses to Al(OH)3 which coagulates colloidal impurities
(c) it dissolves the dirt
(d) it removes dissolved oxygen

**ANSWER: (b)** — Gelatinous `Al(OH)3` **coagulates** the negatively charged
colloidal clay particles and carries them down as a sludge.

---

### Q72
An alum is a

(a) complex salt &nbsp; (b) double salt
(c) coordination compound &nbsp; (d) mixed oxide

**ANSWER: (b)** — A **double salt**: in water it gives all the ions of both
parent salts (`K+`, `Al3+`, `2 SO4 2-`). A complex salt would keep its complex
ion intact.

# PATTERN 13 — Uses

### Q73
The thermite reaction is

(a) `2 Al + Fe2O3 -> Al2O3 + 2 Fe`
(b) `Fe + Al2O3 -> Fe2O3 + Al`
(c) `2 Al + 3 Cl2 -> Al2Cl6`
(d) `Al2O3 + 2 NaOH -> 2 NaAlO2 + H2O`

**ANSWER: (a)** — Hugely exothermic; the molten iron produced is used for
**thermite welding** of rails. The same reaction extracts Cr and Mn.

---

### Q74
Duralumin is an alloy of

(a) Al + Mg only
(b) Al + Cu + Mg + Mn
(c) Al + Ni + Co + Fe
(d) Al + Zn + Sn

**ANSWER: (b)** — Light and strong, used for **aircraft bodies**. Magnalium is
Al + Mg; Alnico is Al + Ni + Co + Fe.

---

### Q75
Aluminium is preferred to copper for overhead transmission lines because

(a) it is a better conductor per unit volume
(b) weight for weight it conducts about twice as well and is cheaper
(c) it does not oxidise
(d) it has a higher melting point

**ANSWER: (b)** — Copper conducts better per unit **volume**, but aluminium is
only about one third as dense, so per unit **mass** it wins easily.

---

### Q76
Borosilicate (Pyrex) glass is made using

(a) borax and boric acid &nbsp; (b) alumina
(c) cryolite &nbsp; (d) bauxite

**ANSWER: (a)** — Boron compounds give the glass its resistance to heat and
sudden temperature change.

---

### Q77
Which is used as an abrasive?

(a) `B2H6` &nbsp; (b) `B4C` &nbsp; (c) `H3BO3` &nbsp; (d) `Na2B4O7.10H2O`

**ANSWER: (b)** — Boron carbide. Cubic boron nitride (**borazon**) and
corundum (`Al2O3`) are also abrasives.

---

### Q78
Borax is used as a flux in soldering because

(a) it is alkaline
(b) molten borax dissolves the metal oxide film and leaves a clean surface
(c) it lowers the melting point of the solder
(d) it conducts electricity

**ANSWER: (b)**
