# Stoichiometry — Concepts Explained Simply

Read one topic. Do its worked example with a pen. Then move to the next.
Nothing here assumes you remember anything from Class 10.

The word **stoichiometry** just means **"measuring the elements"**.
It is the arithmetic of chemistry — how much reacts with how much, and how
much product you get.

---

# TOPIC 1 — Why we need this chapter at all

You cannot see atoms. You cannot pick them up with tweezers and count them.
But chemical reactions happen between definite NUMBERS of atoms.

```
   The equation      2 H2  +  O2  ->  2 H2O

   really means      2 molecules of H2  react with  1 molecule of O2

   But in the lab you cannot count molecules.
   You can only WEIGH things on a balance, or MEASURE a volume.
```

So the whole chapter is about building a **bridge**:

```
   +----------------+          +---------+          +------------------+
   |  WHAT YOU CAN  |          |         |          |  WHAT CHEMISTRY  |
   |  MEASURE       |  <---->  |  MOLES  |  <---->  |  ACTUALLY CARES  |
   |  grams, litres |          |         |          |  ABOUT: numbers  |
   +----------------+          +---------+          +------------------+
```

**Everything in this chapter is either building that bridge or walking on it.**

# TOPIC 2 — The laws of chemical combination

These are six experimental laws discovered before anyone knew what an atom
looked like. They are pure memory marks. Learn one sentence + one example each.

## 2.1 Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1789)

```
  STATEMENT
  In a chemical reaction, matter is neither created nor destroyed.
  The total mass of the reactants = the total mass of the products.

  EXAMPLE
       2 H2   +   O2    ->    2 H2O
        4 g   +  32 g   ->     36 g
              36 g              36 g       <- equal. Law obeyed.
```

**Analogy:** if you break a 100 g biscuit into crumbs, you still have 100 g of
crumbs. Nothing vanished, it only changed shape.

> **TRAP:** in a reaction that produces a GAS, the mass seems to fall because
> the gas escapes. The law is still true — you just did not weigh the gas.
> This is why such experiments must be done in a CLOSED container.

## 2.2 Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions (Proust, 1799)

```
  STATEMENT
  A given pure compound always contains the SAME elements combined in the
  SAME fixed ratio by MASS, no matter where it came from or how it was made.

  EXAMPLE
  Water from a river, from rain, or made in a lab is ALWAYS

           H : O   =   1 : 8   by mass
           (in H2O:  2 g of H  combines with  16 g of O)

  Carbon dioxide is ALWAYS   C : O  =  12 : 32  =  3 : 8  by mass.
```

**Analogy:** an idli is always made with the same rice-to-dal ratio. Change the
ratio and it is no longer an idli, it is something else.

## 2.3 Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton, 1803)

```
  STATEMENT
  When two elements A and B combine to form MORE THAN ONE compound, then
  the masses of B that combine with a FIXED mass of A are in a simple
  whole-number ratio.

  EXAMPLE 1  -  carbon and oxygen

     CO     :  12 g of C  combines with  16 g of O
     CO2    :  12 g of C  combines with  32 g of O

     Fix carbon at 12 g.  Then  O : O  =  16 : 32  =  1 : 2      SIMPLE.

  EXAMPLE 2  -  nitrogen and oxygen

     N2O  ,  NO  ,  N2O3  ,  NO2  ,  N2O5

     Fix nitrogen at 28 g:
        N2O   -> 16 g O
        NO    -> 32 g O
        N2O3  -> 48 g O
        NO2   -> 64 g O
        N2O5  -> 80 g O

     Ratio  16 : 32 : 48 : 64 : 80   =   1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5      SIMPLE.
```

## 2.4 Law of Reciprocal Proportions (Richter, 1792)

```
  STATEMENT
  When two elements B and C combine separately with a FIXED mass of a third
  element A, then the ratio in which B and C combine with each other is
  either the SAME ratio or a simple multiple of it.

  EXAMPLE   take H2O , H2S and SO2

     In H2O :   2 g of H  combines with  16 g of O
     In H2S :   2 g of H  combines with  32 g of S

     So for the same 2 g of hydrogen,   O : S  =  16 : 32  =  1 : 2

     Now look at SO2 :  32 g of S combines with 32 g of O
                        so in SO2 ,  O : S  =  32 : 32  =  1 : 1

     Compare the two ratios:     (1 / 2)  divided by  (1 / 1)   =   1 : 2

     A simple whole-number ratio.  Law obeyed.
```

## 2.5 Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808)

```
  STATEMENT
  When gases react, they do so in volumes that bear a SIMPLE WHOLE-NUMBER
  ratio to one another and to the volumes of the gaseous products,
  provided all volumes are measured at the SAME temperature and pressure.

  EXAMPLE
       H2    +    Cl2    ->    2 HCl
      1 vol      1 vol         2 vol             ratio  1 : 1 : 2

       2 H2   +   O2     ->    2 H2O(g)
      2 vol      1 vol         2 vol             ratio  2 : 1 : 2

       N2    +   3 H2    ->    2 NH3
      1 vol     3 vol          2 vol             ratio  1 : 3 : 2
```

> **TRAP:** this law applies to GASES only. Never to solids or liquids.
> Also, both temperature and pressure must be the same for all gases.

## 2.6 Avogadro's Law (1811)

```
  STATEMENT
  EQUAL VOLUMES of ALL GASES, at the same temperature and pressure,
  contain an EQUAL NUMBER OF MOLECULES.

  So:      volume  is directly proportional to  number of moles
                        V  =  k n         (at constant T and P)
```

This is the single most useful law in the chapter, because it turns a
**volume ratio** straight into a **mole ratio** for gases.

```
  WHAT AVOGADRO'S LAW EXPLAINED

  Gay-Lussac found:      H2  +  Cl2  ->  2 HCl        1 : 1 : 2 by volume

  Avogadro said: then 1 molecule of H2 + 1 molecule of Cl2 gives
  2 molecules of HCl. For each HCl to have hydrogen and chlorine, the
  H2 and Cl2 molecules must have SPLIT.
  -> so a molecule of hydrogen gas contains TWO atoms.
  -> this is how we learned that H2, O2, N2, Cl2 are DIATOMIC.
```

# TOPIC 3 — Dalton's atomic theory

Dalton (1808) explained all the above laws with five simple postulates.

```
  1.  All matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called ATOMS.

  2.  All atoms of a GIVEN element are identical in mass and in
      properties.

  3.  Atoms of DIFFERENT elements have different masses and different
      properties.

  4.  Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in a
      FIXED, simple whole-number ratio.

  5.  Atoms can neither be CREATED nor DESTROYED nor CONVERTED into atoms
      of another element in a chemical reaction.
```

Which postulate explains which law:

```
  +------------------------------+--------------------------------------+
  |  LAW                         |  EXPLAINED BY POSTULATE              |
  +------------------------------+--------------------------------------+
  |  Conservation of mass        |  5 (atoms are not created/destroyed) |
  |  Definite proportions        |  4 (fixed whole-number ratio)        |
  |  Multiple proportions        |  4 (different fixed ratios possible) |
  +------------------------------+--------------------------------------+
```

## Drawbacks of Dalton's theory

```
  1.  Atoms are NOT indivisible - they contain electrons, protons, neutrons.

  2.  Atoms of the same element are NOT always identical in mass -
      ISOTOPES exist (Cl-35 and Cl-37).

  3.  Atoms of different elements can have the SAME mass -
      ISOBARS exist (Ar-40, K-40, Ca-40).

  4.  It cannot explain why atoms combine at all (nothing about bonding).

  5.  It cannot explain non-stoichiometric compounds such as Fe(0.95)O,
      or allotropes.

  6.  In nuclear reactions atoms DO change into other atoms.
```

# TOPIC 4 — Atomic mass, average atomic mass, molecular mass, formula mass

## 4.1 Atomic mass unit

An atom is far too light to measure in grams, so we compare it to a standard.

```
  The standard is the CARBON-12 atom.

  1 atomic mass unit (1 u , also called 1 amu or 1 dalton)
        =   1 / 12  of the mass of one C-12 atom
        =   1.66 x 10^-24  g       =   1.66 x 10^-27  kg

  ATOMIC MASS of an element
        =  how many times heavier one atom of it is than 1 u
```

## 4.2 Average atomic mass

Most elements exist as a mixture of isotopes. The "atomic mass" printed in
the periodic table is a **weighted average**.

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                    |
  |   average       (mass1 x %1)  +  (mass2 x %2)  +  ...              |
  |   atomic   =   -------------------------------------               |
  |   mass                        100                                  |
  |                                                                    |
  |   or, using fractional abundances that add up to 1:                |
  |                                                                    |
  |   average atomic mass = (mass1 x f1) + (mass2 x f2) + ...          |
  |                                                                    |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.1
Chlorine exists as Cl-35 (75%) and Cl-37 (25%). Find the average atomic mass.

```
  STEP 1   Write each mass with its percentage.
              Cl-35  ->  mass 35 ,  75 %
              Cl-37  ->  mass 37 ,  25 %

  STEP 2   Multiply and add.
              (35 x 75)  +  (37 x 25)   =   2625  +  925   =   3550

  STEP 3   Divide by 100.
                        3550
              ------------------  =  35.5  u
                        100

  ANSWER:  average atomic mass of chlorine = 35.5 u
```

**Analogy:** if 75 boys weigh 35 kg and 25 boys weigh 37 kg, the class average
is not 36 — it is pulled closer to 35, because there are more of them.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.2
Boron has two isotopes, B-10 (19.6%) and B-11 (80.4%). Find its average
atomic mass.

```
  (10 x 19.6)  +  (11 x 80.4)      196  +  884.4        1080.4
  ------------------------------ = ---------------- =  ---------- = 10.804 u
              100                       100               100
```

## 4.3 Molecular mass

```
  MOLECULAR MASS  =  sum of the atomic masses of ALL the atoms
                     in one molecule
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.3
Find the molecular mass of H2SO4.

```
  H :   2 atoms  x  1    =    2
  S :   1 atom   x  32   =   32
  O :   4 atoms  x  16   =   64
                            ----
                             98  u          Molecular mass of H2SO4 = 98 u
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.4
Find the molecular mass of Ca(NO3)2.

```
  Careful - the bracket multiplies EVERYTHING inside it by 2.

  Ca :  1  x  40   =   40
  N  :  2  x  14   =   28          (there are 2 nitrogens)
  O  :  6  x  16   =   96          (3 oxygens inside x 2 = 6)
                       ----
                       164  u
```

> **TRAP:** the number outside a bracket multiplies every atom inside it.
> Ca(NO3)2 has 6 oxygens, not 3. Al2(SO4)3 has 3 sulphurs and 12 oxygens.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 4.5
Find the molecular mass of CuSO4 . 5 H2O (blue vitriol).

```
  CuSO4  :  63.5 + 32 + 64        =  159.5
  5 H2O  :  5 x 18                =   90.0
                                     ------
                                     249.5  u

  The dot in a formula means "plus this much water", NOT multiplication of
  the whole formula.
```

## 4.4 Formula mass

Ionic compounds such as NaCl do not exist as separate molecules — they are a
giant lattice. So we do not say "molecular mass", we say **formula mass**.

```
  FORMULA MASS of NaCl  =  23  +  35.5  =  58.5 u

  Use "formula mass" for ionic solids:  NaCl, KCl, CaO, Na2CO3, CaCO3
  Use "molecular mass" for covalent molecules:  H2O, CO2, H2SO4, C6H12O6
```

# TOPIC 5 — THE MOLE CONCEPT (the heart of the chapter)

## 5.1 What a mole is

```
  We use special words for special counting numbers:

      1 pair      =  2 things
      1 dozen     =  12 things
      1 gross     =  144 things
      1 MOLE      =  6.022 x 10^23 things       <-  Avogadro's number, N(A)
```

Why such a strange number? Because it was chosen so that:

```
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                   |
  |   1 mole of ANY substance  =  its atomic / molecular mass          |
  |                               expressed in GRAMS                   |
  |                                                                   |
  |        1 mole of C     =  12 g    =  6.022 x 10^23 C atoms         |
  |        1 mole of H2O   =  18 g    =  6.022 x 10^23 H2O molecules   |
  |        1 mole of NaCl  =  58.5 g  =  6.022 x 10^23 NaCl units      |
  |        1 mole of O2    =  32 g    =  6.022 x 10^23 O2 molecules    |
  |                                                                   |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+

  That mass in grams is called the MOLAR MASS. Unit: g/mol
```

**Analogy:** a shopkeeper does not count rice grains. He weighs 1 kg and knows
roughly how many grains that is. The mole does exactly this for atoms — it
converts a *number* you cannot count into a *mass* you can weigh.

## 5.2 The mole triangle — memorise this picture

```
                          +---------------+
                          |     MOLES     |
                          |      n        |
                          +---------------+
             divide by         /  |  \        multiply by
             molar mass       /   |   \       22.4 (gas at STP)
                    +--------+    |    +--------+
                    |             |             |
              +-----------+  +---------+  +-----------+
              |   MASS    |  | NUMBER  |  |  VOLUME   |
              |  in grams |  |   OF    |  |  in L at  |
              |           |  |PARTICLES|  |   STP     |
              +-----------+  +---------+  +-----------+
                                  |
                          divide / multiply by
                             6.022 x 10^23


  ALL THREE FORMULAS:

       given mass (g)          number of particles         volume at STP (L)
  n = ----------------  =  ------------------------  =  --------------------
      molar mass (g/mol)        6.022 x 10^23                  22.4
```

## 5.3 Molar volume

```
  At STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure = 273 K and 1 atm),
  1 mole of ANY GAS occupies 22.4 litres.

  This is called the GRAM MOLECULAR VOLUME or MOLAR VOLUME.

  It does NOT matter which gas:
       1 mole H2   ( 2 g)   ->  22.4 L
       1 mole O2   (32 g)   ->  22.4 L
       1 mole CO2  (44 g)   ->  22.4 L
  Different masses, SAME volume. That is Avogadro's law in action.
```

> **TRAP:** 22.4 L applies ONLY to gases and ONLY at STP.
> 1 mole of water is 18 g, which is 18 mL of liquid — not 22.4 L.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 5.1
How many moles are there in 22 g of CO2?

```
  STEP 1   Molar mass of CO2  =  12 + (2 x 16)  =  44 g/mol

  STEP 2                  given mass        22
              n  =  --------------------  = ----  =  0.5 mol
                       molar mass            44

  ANSWER:  0.5 mole
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 5.2
Calculate the number of molecules in 4.4 g of CO2, and the number of atoms.

```
  STEP 1   n  =  4.4 / 44  =  0.1 mol

  STEP 2   molecules  =  n  x  6.022 x 10^23
                      =  0.1 x 6.022 x 10^23
                      =  6.022 x 10^22  molecules of CO2

  STEP 3   Each CO2 molecule has 3 atoms (1 C + 2 O).
              atoms  =  3  x  6.022 x 10^22  =  1.807 x 10^23 atoms
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 5.3
What volume will 8 g of oxygen gas occupy at STP?

```
  STEP 1   Molar mass of O2  =  32 g/mol

  STEP 2   n  =  8 / 32  =  0.25 mol

  STEP 3   V  =  n x 22.4  =  0.25 x 22.4  =  5.6  L

  ANSWER:  5.6 litres
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 5.4
How many grams of NaOH contain 3.011 x 10^23 formula units?

```
  STEP 1                3.011 x 10^23         1
              n  =  --------------------  =  ---  =  0.5 mol
                       6.022 x 10^23           2

  STEP 2   Molar mass of NaOH  =  23 + 16 + 1  =  40 g/mol

  STEP 3   mass  =  n x molar mass  =  0.5 x 40  =  20 g
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 5.5
Calculate the mass of one atom of carbon.

```
                molar mass            12 g/mol
  mass  =  ---------------------  =  ----------------  =  1.99 x 10^-23 g
              6.022 x 10^23          6.022 x 10^23
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 5.6
How many atoms of oxygen are present in 0.2 mole of H2SO4?

```
  1 molecule of H2SO4 has 4 oxygen atoms.

  moles of O atoms  =  0.2  x  4  =  0.8 mol

  atoms of O  =  0.8 x 6.022 x 10^23  =  4.82 x 10^23 atoms
```

# TOPIC 6 — Percentage composition

```
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                   |
  |   % of an element    total mass of that element in 1 mole         |
  |   in a compound  =  --------------------------------------- x 100 |
  |                            molar mass of compound                 |
  |                                                                   |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 6.1
Find the percentage composition of ethanol, C2H5OH.

```
  STEP 1   Molar mass
              C :  2 x 12  =  24
              H :  6 x  1  =   6      (5 in C2H5 + 1 in OH = 6)
              O :  1 x 16  =  16
                              ---
                               46 g/mol

  STEP 2   Each percentage

                     24
              C  =  ----  x 100  =  52.17 %
                     46

                      6
              H  =  ----  x 100  =  13.04 %
                     46

                     16
              O  =  ----  x 100  =  34.78 %
                     46

  STEP 3   CHECK: 52.17 + 13.04 + 34.78 = 99.99, near enough to 100. Good.
```

> **TRAP:** always check that your percentages add up to about 100. If they
> do not, you made an arithmetic mistake. This free check has saved many marks.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 6.2
Find the percentage of nitrogen in urea, NH2CONH2.

```
  Molar mass  =  N(2 x 14) + H(4 x 1) + C(12) + O(16)
              =  28 + 4 + 12 + 16  =  60 g/mol

              28
  % N  =  --------  x 100  =  46.67 %
              60
```

# TOPIC 7 — Empirical formula and molecular formula

```
  EMPIRICAL FORMULA  -  the SIMPLEST whole-number ratio of atoms.
  MOLECULAR FORMULA  -  the ACTUAL number of atoms in one molecule.

  +--------------+---------------------+---------------------+
  |  COMPOUND    | EMPIRICAL FORMULA   | MOLECULAR FORMULA   |
  +--------------+---------------------+---------------------+
  |  Glucose     |  CH2O               |  C6H12O6            |
  |  Benzene     |  CH                 |  C6H6               |
  |  Acetylene   |  CH                 |  C2H2               |
  |  Hydrogen    |  HO                 |  H2O2               |
  |  peroxide    |                     |                     |
  |  Water       |  H2O                |  H2O   (same)       |
  +--------------+---------------------+---------------------+
```

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |     MOLECULAR FORMULA   =   n   x   EMPIRICAL FORMULA            |
  |                                                                  |
  |                molecular mass                                    |
  |     where  n = -----------------------                           |
  |                empirical formula mass                            |
  |                                                                  |
  |     n is always a WHOLE NUMBER (1, 2, 3, 6 ...)                  |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## The 5-step method for empirical formula

```
  STEP 1   Write the % of each element. (If masses are given, use them.)
  STEP 2   Divide each % by that element's ATOMIC MASS -> moles.
  STEP 3   Divide every answer by the SMALLEST of them -> simple ratio.
  STEP 4   If any number is still a fraction like 1.5 or 1.33,
           multiply ALL of them by 2 or 3 to clear it.
  STEP 5   Write the whole numbers as subscripts. That is the empirical
           formula.
```

```
  TABLE LAYOUT THE EXAMINER LIKES - use it every time

  +---------+---------+----------+-------------+-------------+---------+
  | Element |   %     | At. mass | % / At.mass | divide by   | whole   |
  |         |         |          |  = moles    | smallest    | number  |
  +---------+---------+----------+-------------+-------------+---------+
  |         |         |          |             |             |         |
  +---------+---------+----------+-------------+-------------+---------+
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.1
A compound contains C 40%, H 6.7% and O 53.3%. Its molecular mass is 180.
Find the empirical and molecular formulas.

```
  +---------+--------+---------+-----------------+-----------+--------+
  | Element |   %    | At.mass |  moles          | / 3.33    | whole  |
  +---------+--------+---------+-----------------+-----------+--------+
  |   C     |  40.0  |   12    | 40 / 12 = 3.33  | 3.33/3.33 |   1    |
  |         |        |         |                 |  = 1.00   |        |
  |   H     |   6.7  |    1    | 6.7 / 1  = 6.70 | 6.70/3.33 |   2    |
  |         |        |         |                 |  = 2.01   |        |
  |   O     |  53.3  |   16    | 53.3/ 16 = 3.33 | 3.33/3.33 |   1    |
  |         |        |         |                 |  = 1.00   |        |
  +---------+--------+---------+-----------------+-----------+--------+

  EMPIRICAL FORMULA  =  C H2 O   ->  written as  CH2O

  Empirical formula mass  =  12 + (2 x 1) + 16  =  30

           molecular mass        180
    n  =  -------------------  = -----  =  6
          empirical f. mass       30

  MOLECULAR FORMULA  =  6 x (CH2O)  =  C6H12O6      (glucose)
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.2
An oxide of iron contains 69.9% iron and 30.1% oxygen. Find its empirical
formula. (Fe = 55.85)

```
  Fe :  69.9 / 55.85  =  1.2516
  O  :  30.1 / 16     =  1.8813

  Divide by the smaller, 1.2516 :

  Fe :  1.2516 / 1.2516  =  1.000
  O  :  1.8813 / 1.2516  =  1.503        <-  still a fraction!

  STEP 4: multiply BOTH by 2

  Fe :  1 x 2  =  2
  O  :  1.5 x 2 = 3

  EMPIRICAL FORMULA  =  Fe2O3
```

> **TRAP:** never round 1.5 down to 1. If you see 1.5 multiply by 2;
> if you see 1.33 or 1.67 multiply by 3; if you see 1.25 multiply by 4.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 7.3
A hydrocarbon contains 92.3% carbon and 7.7% hydrogen. Its vapour density is
39. Find the molecular formula.

```
  STEP 1   C :  92.3 / 12  =  7.69
           H :   7.7 / 1   =  7.70

  STEP 2   Divide by 7.69:   C = 1.00 ,  H = 1.00

  STEP 3   EMPIRICAL FORMULA = CH ,  empirical mass = 13

  STEP 4   Molecular mass = 2 x vapour density = 2 x 39 = 78

  STEP 5   n = 78 / 13 = 6

  MOLECULAR FORMULA = C6H6         (benzene)
```

```
  USEFUL SIDE FACT:      molecular mass  =  2  x  vapour density
```

# TOPIC 8 — Stoichiometric calculations from a balanced equation

A balanced equation is a **recipe in moles**.

```
       CaCO3     ->     CaO     +     CO2

  READ IT AS:
     1 mole CaCO3  gives  1 mole CaO  and  1 mole CO2
     100 g         gives  56 g        and  44 g  (or 22.4 L at STP)
```

## The universal 4-step method

```
  STEP 1   Write and BALANCE the equation.
  STEP 2   Convert the GIVEN quantity into MOLES.
  STEP 3   Use the coefficient ratio to get MOLES of what is asked.
  STEP 4   Convert those moles back into grams (x molar mass) or
           litres (x 22.4, gas at STP only).

           GRAMS  ->  MOLES  ->  ratio  ->  MOLES  ->  GRAMS / LITRES
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 8.1
Calculate the mass of CaO and the volume of CO2 at STP produced by heating
50 g of CaCO3.

```
  STEP 1   CaCO3  ->  CaO  +  CO2                        (already balanced)

  STEP 2   Molar mass CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 g/mol
                       50
              n  =  --------  =  0.5 mol
                      100

  STEP 3   Ratio is 1 : 1 : 1
              moles of CaO  =  0.5      moles of CO2  =  0.5

  STEP 4   mass of CaO   =  0.5  x  56   =  28 g
           volume of CO2 =  0.5  x  22.4 =  11.2 L at STP

  ANSWER:  28 g of CaO and 11.2 L of CO2
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 8.2
How many grams of oxygen are needed to burn 16 g of methane completely?

```
  STEP 1   CH4  +  2 O2  ->  CO2  +  2 H2O

  STEP 2   Molar mass CH4 = 12 + 4 = 16
              n(CH4)  =  16 / 16  =  1 mol

  STEP 3   Ratio CH4 : O2  =  1 : 2
              n(O2)  =  1 x 2  =  2 mol

  STEP 4   mass of O2  =  2 x 32  =  64 g
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 8.3
What volume of hydrogen at STP is liberated when 13 g of zinc reacts with
excess dilute H2SO4? (Zn = 65)

```
  STEP 1   Zn  +  H2SO4  ->  ZnSO4  +  H2

  STEP 2   n(Zn)  =  13 / 65  =  0.2 mol

  STEP 3   Ratio Zn : H2  =  1 : 1   ->  n(H2)  =  0.2 mol

  STEP 4   V  =  0.2 x 22.4  =  4.48 L
```

# TOPIC 9 — LIMITING REAGENT

**The idea in one picture:**

```
  You want to make sandwiches.  Recipe:  2 slices of bread + 1 slice cheese

  You have:   10 slices of bread    ->  enough for 5 sandwiches
               3 slices of cheese   ->  enough for 3 sandwiches

  You can only make 3 sandwiches. CHEESE ran out first.

  CHEESE is the LIMITING REAGENT.
  BREAD is in EXCESS (4 slices left over).
```

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  The LIMITING REAGENT is the reactant that gets used up FIRST.    |
  |  It DECIDES how much product you can make.                        |
  |  Everything must be calculated from the LIMITING reagent.         |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## How to find it — the divide-by-coefficient trick

```
  STEP 1   Balance the equation.
  STEP 2   Convert EVERY given mass into moles.
  STEP 3   Divide each reactant's moles by its COEFFICIENT in the equation.
  STEP 4   The SMALLEST answer belongs to the LIMITING REAGENT.
  STEP 5   Calculate the product from the LIMITING reagent only.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 9.1
4 g of H2 is mixed with 16 g of O2 and ignited. Which is the limiting reagent?
How much water is formed? How much of the excess reactant is left?

```
  STEP 1   2 H2  +  O2  ->  2 H2O

  STEP 2   n(H2)  =  4 / 2   =  2 mol
           n(O2)  =  16 / 32 =  0.5 mol

  STEP 3   Divide by coefficients:
              H2 :  2   / 2  =  1.0
              O2 :  0.5 / 1  =  0.5        <-  SMALLER

  STEP 4   OXYGEN is the LIMITING REAGENT.

  STEP 5   From O2:  ratio O2 : H2O  =  1 : 2
              n(H2O)  =  0.5 x 2  =  1 mol
              mass of water  =  1 x 18  =  18 g

  EXCESS LEFT:
              H2 actually used  =  0.5 x 2  =  1 mol   (ratio O2:H2 = 1:2)
              H2 left over      =  2 - 1    =  1 mol   =  2 g
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 9.2
50.0 kg of N2 and 10.0 kg of H2 are mixed to make ammonia. Find the limiting
reagent and the mass of NH3 produced.

```
  STEP 1   N2  +  3 H2  ->  2 NH3

  STEP 2   n(N2)  =  50000 / 28  =  1785.7 mol
           n(H2)  =  10000 / 2   =  5000 mol

  STEP 3   N2 :  1785.7 / 1  =  1785.7
           H2 :  5000    / 3  =  1666.7      <-  SMALLER

  STEP 4   HYDROGEN is the limiting reagent.

  STEP 5   ratio H2 : NH3  =  3 : 2
                          2
              n(NH3)  =  ---  x  5000   =  3333.3 mol
                          3

              mass  =  3333.3  x  17  =  56666 g  =  56.67 kg
```

> **TRAP:** the reactant with the smaller MASS is not automatically limiting.
> You must compare MOLES divided by COEFFICIENTS. In Example 9.2 hydrogen
> weighed far less but it is still the correct comparison that decides.

# TOPIC 10 — Concentration terms

A "solution" is solute dissolved in solvent. There are six ways to say
"how strong" it is.

```
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  |  TERM           |  DEFINITION                        |  DEPENDS ON |
  |                 |                                    | TEMPERATURE?|
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  | Mass percent    | mass of solute / mass of solution  |     NO      |
  |     w/w %       | x 100                              |             |
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  | Mole fraction   | moles of one component /           |     NO      |
  |      x          | total moles                        |             |
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  | Molarity  M     | moles of solute / litre of         |    YES      |
  |                 | SOLUTION                           |             |
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  | Molality  m     | moles of solute / kg of SOLVENT    |     NO      |
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  | Normality N     | gram equivalents of solute /       |    YES      |
  |                 | litre of solution                  |             |
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
  | ppm             | mass of solute / mass of solution  |     NO      |
  |                 | x 10^6                             |             |
  +-----------------+------------------------------------+-------------+
```

## 10.1 Mass percent

```
                            mass of solute
   mass %  =  ------------------------------------------  x 100
              mass of solute  +  mass of solvent
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.1
20 g of sugar is dissolved in 80 g of water. Find the mass percent of sugar.

```
  mass of solution  =  20 + 80  =  100 g

            20
  mass % = -----  x 100  =  20 %
            100
```

## 10.2 Mole fraction

```
   For a solution of A (solute) in B (solvent):

                    n(A)                              n(B)
      x(A)  =  --------------        x(B)  =  --------------
                n(A) + n(B)                    n(A) + n(B)

      ALWAYS:      x(A)  +  x(B)  =  1
      Mole fraction has NO UNITS.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.2
Find the mole fraction of ethanol in a solution of 46 g ethanol (C2H5OH) in
54 g water.

```
  n(ethanol) =  46 / 46  =  1 mol
  n(water)   =  54 / 18  =  3 mol

                    1            1
  x(ethanol) =  --------  =  -------  =  0.25
                 1 + 3          4

  x(water)   =  3 / 4  =  0.75           Check: 0.25 + 0.75 = 1  GOOD
```

## 10.3 Molarity

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |                 moles of solute            mass of solute        |
  |   MOLARITY M = --------------------- = ------------------------- |
  |                 volume of SOLUTION      molar mass x V (litres)  |
  |                 in LITRES                                        |
  |                                                                  |
  |   If volume is in mL:      M  =  (mass x 1000)                   |
  |                                  -------------------------       |
  |                                  molar mass x volume in mL       |
  |                                                                  |
  |   Unit:  mol/L , written M or mol L^-1                           |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.3
Calculate the molarity of a solution containing 5.85 g of NaCl in 500 mL of
solution.

```
  STEP 1   Molar mass NaCl = 58.5 g/mol
  STEP 2   n = 5.85 / 58.5 = 0.1 mol
  STEP 3   V = 500 mL = 0.5 L
  STEP 4   M = 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2 M
```

## 10.4 Molality

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |                  moles of solute            mass of solute x 1000|
  |   MOLALITY m = -------------------------  = ---------------------|
  |                 mass of SOLVENT in kg       molar mass x mass of |
  |                                             solvent in GRAMS     |
  |                                                                  |
  |   Unit:  mol/kg , written m                                      |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.4
Calculate the molality of a solution of 18 g of glucose (C6H12O6) in 500 g of
water.

```
  STEP 1   Molar mass glucose = 180 g/mol
  STEP 2   n = 18 / 180 = 0.1 mol
  STEP 3   mass of solvent = 500 g = 0.5 kg
  STEP 4   m = 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2 mol/kg  =  0.2 m
```

> **TRAP — the single biggest mistake students make here:**
> MOLARITY uses **volume of the SOLUTION**.
> MOLALITY uses **mass of the SOLVENT**.
> "MolaRity - R for volume of solution", "MolaLity - L for weight of Liquid
> solvent". Whatever hook works, use it. Examiners love this trap.

## 10.5 Normality and equivalent weight

```
              number of gram equivalents of solute
   NORMALITY = -------------------------------------
                     volume of solution in litres

              mass of solute
            = ---------------------------------
              equivalent weight  x  V (litres)

   RELATION:      N   =   M   x   n-factor
```

```
  HOW TO FIND THE EQUIVALENT WEIGHT

  +--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------+
  |  SUBSTANCE         |  EQUIVALENT WEIGHT =              | example   |
  +--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------+
  |  ACID              |  molar mass / BASICITY            | H2SO4     |
  |                    |  (number of replaceable H+)       | 98/2 = 49 |
  +--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------+
  |  BASE              |  molar mass / ACIDITY             | Ca(OH)2   |
  |                    |  (number of OH-)                  | 74/2 = 37 |
  +--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------+
  |  SALT              |  molar mass / total positive      | Na2CO3    |
  |                    |  charge                           |106/2 = 53 |
  +--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------+
  |  OXIDISING or      |  molar mass / number of electrons | KMnO4     |
  |  REDUCING AGENT    |  gained or lost per formula unit  | (acidic)  |
  |                    |                                   |158/5=31.6 |
  +--------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------+
```

```
  KMnO4 - the equivalent weight CHANGES with the medium!

     ACIDIC medium :  Mn(+7) -> Mn(+2) , 5 electrons , E = 158/5 = 31.6
     NEUTRAL /
     WEAKLY BASIC  :  Mn(+7) -> Mn(+4) , 3 electrons , E = 158/3 = 52.67
     STRONGLY BASIC:  Mn(+7) -> Mn(+6) , 1 electron  , E = 158/1 = 158

  K2Cr2O7 in acid : Cr(+6) -> Cr(+3) , 2 Cr x 3 e = 6 e , E = 294/6 = 49
```

```
  NORMALITY EQUATION for titrations / dilution:

                 N1 V1   =   N2 V2                 M1 V1  =  M2 V2

  Use it whenever a solution is DILUTED or TITRATED against another.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.5
Calculate the normality of a solution containing 4.9 g of H2SO4 in 500 mL.

```
  STEP 1   Equivalent weight of H2SO4  =  98 / 2  =  49
  STEP 2   gram equivalents  =  4.9 / 49  =  0.1
  STEP 3   V = 0.5 L
  STEP 4   N = 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2 N
```

Used for VERY dilute solutions — pollutants in water, for example.

```
                  mass of solute
     ppm  =  ------------------------  x  10^6
              mass of solution
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.6
A sample of drinking water contains 15 ppm of chloroform. Express this as a
mass percent.

```
  15 ppm  means  15 g of chloroform in 10^6 g of solution.

                      15

```

## 10.7 Interconversion formulas

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |   MASS %  ->  MOLARITY                                           |
  |                                                                  |
  |            10  x  d  x  (mass %)                                 |
  |     M  =  ----------------------------      d in g/mL            |
  |            molar mass of solute                                  |
  |                                                                  |
  |                                                                  |
  |   MOLARITY  ->  MOLALITY                                         |
  |                                                                  |
  |                  1000  M                                         |
  |     m  =  --------------------------                             |
  |            (1000 d)  -  (M x Mw)            Mw = molar mass      |
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 10.7
Commercial sulphuric acid is 98% H2SO4 by mass and has a density of
1.84 g/mL. Calculate (a) its molarity (b) its molality.

```
  (a) MOLARITY

           10  x  d  x  mass%       10  x  1.84  x  98      1803.2
      M = ----------------------  = --------------------- = -------- = 18.4 M
             molar mass                     98                 98

  (b) MOLALITY  -  do it from first principles, it is safer

      Take 100 g of solution.
         mass of H2SO4   =  98 g
         mass of water   =  100 - 98  =  2 g  =  0.002 kg

         moles of H2SO4  =  98 / 98  =  1 mol

                     1
         m  =  -------------  =  500  mol/kg  =  500 m
                  0.002

      (A huge number, but correct - the acid is almost pure.)
```

## 10.8 Effect of temperature — a favourite 2-mark question

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                  |
  |  When you HEAT a solution, its VOLUME EXPANDS but its MASS        |
  |  stays exactly the same.                                         |
  |                                                                  |
  |  MOLARITY  =  moles / VOLUME     ->  volume increases            |
  |                                  ->  MOLARITY DECREASES          |
  |                                                                  |
  |  MOLALITY  =  moles / MASS of solvent  ->  mass never changes    |
  |                                        ->  MOLALITY IS CONSTANT  |
  |                                                                  |
  |  SAME REASON: mass %, mole fraction and ppm are also independent  |
  |  of temperature. Only MOLARITY and NORMALITY change.             |
  |                                                                  |
  |  THIS IS WHY: in accurate work (and in Class 12 colligative       |
  |  properties) molality is preferred over molarity.                |
  |                                                                  |
  +------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

# TOPIC 11 — Oxidation number

An **oxidation number** is a bookkeeping number: the charge an atom *would*
have if every bond in the compound were fully ionic.

```
  OXIDATION  =  loss of electrons  =  oxidation number INCREASES
  REDUCTION  =  gain of electrons  =  oxidation number DECREASES

  MEMORY:   O I L   R I G
            Oxidation Is Loss , Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

  The substance that is OXIDISED is the REDUCING AGENT.
  The substance that is REDUCED is the OXIDISING AGENT.
```

```
                  loses electrons
      REDUCING  ----------------->  gets OXIDISED  (ox. no. goes UP)
       AGENT

                  gains electrons
      OXIDISING ----------------->  gets REDUCED   (ox. no. goes DOWN)
       AGENT
```

## The rules, in the order you must apply them

```
  RULE 1   Any element in its FREE state has oxidation number ZERO.
              Na , O2 , H2 , Cl2 , P4 , S8 , Fe   ->  all  0

  RULE 2   For a MONATOMIC ION, the oxidation number = its charge.
              Na+ = +1 ,  Mg2+ = +2 ,  Cl- = -1 ,  O2- = -2

  RULE 3   HYDROGEN is  +1  in almost everything.
              EXCEPTION: in METAL HYDRIDES it is  -1
                         (NaH , CaH2 , LiAlH4)

  RULE 4   OXYGEN is  -2  in almost everything.
              EXCEPTIONS:  in PEROXIDES (H2O2, Na2O2)   ->  -1
                           in SUPEROXIDES (KO2)         ->  -1/2
                           in OF2 (fluorine wins)       ->  +2

  RULE 5   FLUORINE is ALWAYS  -1 . No exceptions ever.

  RULE 6   Group 1 metals are always +1 ; Group 2 metals always +2 ;
           Aluminium is always +3.

  RULE 7   The SUM of all oxidation numbers in a NEUTRAL molecule = 0
           The SUM in a POLYATOMIC ION = the charge on the ion.
```

## The 3-step method

```
  STEP 1   Write the known oxidation numbers (H = +1, O = -2, etc.)
  STEP 2   Let the unknown be x. Multiply each by the number of atoms.
  STEP 3   Set the total equal to 0 (molecule) or to the charge (ion),
           then solve for x.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.1
Find the oxidation number of S in H2SO4.

```
  STEP 1   H = +1 (two of them) ,  O = -2 (four of them) ,  S = x

  STEP 2   2(+1)  +  x  +  4(-2)  =  0

  STEP 3   2  +  x  -  8  =  0
           x  -  6  =  0
           x  =  +6

  ANSWER:  oxidation number of S in H2SO4 is  +6
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.2
Find the oxidation number of Cr in K2Cr2O7.

```
  K = +1 (two) ,  O = -2 (seven) ,  Cr = x (TWO of them)

  2(+1)  +  2x  +  7(-2)  =  0
      2  +  2x  -  14     =  0
             2x           =  12
              x           =  +6
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.3
Find the oxidation number of Mn in MnO4^- (permanganate ion).

```
  This is an ION with charge -1, so the total must be -1, not 0.

      x  +  4(-2)  =  -1
      x  -  8      =  -1
      x            =  +7
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.4
Find the oxidation number of S in Na2S2O3 (sodium thiosulphate).

```
  2(+1)  +  2x  +  3(-2)  =  0
      2  +  2x  -  6      =  0
             2x           =  4
              x           =  +2

  (An AVERAGE value. The two sulphurs are actually not identical, but
  for the exam the average +2 is the expected answer.)
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 11.5
Find the oxidation number of C in glucose C6H12O6.

```
  6x  +  12(+1)  +  6(-2)  =  0
  6x  +  12  -  12  =  0
  6x  =  0
   x  =  0            <- yes, ZERO is a perfectly valid answer.
```

> **TRAP:** an oxidation number can be zero, negative, or even a fraction
> (Fe3O4 gives +8/3 for iron; C in C3O2; S in Na2S4O6 gives +2.5).
> A fraction just means it is an AVERAGE. Do not panic and re-do the sum.

# TOPIC 12 — Types of redox reactions

```
  +--------------------+------------------------------------------------+
  |  TYPE              |  WHAT HAPPENS + EXAMPLE                        |
  +--------------------+------------------------------------------------+
  |  1. COMBINATION    |  Two substances join to make one.              |
  |                    |  At least one reactant must be an ELEMENT.     |
  |                    |     C + O2  ->  CO2                            |
  |                    |     0   0        +4 -2                         |
  |                    |     2 Mg + O2  ->  2 MgO                       |
  |                    |     3 Mg + N2  ->  Mg3N2                       |
  +--------------------+------------------------------------------------+
  |  2. DECOMPOSITION  |  One substance breaks into two or more.        |
  |                    |  At least one product must be an ELEMENT.      |
  |                    |     2 H2O  ->  2 H2 + O2                       |
  |                    |     2 KClO3  ->  2 KCl + 3 O2                  |
  |                    |     2 NaH  ->  2 Na + H2                       |
  +--------------------+------------------------------------------------+
  |  3. DISPLACEMENT   |  One element pushes another out of a compound. |
  |     (a) METAL      |     Zn + CuSO4  ->  ZnSO4 + Cu                 |
  |         displace-  |     Fe + CuSO4  ->  FeSO4 + Cu                 |
  |         ment       |     (more reactive metal displaces less)       |
  |                    |                                                |
  |     (b) NON-METAL  |     Cl2 + 2 KBr  ->  2 KCl + Br2               |
  |         displace-  |     Zn + 2 HCl   ->  ZnCl2 + H2                |
  |         ment       |         (hydrogen displacement)                |
  +--------------------+------------------------------------------------+
  |  4. DISPROPORTION- |  The SAME element is BOTH oxidised AND         |
  |     ATION          |  reduced in the SAME reaction.                 |
  |                    |     2 H2O2  ->  2 H2O + O2                     |
  |                    |     O is -1 -> goes to -2 (reduced) AND        |
  |                    |                        to 0  (oxidised)        |
  |                    |                                                |
  |                    |     Cl2 + 2 NaOH -> NaCl + NaOCl + H2O         |
  |                    |     Cl is 0 -> -1 and +1                       |
  |                    |                                                |
  |                    |     P4 + 3 NaOH + 3 H2O -> PH3 + 3 NaH2PO2     |
  +--------------------+------------------------------------------------+
```

```
  WHEN CAN DISPROPORTIONATION HAPPEN?
  Only when the element is in an INTERMEDIATE oxidation state - it must
  have somewhere to go UP and somewhere to go DOWN.

  That is why fluorine NEVER disproportionates: its only states are 0 and
  -1, so it cannot go down further than -1.
```

# TOPIC 13 — Balancing redox equations, METHOD 1: oxidation-number method

```
  THE FIVE STEPS

  STEP 1   Write the unbalanced (skeletal) equation.
  STEP 2   Assign oxidation numbers to every atom. Find the ONE element
           that goes UP and the ONE that goes DOWN.
  STEP 3   Work out the TOTAL increase and the TOTAL decrease per formula
           unit.
  STEP 4   Multiply by whatever numbers make  INCREASE = DECREASE.
  STEP 5   Balance the remaining atoms by inspection - O last, then add
           H2O / H+ / OH- as the medium allows.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 13.1
Balance:  Cu + HNO3 -> Cu(NO3)2 + NO2 + H2O

```
  STEP 1   Cu  +  HNO3  ->  Cu(NO3)2  +  NO2  +  H2O

  STEP 2   Cu :   0   ->  +2       INCREASE of 2      (oxidised)
           N  :  +5   ->  +4       DECREASE of 1      (reduced)
                       (only the N that becomes NO2 changes;
                        the N in Cu(NO3)2 stays at +5)

  STEP 3   increase per Cu = 2 ,  decrease per N = 1

  STEP 4   To make them equal:  1 Cu  and  2 NO2

              Cu  +  HNO3  ->  Cu(NO3)2  +  2 NO2  +  H2O

  STEP 5   Count N on the right: 2 (in the nitrate) + 2 (in NO2) = 4
           So put 4 in front of HNO3.

              Cu  +  4 HNO3  ->  Cu(NO3)2  +  2 NO2  +  2 H2O

           CHECK   H :  4 left , 4 right           OK
                   N :  4 left , 2 + 2 = 4 right   OK
                   O : 12 left , 6 + 4 + 2 = 12    OK
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 13.2
Balance:  MnO2 + HCl -> MnCl2 + Cl2 + H2O

```
  STEP 2   Mn : +4  ->  +2        DECREASE of 2
           Cl : -1  ->   0        INCREASE of 1  (per Cl atom)

  STEP 3   Need 2 Cl atoms oxidised for every 1 Mn reduced.

  STEP 4   MnO2  +  HCl  ->  MnCl2  +  Cl2  +  H2O

  STEP 5   Cl on the right: 2 (in MnCl2) + 2 (in Cl2) = 4 -> 4 HCl
              MnO2  +  4 HCl  ->  MnCl2  +  Cl2  +  2 H2O

           CHECK  H: 4/4 , Cl: 4/4 , O: 2/2 , Mn: 1/1     BALANCED
```

# TOPIC 14 — Balancing redox equations, METHOD 2: ion-electron (half-reaction) method

This is the method the board usually asks for by name. It is longer but it
never fails.

## In ACIDIC medium — the seven steps

```
  STEP 1   Write the skeletal ionic equation.
  STEP 2   Split it into two HALF reactions: one oxidation, one reduction.
  STEP 3   Balance all atoms EXCEPT hydrogen and oxygen.
  STEP 4   Balance OXYGEN by adding H2O to the side that needs it.
  STEP 5   Balance HYDROGEN by adding H+ to the side that needs it.
  STEP 6   Balance the CHARGE by adding electrons (e-) to the more
           positive side.
  STEP 7   Multiply the half reactions so the electrons cancel, then add
           them. Cancel anything that appears on both sides.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 14.1 (acidic medium)
Balance:  MnO4^- + Fe2+ -> Mn2+ + Fe3+   (acidic)

```
  STEP 2   REDUCTION half :   MnO4^-  ->  Mn2+
           OXIDATION half :   Fe2+    ->  Fe3+

  --- REDUCTION HALF ---
  STEP 3   Mn is already 1 : 1              MnO4^-  ->  Mn2+
  STEP 4   4 O on left, 0 on right -> add 4 H2O on the RIGHT
                                      MnO4^-  ->  Mn2+  +  4 H2O
  STEP 5   8 H on right -> add 8 H+ on the LEFT
                            MnO4^- + 8 H+  ->  Mn2+  +  4 H2O
  STEP 6   Charge:  left  = -1 + 8 = +7
                    right = +2
           Add 5 electrons to the LEFT to bring +7 down to +2:
                    MnO4^- + 8 H+ + 5 e-  ->  Mn2+  +  4 H2O

  --- OXIDATION HALF ---
  STEP 6   Fe2+  ->  Fe3+  +  e-

  STEP 7   Multiply the oxidation half by 5 so 5 electrons cancel:
                    5 Fe2+  ->  5 Fe3+  +  5 e-

           ADD the two halves:

           MnO4^- + 8 H+ + 5 Fe2+  ->  Mn2+ + 5 Fe3+ + 4 H2O

  FINAL CHECK
     Atoms   Mn 1/1 ,  O 4/4 ,  H 8/8 ,  Fe 5/5              OK
     Charge  left  = -1 + 8 + 10 = +17
             right = +2 + 15 = +17                            OK
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 14.2 (acidic medium)
Balance:  Cr2O7^2- + SO2 -> Cr3+ + SO4^2-   (acidic)

```
  --- REDUCTION HALF ---
     Cr2O7^2-  ->  2 Cr3+
     add 7 H2O right :   Cr2O7^2-  ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O
     add 14 H+ left  :   Cr2O7^2- + 14 H+  ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O
     charge: left -2 + 14 = +12 , right +6  -> add 6 e- on the LEFT
          Cr2O7^2- + 14 H+ + 6 e-  ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O

  --- OXIDATION HALF ---
     SO2  ->  SO4^2-
     add 2 H2O left  :   SO2 + 2 H2O  ->  SO4^2-
     add 4 H+ right  :   SO2 + 2 H2O  ->  SO4^2- + 4 H+
     charge: left 0 , right -2 + 4 = +2  -> add 2 e- on the RIGHT
          SO2 + 2 H2O  ->  SO4^2- + 4 H+ + 2 e-

  MULTIPLY the oxidation half by 3, then ADD:

     Cr2O7^2- + 14 H+ + 6 e-       ->  2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O
     3 SO2 + 6 H2O                 ->  3 SO4^2- + 12 H+ + 6 e-
     ---------------------------------------------------------
     Cr2O7^2- + 14 H+ + 3 SO2 + 6 H2O  ->  2 Cr3+ + 3 SO4^2- + 12 H+ + 7 H2O

  CANCEL what appears on both sides:
     H+   :  14 - 12  =  2 H+ left over on the LEFT
     H2O  :   7 -  6  =  1 H2O left over on the RIGHT

  FINAL:   Cr2O7^2- + 3 SO2 + 2 H+  ->  2 Cr3+ + 3 SO4^2- + H2O

  CHECK  charge  left = -2 + 2 = 0 , right = +6 - 6 = 0     OK
```

## In BASIC medium — one extra step at the end

```
  Do the WHOLE thing exactly as if it were acidic. Then:

  EXTRA STEP:  For every H+ in the final equation, add the SAME number of
               OH- to BOTH sides.
               On the H+ side, H+ + OH- combines to make H2O.
               Then cancel any water that now appears on both sides.
```

### WORKED EXAMPLE 14.3 (basic medium)
Balance:  MnO4^- + I^- -> MnO2 + I2   (basic medium)

```
  --- REDUCTION HALF (do it acid-style first) ---
     MnO4^-  ->  MnO2
     O: 4 left, 2 right -> add 2 H2O on the RIGHT
        MnO4^-  ->  MnO2 + 2 H2O
     H: 4 on right -> add 4 H+ on the LEFT
        MnO4^- + 4 H+  ->  MnO2 + 2 H2O
     charge: left -1 + 4 = +3 , right 0  -> add 3 e- LEFT
        MnO4^- + 4 H+ + 3 e-  ->  MnO2 + 2 H2O

     NOW CONVERT TO BASIC: add 4 OH- to BOTH sides
        MnO4^- + 4 H+ + 4 OH- + 3 e-  ->  MnO2 + 2 H2O + 4 OH-
        MnO4^- + 4 H2O + 3 e-         ->  MnO2 + 2 H2O + 4 OH-
     Cancel 2 H2O from both sides:
        MnO4^- + 2 H2O + 3 e-  ->  MnO2 + 4 OH-

  --- OXIDATION HALF ---
        2 I^-  ->  I2 + 2 e-

  --- COMBINE ---
     Multiply reduction half by 2 and oxidation half by 3 (LCM of 3 and 2
     is 6):

        2 MnO4^- + 4 H2O + 6 e-  ->  2 MnO2 + 8 OH-
        6 I^-                    ->  3 I2 + 6 e-
        ------------------------------------------------
        2 MnO4^- + 6 I^- + 4 H2O  ->  2 MnO2 + 3 I2 + 8 OH-

  CHECK  Mn 2/2 , I 6/6 , O: left 8+4 = 12 , right 4+8 = 12 , H 8/8
         charge  left = -2 - 6 = -8 , right = -8                 OK
```

> **TRAP:** in BASIC medium your final answer must NOT contain any H+.
> If you see an H+ in your answer, you forgot the OH- conversion step.
> Likewise in ACIDIC medium your answer must NOT contain OH-.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 15.1
25 mL of 0.1 M NaOH is required to neutralise 20 mL of an HCl solution.
Find the molarity of the HCl.

```
     (0.1)(25)  =  M2 (20)

              2.5
     M2  =  --------  =  0.125  M
              20
```
