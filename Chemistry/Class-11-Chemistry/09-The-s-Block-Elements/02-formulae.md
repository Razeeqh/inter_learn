# 3. TRENDS — the direction AND the reason

## 3.1 Group 1

| PROPERTY | DIRECTION DOWN THE GROUP | REASON |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | INCREASES | A new shell is added each period |
| Ionic radius M+ | INCREASES | Same — more shells |
| Ionisation enthalpy | DECREASES | Outer electron farther out, better shielded |
| Hydration enthalpy | DECREASES (Li+ largest) | The ion gets bigger, so it attracts water less |
| Melting / boiling point | DECREASES | Weak metallic bond gets weaker as size grows |
| Hardness | DECREASES (Cs softest) | Weaker metallic bonding |
| Density | INCREASES (K < Na is the exception) | Mass rises faster than volume, except at K |
| Reactivity | INCREASES | IE falls, so electrons are given up more easily |
| Electropositive character | INCREASES | IE falls |
| Basic strength of MOH | INCREASES | Bigger cation holds OH- more loosely |

## 3.2 Group 2

| PROPERTY | DIRECTION DOWN THE GROUP | REASON |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic and ionic radius | INCREASES | New shell each period |
| Ionisation enthalpy | DECREASES | Bigger, better-shielded atoms |
| Hydration enthalpy | DECREASES (Be2+ largest) | Bigger ion attracts water less |
| Reactivity | INCREASES | IE falls |
| Melting / boiling point | NO regular trend | Different crystal structures |
| Density | Falls to Ca, then rises | Ca has the lowest density in the group |
| Basic character of MO and M(OH)2 | INCREASES | Bigger, less polarising cation |
| Solubility of hydroxides | INCREASES | Lattice energy falls faster than hydration energy (small OH- anion) |
| Solubility of sulphates | DECREASES | Hydration energy falls faster than lattice energy (large SO4 2- anion) |
| Solubility of carbonates | DECREASES | Same large-anion reasoning |
| Thermal stability of carbonates | INCREASES | Bigger cation = lower polarising power = carbonate ion not distorted |

## 3.3 Going ACROSS: Group 1 to Group 2

| PROPERTY | GROUP 2 COMPARED WITH GROUP 1 | REASON |
|---|---|---|
| Size | SMALLER | Higher nuclear charge |
| IE1 | HIGHER | Smaller size, stronger pull |
| IE2 | LOWER | Group 2 still has a second valence electron to give; for Group 1 it means breaking a noble-gas core |
| Hardness, m.p., density | HIGHER | Two valence electrons make a stronger metallic bond |
| Hydration enthalpy | MUCH HIGHER | Smaller ion carrying double the charge |
| Reactivity | LOWER | Higher ionisation enthalpy |

  THE OXYGEN RULE - THE REASON

      O2-     oxide       SMALL      needs a SMALL cation   -> Li2O
      O2 2-   peroxide    MEDIUM                            -> Na2O2
      O2 -    superoxide  LARGE      needs a LARGE cation   -> KO2

      superoxide ion O2- has ONE UNPAIRED ELECTRON.

# 6. THE ANOMALOUS ELEMENTS

## 6.1 Why Li and Be are anomalous (the same three causes)

```
  1.  Exceptionally SMALL size (atom and ion).
  2.  Very HIGH charge / size ratio, so very high POLARISING POWER,
      which gives their compounds strong COVALENT character.
  3.  ABSENCE of d orbitals in the valence shell.
      (Be therefore has a MAXIMUM COVALENCY OF 4.)
  Also: highest ionisation enthalpy and highest electronegativity
  within their own group.
```

## 6.2 Anomalous behaviour of LITHIUM

| # | POINT |
|---|---|
| 1 | Li is the HARDEST alkali metal and has the highest m.p. and b.p. |
| 2 | Li is the LEAST reactive; it reacts only slowly with water |
| 3 | Li burns in air to give ONLY the oxide Li2O (no peroxide, no superoxide) |
| 4 | Li is the ONLY alkali metal that combines directly with N2: 6 Li + N2 --> 2 Li3N |
| 5 | Li compounds are the most COVALENT; LiI is the most covalent halide |
| 6 | LiOH decomposes on heating: 2 LiOH --(heat)--> Li2O + H2O; other MOH do not |
| 7 | Li2CO3 decomposes on heating: Li2CO3 --(heat)--> Li2O + CO2; others are stable |
| 8 | LiNO3 --(heat)--> Li2O + NO2 + O2 , while NaNO3 --(heat)--> NaNO2 + O2 |
| 9 | LiHCO3 exists ONLY IN SOLUTION; NaHCO3 and KHCO3 are solids |
| 10 | LiF and Li2CO3 are SPARINGLY SOLUBLE; the corresponding Na and K salts are soluble |
| 11 | LiCl is DELIQUESCENT and crystallises as LiCl.2H2O; it dissolves in ethanol and pyridine |
| 12 | Li salts are usually HYDRATED; Na and K salts usually are not |
| 13 | Li does not form a stable ethynide (acetylide) like the others do |

```
  BALANCED VERSIONS

     2 LiOH     --(heat)-->   Li2O   +  H2O
       Li2CO3   --(heat)-->   Li2O   +  CO2
     4 LiNO3    --(heat)-->   2 Li2O + 4 NO2 + O2
     2 NaNO3    --(heat)-->   2 NaNO2 + O2
```

## 6.3 Anomalous behaviour of BERYLLIUM

| # | POINT |
|---|---|
| 1 | Be compounds are largely COVALENT; the rest are ionic |
| 2 | Be gives NO flame colour |
| 3 | Be does NOT react with water, even with steam |
| 4 | BeO and Be(OH)2 are AMPHOTERIC; the others are basic |
| 5 | Be dissolves in alkali: Be + 2 NaOH + 2 H2O --> Na2[Be(OH)4] + H2 |
| 6 | Maximum covalency of Be is 4 (no d orbitals); others reach 6 |
| 7 | BeCl2 is covalent, a strong LEWIS ACID, polymeric chain in the solid, Cl-bridged dimer in the vapour, soluble in organic solvents |
| 8 | Be2C + 4 H2O --> 2 Be(OH)2 + CH4 (METHANE), while CaC2 + 2 H2O --> Ca(OH)2 + C2H2 (ACETYLENE) |
| 9 | Be forms many complex ions, e.g. [BeF4]2-; the others hardly do |
| 10 | BeH2 cannot be prepared by direct combination with H2 |
| 11 | Be is rendered PASSIVE by concentrated HNO3 |

# 7. THE DIAGONAL RELATIONSHIPS

```
      GROUP 1     GROUP 2     GROUP 13

        Li    \
               \
        Na       Mg    \
                        \
                          Al

      Li ~ Mg          Be ~ Al

  WHY:  going ACROSS a period size DECREASES and electronegativity
        INCREASES; going DOWN a group size INCREASES and
        electronegativity DECREASES. Moving DIAGONALLY makes the two
        effects CANCEL, so the pair end up with nearly the same size,
        electronegativity and POLARISING POWER - hence similar chemistry.
```

## 7.1 Li and Mg

| # | SIMILARITY |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both are hard, with high melting points for their groups |
| 2 | Both react only SLOWLY with water |
| 3 | Both give only the NORMAL OXIDE on burning: Li2O and MgO |
| 4 | Both react directly with N2: Li3N and Mg3N2 |
| 5 | Their carbonates decompose on heating: Li2CO3 and MgCO3 |
| 6 | Their hydroxides are weak bases and decompose on heating |
| 7 | LiHCO3 and Mg(HCO3)2 exist only IN SOLUTION |
| 8 | LiCl and MgCl2 are DELIQUESCENT and form hydrates: LiCl.2H2O, MgCl2.6H2O |
| 9 | LiCl and MgCl2 are soluble in ethanol and pyridine |
| 10 | LiF and MgF2 are both sparingly soluble |
| 11 | Both form useful organometallic compounds |

## 7.2 Be and Al

| # | SIMILARITY |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both are made PASSIVE by concentrated HNO3 |
| 2 | Both dissolve in alkalis liberating H2: [Be(OH)4]2- and [Al(OH)4]- |
| 3 | Their oxides and hydroxides are AMPHOTERIC |
| 4 | BeCl2 and AlCl3 are covalent, LEWIS ACIDS, soluble in organic solvents |
| 5 | Both chlorides have Cl-BRIDGED structures (BeCl2 chain, Al2Cl6 dimer) |
| 6 | Both chlorides catalyse Friedel-Crafts reactions |
| 7 | Both form stable complexes: [BeF4]2- and [AlF6]3- |
| 8 | Their carbides give METHANE with water: Be2C and Al4C3 |

```
  Al4C3  +  12 H2O   -->   4 Al(OH)3  +  3 CH4
  Be2C   +   4 H2O   -->   2 Be(OH)2  +  CH4

# 9. LATTICE ENERGY versus HYDRATION ENERGY — the solubility argument

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                      |
  |   TO DISSOLVE AN IONIC SOLID, WATER MUST WIN A TUG OF WAR:           |
  |                                                                      |
  |     LATTICE ENERGY      = energy needed to PULL THE CRYSTAL APART    |
  |                           (big when ions are SMALL and highly charged)|
  |                           BIG lattice energy  ->  HARD to dissolve   |
  |                                                                      |
  |     HYDRATION ENERGY    = energy RELEASED when water wraps around    |
  |                           the freed ions                             |
  |                           (big when ions are SMALL)                  |
  |                           BIG hydration energy ->  EASY to dissolve  |
  |                                                                      |
  |     hydration energy  >  lattice energy   ->   SOLUBLE               |
  |     hydration energy  <  lattice energy   ->   INSOLUBLE             |
  |                                                                      |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

```
  WHICH ONE WINS?  It depends on the size of the ANION.

  SMALL anion (OH-, F-):
        the lattice energy depends STRONGLY on the cation size.
        Going down the group, lattice energy COLLAPSES faster than
        hydration energy falls.
             =>  SOLUBILITY INCREASES DOWN THE GROUP.

  LARGE anion (SO4 2-, CO3 2-):
        the big anion already dominates the lattice, so growing the
        cation changes lattice energy only a LITTLE. But hydration
        energy falls SHARPLY.
             =>  SOLUBILITY DECREASES DOWN THE GROUP.
```

| SERIES | ORDER OF SOLUBILITY | TREND |
|---|---|---|
| Group 2 hydroxides | Be(OH)2 < Mg(OH)2 < Ca(OH)2 < Sr(OH)2 < Ba(OH)2 | INCREASES down |
| Group 2 sulphates | BeSO4 > MgSO4 > CaSO4 > SrSO4 > BaSO4 | DECREASES down |
| Group 2 carbonates | all insoluble; solubility falls down the group | DECREASES down |

```
  MEMORY LINE:   "HYDROXIDES GO UP , SULPHATES GO DOWN."

  BaSO4 is completely insoluble and opaque to X-rays -> the BARIUM MEAL.
```

```
  THERMAL STABILITY OF CARBONATES  (a POLARISATION argument, not solubility)

      BeCO3  <  MgCO3  <  CaCO3  <  SrCO3  <  BaCO3      stability INCREASES

      MCO3  --(heat)-->  MO  +  CO2

      A SMALL cation (Be2+) has HIGH polarising power. It distorts the
      big CO3 2- ion and weakens a C-O bond, so CO2 leaves easily.
      A BIG cation (Ba2+) has LOW polarising power, leaves the carbonate
      ion undistorted, so a much higher temperature is needed.

      BeCO3 is so unstable that it is stored under an atmosphere of CO2.

      The SAME argument gives increasing thermal stability down the group
      for NITRATES, SULPHATES and HYDROXIDES.
```

# 10. WHY IS LITHIUM THE STRONGEST REDUCING AGENT? — the three-step answer

```
  STANDARD ELECTRODE POTENTIALS (volts)

      Li  -3.04   <-- most negative      Be  -1.97
      Na  -2.71                          Mg  -2.37
      K   -2.93                          Ca  -2.87
      Rb  -2.98                          Sr  -2.89
      Cs  -2.99                          Ba  -2.90
```

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                      |
  |   STEP 1   M(s)    -->  M(g)              costs SUBLIMATION enthalpy |
  |   STEP 2   M(g)    -->  M+(g)  +  e-      costs IONISATION enthalpy  |
  |   STEP 3   M+(g)   -->  M+(aq)            RELEASES HYDRATION enthalpy|
  |                                                                      |
  |   For Li, steps 1 and 2 cost the MOST energy in the group.           |
  |   BUT step 3 releases FAR MORE energy for Li than for anyone else,   |
  |   because Li+ is the SMALLEST ion and so has the LARGEST             |
  |   HYDRATION ENTHALPY.                                                |
  |                                                                      |
  |   The huge hydration enthalpy MORE THAN COMPENSATES the high         |
  |   ionisation enthalpy.                                               |
  |                                                                      |
  |   =>  Li has the most negative E value                               |
  |   =>  Li is the STRONGEST REDUCING AGENT  *** IN AQUEOUS SOLUTION ***|
  |                                                                      |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+

  IN THE GAS PHASE (no water), Cs is the strongest reducing agent,
  because there only ionisation enthalpy matters.
```

```
  SEPARATE POINT, DO NOT CONFUSE:
      Li reacts with water the LEAST VIOLENTLY of the alkali metals,
      because it is a hard, high-melting metal with a large
      atomisation energy - the reaction is SLOW.
      Electrode potential tells you the THERMODYNAMICS, not the SPEED.
```

## 11.2 NaOH — the CASTNER-KELLNER CELL

```
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                    C A S T N E R  -  K E L L N E R   C E L L            |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------------------+

              brine in                                    Cl2 gas out
                 |                                             ^
                 v                                             |
     +===========================================================+
     |    [+] CARBON / TITANIUM ANODE  [+]     [+]               |
     |     ||          ||          ||                            |
     |          brine  =  concentrated NaCl solution             |
     |___________________________________________________________|
     |~~~~~~~~~~~~~ MERCURY  ( flowing CATHODE ) ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|
     +===========================================================+
                              |
                       sodium amalgam  Na/Hg
                              |
                              v
     +===========================================================+
     |                     D E N U D E R                         |
     |        Na/Hg  +  water ,  iron as catalyst                |
     |        -->  NaOH solution  +  H2 gas  +  Hg               |
     +===========================================================+
              |               |                     |
              v               v                     |
        NaOH solution      H2 gas               MERCURY RECYCLED
                                                <---------------+
```

```
  AT THE CATHODE (mercury):     Na+  +  e-   -->   Na
                                Na   +  Hg   -->   Na/Hg  (amalgam)

  AT THE ANODE (carbon):        Cl-  -->  Cl  +  e-
                                2 Cl -->  Cl2

  IN THE DENUDER:               2 Na/Hg + 2 H2O --(Fe)--> 2 NaOH + H2 + 2 Hg

  OVERALL:  2 NaCl + 2 H2O  --(electrolysis)-->  2 NaOH + Cl2 + H2

  WHY MERCURY?  It keeps the sodium AWAY from the water and from the
                chlorine, so NaOH does not get contaminated with NaOCl.
```

|---|---|
| With acid | NaOH + HCl --> NaCl + H2O |
| With CO2 | 2 NaOH + CO2 --> Na2CO3 + H2O |
| With aluminium | 2 Al + 2 NaOH + 6 H2O --> 2 Na[Al(OH)4] + 3 H2 |
| With zinc | Zn + 2 NaOH --> Na2ZnO2 + H2 |
| With silicon | Si + 2 NaOH + H2O --> Na2SiO3 + 2 H2 |

                 2 NH4Cl + Ca(OH)2 --> 2 NH3 + CaCl2 + 2 H2O

                              v
                     NH3 RECYCLED to the brine
                     CaCl2 is the ONLY waste by-product
```

```
  THE EQUATIONS

   1)  CaCO3      --(heat, 1200 K)-->   CaO  +  CO2
   2)  NH3 + H2O + CO2        -->       NH4HCO3
   3)  NH4HCO3  +  NaCl       -->       NaHCO3  +  NH4Cl
   4)  2 NaHCO3   --(heat, 373 K)-->    Na2CO3  +  CO2  +  H2O
   5)  CaO  +  H2O            -->       Ca(OH)2
   6)  2 NH4Cl  +  Ca(OH)2    -->       2 NH3  +  CaCl2  +  2 H2O

   OVERALL:   2 NaCl  +  CaCO3   -->   Na2CO3  +  CaCl2

   RAW MATERIALS:  brine (NaCl), ammonia, carbon dioxide, limestone
   RECYCLED:       NH3 and CO2
   BY-PRODUCT:     CaCl2

   KEY IDEA:  NaHCO3 is only slightly soluble, so it precipitates and
              can be filtered off.

   CANNOT MAKE K2CO3 THIS WAY, because KHCO3 is TOO SOLUBLE and never
   precipitates.
```

| ELEMENT | AMOUNT IN A 70 kg ADULT | WHERE | ROLE |

# 14. ODDS AND ENDS THAT GET ASKED

```
  Why are alkali metals not found free?      Very low IE -> extremely reactive.

  Why are Na and K stored in kerosene?       They react violently with air
                                             and moisture.

  Which is the lightest metal?               LITHIUM, density 0.53 g/cm3.

  Which alkali metals float on water?        Li, Na and K (density < 1).

  Why is KO2 paramagnetic?                   The superoxide ion O2- has
                                             one unpaired electron.

  Why does Li not form LiHCO3 as a solid?    Li+ is too small and highly
                                             polarising to stabilise the
                                             large HCO3- ion in a lattice.

  Why is BeCl2 soluble in organic solvents?  It is COVALENT, because Be2+
                                             has a very high polarising power.

  Why is MgO used as a refractory?           Extremely high melting point.

  Why is a barium meal used for X-rays?      BaSO4 is insoluble (so it is
                                             not absorbed and is non-toxic)
                                             and it is opaque to X-rays.

  Which Group 2 metal reacts with alkali?    ONLY BERYLLIUM.

  Why is Ca(OH)2 used to test for CO2?       CO2 turns lime water milky by
                                             forming insoluble CaCO3.
```
