# TOPIC 1 — What exactly is a wave?

Throw a stone into a pond. Circular ripples spread outwards. Put a leaf on the
water and watch it: **the leaf goes up and down. It does NOT travel to the bank.**

That single observation is the whole definition.

```
  A WAVE is a DISTURBANCE that travels through a medium, carrying
  ENERGY and MOMENTUM from one place to another, WITHOUT transporting
  the matter of the medium.
```

```
  WHAT TRAVELS               WHAT DOES NOT TRAVEL
  ------------               --------------------
  the disturbance            the particles of the medium
  energy                     matter
  momentum                   the leaf on the pond
  the "shape" of the wave
```

## The everyday picture

Think of a stadium "Mexican wave". The wave races around the whole stadium in a
few seconds. But **no person runs around the stadium** — each person just stands
up and sits down in their own seat. The people are the "particles of the medium";
standing up and sitting down is the "vibration"; the pattern racing round the
stadium is the "wave".

## What a wave needs

```
  For a MECHANICAL wave the medium must have TWO properties:

    1.  ELASTICITY   - if you disturb a particle it must be pulled back
                       towards its old position (this is the "restoring force")

    2.  INERTIA      - the particle must overshoot when it comes back,
                       so it hands the disturbance on to its neighbour

  Elasticity + inertia together  ->  the disturbance keeps moving forward.
```

> **TRAP:** In the exam, "a wave transports energy but not matter" is the
> full-mark answer. Students who write only "a wave is a vibration" lose the mark.
> The words **energy** and **without transporting matter** must appear.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 1.1

*A cork floats on the sea. A wave of speed 2 m/s passes it. After 10 seconds, how
far has the cork moved towards the shore?*

**Solution:**
```
  Distance moved by the WAVE  =  2 x 10  =  20 m
  Distance moved by the CORK  =  ZERO (towards the shore)

  The cork only bobs up and down about its mean position.
  Only the disturbance travelled 20 m.
```

# TOPIC 2 — Transverse and longitudinal waves

This is decided by **one question only**:

```
  In WHICH DIRECTION do the particles vibrate,
  compared to the direction the wave travels?
```

## (a) TRANSVERSE WAVE

Particles vibrate **perpendicular (at 90 degrees)** to the direction of travel.

```
   particle motion
        ^
        |                    WAVE TRAVELS THIS WAY  ------->
        v

              CREST              CREST              CREST
               ..                  ..                 ..
             .    .              .    .             .    .
           .        .          .        .         .        .
   -------.----------.--------.----------.-------.----------.------  mean
                      .      .            .     .            .       level
                       .    .              .   .              .
                         ..                  ..                 ..
                       TROUGH              TROUGH             TROUGH

        |<------ 1 wavelength ------>|

        Amplitude A = height of a crest above the mean level
```

**Examples:** waves on a string, waves on the surface of water, all
electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays), a wave on a slinky shaken sideways.

```
  A transverse wave has CRESTS (upward humps) and TROUGHS (downward dips).
```

**Key restriction:** transverse *mechanical* waves need the medium to resist
**shearing**. Only **solids** and the free surface of a liquid can do this.

```
  Transverse mechanical waves can travel through SOLIDS ONLY
  (plus the free surface of a liquid).
  They CANNOT travel through the body of a liquid or through a gas,
  because gases and liquids have no modulus of rigidity.
```

## (b) LONGITUDINAL WAVE

Particles vibrate **parallel (along)** the direction of travel.

```
   particle motion  <-->            WAVE TRAVELS THIS WAY  ------->

    C            R            C            R            C
  |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||
  |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||
  |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||
  |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||   |  |  |  |  |     |||||

   C = COMPRESSION   - particles crowded together, PRESSURE HIGH, DENSITY HIGH
   R = RAREFACTION   - particles spread apart,     PRESSURE LOW,  DENSITY LOW

  |<--------- 1 wavelength --------->|
   (one compression + one rarefaction)
```

**Examples:** sound in air, sound in water, sound in a metal rod, a slinky pushed
and pulled along its length, earthquake P-waves.

```
  A longitudinal wave has COMPRESSIONS and RAREFACTIONS.
```

**Key freedom:** longitudinal waves need only **bulk elasticity** (resistance to
being squeezed), which every material has. So:

```
  Longitudinal waves travel through SOLIDS, LIQUIDS and GASES — all three.
```

## THE COMPARISON TABLE (learn this — it is a guaranteed 2 or 4 mark question)

| | TRANSVERSE WAVE | LONGITUDINAL WAVE |
|---|---|---|
| Particle vibration | perpendicular to wave direction | parallel to wave direction |
| Wave is made of | crests and troughs | compressions and rarefactions |
| Elasticity needed | modulus of **rigidity** (shear) | **bulk** modulus |
| Travels through | solids only (and liquid surfaces) | solids, liquids AND gases |
| Pressure change | no pressure variation | pressure and density vary |
| Can it be polarised? | **YES** | **NO** |
| One wavelength = | 1 crest + 1 trough | 1 compression + 1 rarefaction |
| Examples | string wave, water ripple, light, radio | sound in air, sound in water, P-waves |

> **TRAP:** "Sound is a transverse wave" is the most common wrong answer in the
> whole chapter. **Sound in air is ALWAYS longitudinal.** Light is transverse.

> **TRAP:** Only transverse waves can be **polarised**. That is the single fact
> that proves light is transverse. Sound can never be polarised.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 2.1

*Why can transverse mechanical waves not travel through a gas?*

**Solution:**
```
  A transverse wave needs the layers of the medium to be able to SLIDE
  past each other and be pulled BACK — that is, the medium must have a
  MODULUS OF RIGIDITY (shear elasticity).

  A gas has NO modulus of rigidity. If one layer of gas slides sideways,
  nothing pulls it back. So no restoring force, so no transverse wave.

  A gas DOES resist being compressed (it has bulk modulus), so a
  LONGITUDINAL wave can pass.
```

# TOPIC 3 — Mechanical waves and electromagnetic waves

```
  MECHANICAL WAVES (also called ELASTIC waves)
     - NEED a material medium
     - cannot travel through vacuum
     - the medium's particles actually vibrate
     - examples: sound, string waves, water waves, seismic waves
     - speed is small (about 332 m/s for sound in air)

  ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES
     - do NOT need a medium; they travel through vacuum
     - made of an oscillating ELECTRIC field and MAGNETIC field
       at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel
     - always TRANSVERSE
     - examples: light, radio, microwaves, X-rays, gamma rays
     - speed in vacuum c = 3 x 10^8 m/s for ALL of them

  MATTER WAVES (de Broglie waves)  - a third type; associated with moving
     particles such as electrons. You meet these in the Dual Nature chapter.
```

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  Why can we not hear an explosion on the Moon?               |
  |                                                              |
  |  Sound is a MECHANICAL wave and needs a material medium.     |
  |  The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is nothing to carry    |
  |  the compressions and rarefactions.                          |
  |  But we CAN see the flash, because light is an EM wave and   |
  |  travels through vacuum.                                     |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

# TOPIC 4 — The characteristics of a wave

Five quantities describe any wave completely. Learn all five definitions word for
word; each is a possible 2-mark question.

```
                        A = amplitude
              ..        |
            .    .      |
          .        .    v
  -------.----------.------------------------------  mean position
          |          .      .
          |            .   .
          |              ..
          |
          |<---- lambda ---->|
```

```
  1. AMPLITUDE  (A)
     The maximum displacement of a particle from its mean position.
     Unit: metre (m).
     Loudness of sound depends on A.   Intensity is proportional to A^2.

  2. WAVELENGTH  (lambda)
     The distance between two consecutive particles that are in the SAME PHASE.
     = distance between two successive crests (or two successive troughs)
     = distance between two successive compressions (or rarefactions)
     Unit: metre (m).

  3. FREQUENCY  (f  or  n  or  nu)
     The number of complete waves produced (or passing a point) in one second.
     Unit: hertz (Hz).
     PITCH of a sound depends on f.  High f = shrill, low f = deep.

  4. TIME PERIOD  (T)
     The time taken for ONE complete vibration.
     Unit: second (s).             T = 1 / f       and       f = 1 / T

  5. WAVE VELOCITY  (v)
     The distance travelled by the disturbance in one second.
     Unit: m/s.
     It depends ONLY ON THE MEDIUM, never on the source.
```

## The master relation v = f lambda

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                          |
  |                 v   =   f   x   lambda                   |
  |                                                          |
  |   PROOF:  in one time period T the wave moves forward     |
  |           exactly ONE wavelength.                        |
  |                                                          |
  |               distance     lambda                        |
  |        v  =  ----------  = --------  =  f x lambda       |
  |                 time          T                          |
  |                                                          |
  +----------------------------------------------------------+
```

## The rule that catches everybody

```
  When a wave passes from one medium into another:

      FREQUENCY   f       stays the SAME   (it is fixed by the SOURCE)
      VELOCITY    v       CHANGES          (it is fixed by the MEDIUM)
      WAVELENGTH  lambda  CHANGES          (because v = f lambda)
```

> **TRAP:** Students write "when sound goes from air into water the frequency
> increases". **Wrong.** The frequency NEVER changes. The speed goes up
> (332 -> 1490 m/s) so the wavelength goes up in the same ratio.

## Two more terms you must know

```
  PARTICLE VELOCITY  =  dy/dt  - the speed of a single particle vibrating
                                  up and down. It KEEPS CHANGING (zero at the
                                  extremes, maximum at the mean position).

  WAVE VELOCITY      =  v      - the speed of the disturbance. CONSTANT.

  These two are completely different things. Do not mix them.

  Maximum particle velocity  =  A x omega  =  A x 2 pi f
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 4.1

*A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. Find the wavelength of the radio wave.*

**Solution:**
```
  Radio waves are EM waves    ->   v = c = 3 x 10^8 m/s
  f = 100 MHz = 100 x 10^6 Hz = 1 x 10^8 Hz

              v            3 x 10^8
  lambda  =  ---   =   --------------   =   3 m
              f            1 x 10^8
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 4.2

*A sound wave of frequency 500 Hz travels in air at 350 m/s. It then enters water
where the speed is 1400 m/s. Find the wavelength in air and in water.*

**Solution:**
```
  IN AIR:      lambda1 = v1 / f = 350 / 500  = 0.7 m

  IN WATER:    frequency is UNCHANGED, still 500 Hz
               lambda2 = v2 / f = 1400 / 500 = 2.8 m

  Check:  the speed became 4 times bigger, so the wavelength
          became 4 times bigger. Correct.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 4.3

*A wave has amplitude 2 cm, frequency 50 Hz and travels at 300 m/s. Find (i) the
wavelength (ii) the time period (iii) the maximum particle velocity.*

**Solution:**
```
  (i)    lambda = v / f = 300 / 50 = 6 m

  (ii)   T = 1 / f = 1 / 50 = 0.02 s

  (iii)  v_particle(max) = A omega = A x 2 pi f
                         = 0.02 x 2 x 3.14 x 50
                         = 6.28 m/s

  Note this (6.28 m/s) is NOT the wave speed (300 m/s). Different things.
```

# TOPIC 5 — The progressive (travelling) wave equation

A **progressive wave** is one that keeps moving forward, carrying energy with it.

Suppose the particle at the origin (x = 0) is doing simple harmonic motion:

```
  y  =  A sin( omega t )
```

A particle at distance x further along has not started yet — it is **behind in
phase**, because the disturbance took time x/v to reach it.

```
                                     x
  Phase lag  =  omega x (time lag) = omega x ---
                                     v
```

So the displacement of the particle at position x at time t is:

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                              |
  |                    +-              -+                        |
  |     y  =  A  sin   |  omega t  -  k x |                      |
  |                    +-              -+                        |
  |                                                              |
  |     for a wave travelling in the +x direction                |
  |                                                              |
  |                                                              |
  |     y  =  A  sin ( omega t  +  k x )                         |
  |                                                              |
  |     for a wave travelling in the -x direction                |
  |                                                              |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+

  MINUS sign  ->  wave moves in the POSITIVE x direction
  PLUS  sign  ->  wave moves in the NEGATIVE x direction
```

> **TRAP:** the sign is the opposite of what it looks like. `omega t - k x` moves
> to the **RIGHT**. Remember: "minus means moving in the plus direction."

## The two constants inside

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |   ANGULAR FREQUENCY                                           |
  |                        2 pi                                   |
  |          omega  =  ----------  =  2 pi f      unit: rad/s     |
  |                         T                                     |
  |                                                               |
  |   PROPAGATION CONSTANT  /  WAVE NUMBER  /  ANGULAR WAVE NUMBER|
  |                                                               |
  |                        2 pi                                   |
  |            k    =  ----------                 unit: rad/m     |
  |                      lambda                                   |
  |                                                               |
  |   k tells you how much PHASE is packed into one metre.        |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## The speed hidden in the equation

```
                omega        2 pi f
     v   =    ---------  =  ----------   =  f x lambda      ***
                  k         2 pi/lambda

  So from ANY given wave equation you can read the speed as omega / k.
  This is the single most useful trick in the chapter.
```

## Other correct forms of the same equation

```
                  +-  t        x   -+
  y  =  A  sin 2pi|  ---   -  ------ |
                  +-  T       lambda +


                2 pi
  y  =  A  sin ------- ( v t  -  x )
               lambda
```

All three are the same wave written differently. Any of them is accepted.

## PHASE

```
  PHASE  =  the whole angle inside the sine  =  ( omega t  -  k x )

  It tells you the STATE of the particle: where it is in its cycle
  and which way it is moving.

  Particles with the SAME PHASE  ->  identical motion  ->  "in phase"
  Phase difference of pi (180 deg) -> exactly opposite motion -> "out of phase"
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 5.1

*A progressive wave is given by `y = 5 sin(100 pi t - 0.5 pi x)` where y is in cm,
x is in metres and t in seconds. Find (i) amplitude (ii) frequency (iii)
wavelength (iv) wave velocity (v) direction of travel.*

**Solution:**
```
  Compare with   y = A sin( omega t - k x )

  (i)    A = 5 cm  =  0.05 m

  (ii)   omega = 100 pi rad/s

                omega        100 pi
         f  =  --------  =  --------  =  50 Hz
                 2 pi         2 pi

  (iii)  k = 0.5 pi rad/m

                     2 pi        2 pi
         lambda  =  ------  =  --------  =  4 m
                       k        0.5 pi

  (iv)          omega       100 pi
         v  =  -------  =  --------  =  200 m/s
                   k        0.5 pi

         CHECK:  v = f lambda = 50 x 4 = 200 m/s.  Matches.

  (v)    The sign is MINUS, so the wave travels along +x.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 5.2

*Write the equation of a wave of amplitude 0.02 m, frequency 200 Hz, travelling
at 400 m/s along the +x direction.*

**Solution:**
```
  omega  =  2 pi f  =  2 pi (200)  =  400 pi rad/s

  lambda =  v / f   =  400 / 200   =  2 m

  k      =  2 pi / lambda  =  2 pi / 2  =  pi rad/m

  y  =  0.02 sin( 400 pi t  -  pi x )     metres
```

# TOPIC 6 — Phase difference and path difference

Two particles sitting at different points on the same wave are at different
stages of their cycle. The relation between the two "differences" is fixed:

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |                        2 pi                                   |
  |   PHASE DIFFERENCE = ---------  x  PATH DIFFERENCE            |
  |                       lambda                                  |
  |                                                               |
  |            phi   =   k  x  (delta x)                          |
  |                                                               |
  |                                                               |
  |   Turned around:                                              |
  |                                                               |
  |                        lambda                                 |
  |   PATH DIFFERENCE  =  --------  x  PHASE DIFFERENCE           |
  |                        2 pi                                   |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Easy way to remember it:

```
  ONE FULL WAVELENGTH of path  =  2 pi radians (360 degrees) of phase

     path lambda        ->  phase  2 pi   (in phase)
     path lambda / 2    ->  phase  pi     (exactly opposite)
     path lambda / 4    ->  phase  pi/2   (quarter cycle)
```

Also, for the SAME particle at two different times:

```
                             2 pi
  Phase difference  =      --------  x  time difference
                              T
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 6.1

*Two points on a wave of wavelength 2 m are 0.5 m apart. Find the phase
difference between them.*

**Solution:**
```
            2 pi                     2 pi
  phi  =  --------  x  delta x  =  --------  x  0.5  =  pi / 2  rad
           lambda                     2

  = 90 degrees.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 6.2

*The phase difference between two points on a wave is 60 degrees and the
wavelength is 1.2 m. Find the path difference.*

**Solution:**
```
  60 degrees  =  pi / 3  radian

               lambda            1.2      pi
  delta x  =  --------  x phi = ------ x  ---  =  0.2 m
                2 pi             2 pi      3
```

# TOPIC 7 — Speed of a transverse wave on a stretched string

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                            |
  |                       +-       -+                          |
  |                       |    T    |                          |
  |          v   =   sqrt |  -----  |                          |
  |                       |   mu    |                          |
  |                       +-       -+                          |
  |                                                            |
  |    T  =  TENSION in the string        (newton)             |
  |   mu  =  LINEAR DENSITY = mass per unit length  (kg/m)     |
  |                                                            |
  |                     mass         M                         |
  |             mu  =  --------  =  ---                        |
  |                    length        L                         |
  |                                                            |
  +------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Where it comes from (dimensional argument, worth writing in the exam):**

```
  Guess     v  =  k  T^a  mu^b

  [ L T^-1 ]  =  [ M L T^-2 ]^a  x  [ M L^-1 ]^b

  Compare M :   0  =  a + b
  Compare L :   1  =  a - b
  Compare T :  -1  =  -2a       ->  a = 1/2 ,  then b = -1/2

  v  =  k  sqrt( T / mu )      and experiment gives k = 1
```

**Useful re-writes:**

```
  If the string has radius r and material density rho:

       mu  =  area x density  =  pi r^2 rho

                    +-             -+
                    |      T        |
       v  =   sqrt  |  -----------  |
                    |  pi r^2 rho   |
                    +-             -+
```

> **TRAP:** `mu` is the **linear** density in kg per METRE, not kg per m^3.
> If a question says "mass per unit length = 2 g/m", convert: 2 g/m = 0.002 kg/m.
> Forgetting this converts a right answer into a wrong one by a factor of 31.6.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 7.1

*A wire of length 2 m and mass 10 g is stretched with a tension of 100 N. Find
the speed of a transverse wave on it.*

**Solution:**
```
              M         10 g          0.010 kg
  mu   =     ---   =   -------   =   ----------   =  0.005 kg/m
              L          2 m             2 m

              +-           -+
              |    100      |
  v  =  sqrt  |  ---------  |  =  sqrt( 20000 )  =  141.4 m/s
              |   0.005     |
              +-           -+
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 7.2

*If the tension in a string is increased 4 times, what happens to the wave speed?*

**Solution:**
```
  v  is proportional to  sqrt(T)

  T -> 4T    means    v -> sqrt(4) v  =  2 v

  The speed DOUBLES. (Not 4 times — the square root halves the effect.)
```

# TOPIC 8 — Speed of longitudinal waves; Newton's formula and Laplace's correction

## The general form

Every mechanical wave speed has the same shape:

```
  +------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                       +-                       -+          |
  |                       |  ELASTICITY of medium   |          |
  |          v   =   sqrt |  --------------------   |          |
  |                       |    DENSITY of medium    |          |
  |                       +-                       -+          |
  +------------------------------------------------------------+
```

```
  In a SOLID ROD  (longitudinal):      v  =  sqrt( Y / rho )
                                       Y   = Young's modulus

  In a LIQUID or GAS (longitudinal):   v  =  sqrt( B / rho )
                                       B   = Bulk modulus

  On a STRING (transverse):            v  =  sqrt( T / mu )
```

This immediately explains the ordering:

```
  v(solid)  >  v(liquid)  >  v(gas)

  Steel  5100 m/s      Water  1490 m/s      Air  332 m/s

  Reason: elasticity increases enormously from gas to solid,
          much faster than density increases.
```

## NEWTON'S FORMULA for the speed of sound in air

Newton assumed that when sound passes, the compressions and rarefactions happen
**slowly enough for the heat to leak away**, so the temperature of the gas stays
constant. That is an **ISOTHERMAL** process.

```
  For an isothermal change of an ideal gas:

        P V  =  constant

  Differentiate:      P dV  +  V dP  =  0

                          dP              dP
        ->     P   =   - -------  =  -  --------  =  B(isothermal)
                          dV / V        dV / V

  So the ISOTHERMAL BULK MODULUS of a gas is simply  B = P.

  +------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                      |
  |     NEWTON'S FORMULA:      v  =  sqrt( P / rho )     |
  |                                                      |
  +------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Put the numbers in (at NTP):**

```
  P    =  1.013 x 10^5  N/m^2
  rho  =  1.293 kg/m^3

              +-                  -+
              |   1.013 x 10^5     |
  v  =  sqrt  |  ---------------   |  =  sqrt( 78345 )  =  280 m/s
              |      1.293         |
              +-                  -+
```

**But the experimental value is 332 m/s.** Newton's answer is about **16 % too
low** — far too big an error to be experimental. Newton's assumption must be wrong.

## LAPLACE'S CORRECTION

Laplace pointed out the flaw:

```
  Sound travels FAST (hundreds of metres per second) and air is a very
  POOR CONDUCTOR of heat.

  So the heat produced in a compression has NO TIME to escape before that
  region becomes a rarefaction again.

  Therefore the process is ADIABATIC, not isothermal.
```

```
  For an adiabatic change:

        P V^gamma  =  constant

  Differentiate:   V^gamma dP  +  P gamma V^(gamma-1) dV  =  0

                            dP
        ->   gamma P  =  - --------  =  B(adiabatic)
                            dV / V

  So the ADIABATIC BULK MODULUS is  B = gamma P.

  +------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                            |
  |   LAPLACE'S CORRECTED FORMULA:   v = sqrt( gamma P / rho ) |
  |                                                            |
  |   gamma = Cp / Cv  =  1.41 for air  (a diatomic gas)       |
  |                                                            |
  +------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Put the numbers in:**

```
              +-                          -+
              |  1.41 x 1.013 x 10^5       |
  v  =  sqrt  | ------------------------   |  =  sqrt( 110466 )
              |          1.293             |
              +-                          -+

     =  332.4 m/s        <-- matches the experimental value exactly
```

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  SUMMARY LINE FOR THE EXAM                                     |
  |                                                                |
  |  Newton    isothermal   B = P           v = 280 m/s   WRONG    |
  |  Laplace   adiabatic    B = gamma P     v = 332 m/s   CORRECT  |
  |                                                                |
  |  v(Laplace)  =  sqrt(gamma) x v(Newton) = 1.187 x 280 = 332    |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

> **TRAP:** The examiner wants the WORD **adiabatic** and the REASON
> ("air is a bad conductor of heat and the compressions/rarefactions are too
> rapid for heat exchange"). Writing only the formula gets half marks.

# TOPIC 9 — Factors affecting the speed of sound in a gas

Start from Laplace's formula and use the gas laws. Learn all four with reasons —
this is a standard 4-mark question.

## (1) EFFECT OF DENSITY — speed DECREASES as density increases

```
                +-             -+
                |   gamma P     |                        1
  v  =   sqrt   |  ----------   |     ->    v  ~  ----------------
                |     rho       |                  sqrt( rho )
                +-             -+

  At the same pressure, a DENSER gas carries sound MORE SLOWLY.

           v1        sqrt( rho2 )
          ----  =   --------------
           v2        sqrt( rho1 )

  Example: sound travels about 4 times faster in hydrogen than in oxygen,
  because oxygen is 16 times denser  (sqrt 16 = 4).
```

## (2) EFFECT OF PRESSURE — NO EFFECT AT ALL

This is the favourite trick question.

```
  From the gas equation for a fixed mass at constant temperature:

        P V  =  m R' T           and        rho = m / V

                     P            R' T
        ->        -------   =   --------   =  CONSTANT at constant temperature
                    rho             1

  So if P doubles, rho also doubles, and the RATIO P / rho is unchanged.

  +-------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  PRESSURE HAS NO EFFECT on the speed of sound in a gas,      |
  |  provided the TEMPERATURE stays constant, because P and rho  |
  |  change in the same proportion.                              |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## (3) EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE — speed INCREASES with temperature

```
  Using   P / rho  =  R T / M   (M = molar mass) :

               +-                -+
               |   gamma R T      |
   v  =  sqrt  |  -------------   |          ->    v  ~  sqrt( T )
               |       M          |
               +-                -+

           v1        sqrt( T1 )              T in KELVIN, always
          ----  =   ------------
           v2        sqrt( T2 )

  APPROXIMATE WORKING RULE:

        v(t deg C)  =  v(0 deg C)  +  0.61 t

        The speed of sound in air rises by about 0.61 m/s for
        every 1 degree Celsius rise in temperature.
```

## (4) EFFECT OF HUMIDITY — speed INCREASES with humidity

```
  Moist air contains water vapour.

      molar mass of water vapour  H2O   =  18
      molar mass of dry air (mostly N2, O2) = about 29

  Water vapour is LIGHTER than the air it displaces, so MOIST AIR IS
  LESS DENSE than dry air.

  Since  v ~ 1 / sqrt(rho) ,  lower density means HIGHER speed.

  ->  Sound travels FASTER on a humid day than on a dry day.
      (This is why sound carries further on a damp evening.)
```

## Two more (say them if asked "any other factors")

```
  WIND      -  the speed of the wind ADDS to the speed of sound
               in the direction the wind blows.
               v(effective) = v + w cos(theta)

  AMPLITUDE, FREQUENCY, WAVELENGTH, PHASE  -  NO EFFECT.
               (If the speed depended on frequency, a distant band would
                arrive out of tune — it does not.)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 9.1

*The speed of sound in air at 0 deg C is 332 m/s. Find its speed at 27 deg C.*

**Solution — method 1 (exact):**
```
  T1 = 0 + 273 = 273 K          T2 = 27 + 273 = 300 K

           v2        sqrt(T2)                    +-      -+
          ----  =   ----------      ->  v2 = 332 | sqrt   | 300/273
           v1        sqrt(T1)                    +-      -+

              =  332 x sqrt(1.0989)  =  332 x 1.0483  =  348 m/s
```

**Solution — method 2 (quick rule):**
```
  v = 332 + 0.61 x 27  =  332 + 16.5  =  348.5 m/s

  Both methods agree. Use whichever you remember.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 9.2

*At what temperature will the speed of sound in air be double its value at
0 deg C?*

**Solution:**
```
  v ~ sqrt(T)    ->    to DOUBLE v you must make T FOUR times bigger.

  T2  =  4 x 273  =  1092 K

  t2  =  1092 - 273  =  819 deg C
```

# TOPIC 10 — The principle of superposition

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |  PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION                                   |
  |                                                               |
  |  When two or more waves travel through the same medium at     |
  |  the same time, the resultant displacement of a particle is   |
  |  the VECTOR SUM of the displacements that each wave would     |
  |  have produced on its own.                                    |
  |                                                               |
  |        y  =  y1  +  y2  +  y3  +  ...                         |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Each wave passes through the other **completely unchanged**. After they separate,
each carries on as if nothing had happened. (This is why two people can talk
across each other and both still be heard.)

```
   Same direction of displacement (in phase):        Opposite (out of phase):

     ^  y1 = +3                                        ^  y1 = +3
     |                                                 |
     ^  y2 = +2      ->   y = +5                       v  y2 = -2   ->  y = +1
     |                                                 |
```

## The three children of superposition

```
  +------------------+-------------------------------+------------------+
  |  Two waves ...   |  ... give                     |  called          |
  +------------------+-------------------------------+------------------+
  |  same f, same    |  permanent regions of loud    |  INTERFERENCE    |
  |  direction       |  and quiet in SPACE           |                  |
  +------------------+-------------------------------+------------------+
  |  same f,         |  a pattern that does not      |  STATIONARY      |
  |  OPPOSITE        |  move, with nodes and         |  WAVES           |
  |  directions      |  antinodes                    |                  |
  +------------------+-------------------------------+------------------+
  |  SLIGHTLY        |  loudness rising and falling  |  BEATS           |
  |  different f     |  in TIME                      |                  |
  +------------------+-------------------------------+------------------+
```

Remember it as: **interference = loud/soft in SPACE. Beats = loud/soft in TIME.**

# TOPIC 11 — Interference of sound waves

Two sources of the **same frequency** and a **constant phase relationship**
(called *coherent* sources) send waves to the same point.

```
        S1  ------------ path x1 ------------\
                                              >------  P (listener)
        S2  ------------ path x2 ------------/

        PATH DIFFERENCE   delta x  =  x2 - x1
```

## CONSTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE (loud)

The crests of one wave arrive with the crests of the other.

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |  Phase difference  phi        =  0, 2pi, 4pi, ...  =  2 n pi   |
  |                                                                |
  |  Path difference   delta x    =  0, lambda, 2 lambda, ...      |
  |                               =  n lambda      (n = 0,1,2,...) |
  |                                                                |
  |  A(resultant)  =  A1 + A2                                      |
  |  I(resultant)  =  ( sqrt(I1) + sqrt(I2) )^2      -> MAXIMUM    |
  |                                                                |
  |  If A1 = A2 = A :    A(res) = 2A ,   I(res) = 4 I              |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+

     wave 1   /\    /\    /\
     wave 2   /\    /\    /\        added
     ------------------------------------
     result   /\    /\    /\   (double height)  LOUD
```

## DESTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE (quiet)

The crest of one arrives with the trough of the other.

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |  Phase difference  phi     =  pi, 3pi, 5pi, ... = (2n-1) pi    |
  |                                                                |
  |  Path difference   delta x =  lambda/2, 3 lambda/2, ...        |
  |                                                                |
  |                            =  (2n - 1) lambda / 2              |
  |                                                                |
  |  A(resultant)  =  | A1 - A2 |                                  |
  |  I(resultant)  =  ( sqrt(I1) - sqrt(I2) )^2      -> MINIMUM    |
  |                                                                |
  |  If A1 = A2 :   A(res) = 0 ,  I(res) = 0   ->  TOTAL SILENCE   |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+

     wave 1   /\    /\
     wave 2   \/    \/       added
     ------------------------------------
     result  ---------------  FLAT     SILENCE
```

## Conditions for a good interference pattern

```
  1.  The two sources must be COHERENT (constant phase difference).
  2.  They must have the SAME FREQUENCY.
  3.  Their amplitudes should be EQUAL (or nearly) for complete silence
      at the minima.
  4.  They should be CLOSE together.
```

```
  IMPORTANT: energy is NOT destroyed at the quiet points.
  It is simply REDISTRIBUTED — the energy missing from the minima
  reappears at the maxima. Total energy is conserved.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 11.1

*Two loudspeakers emit sound of wavelength 1 m in phase. A listener is 5 m from
one and 7.5 m from the other. Will the sound be loud or quiet?*

**Solution:**
```
  Path difference  =  7.5 - 5  =  2.5 m

                        2.5
  In wavelengths:     ------  =  2.5 lambda  =  5 lambda / 2
                        1

  This is an ODD multiple of lambda/2  (5 is odd).

  ->  DESTRUCTIVE interference.  The listener hears a MINIMUM (quiet).
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 11.2

*Two waves of intensity ratio 9 : 1 interfere. Find the ratio of maximum to
minimum intensity.*

**Solution:**
```
  I1 : I2  =  9 : 1     ->     A1 : A2  =  3 : 1     (A ~ sqrt(I))

  I(max)      ( A1 + A2 )^2      ( 3 + 1 )^2      16
  --------  = --------------  =  ------------  =  ----  =  4 : 1
  I(min)      ( A1 - A2 )^2      ( 3 - 1 )^2       4
```

# TOPIC 12 — Reflection of waves at a boundary

When a wave reaches the end of its medium, it bounces back. **What happens
depends on whether that end is held fixed or left free.**

## (a) REFLECTION AT A RIGID (FIXED / DENSER) BOUNDARY

```
  Incident pulse ---->
                                    |
        /\                          |  RIGID WALL
   ____/  \_____________________    |
                                    |

  After reflection:
                                    |
   _________________________        |
                    \  /            |
                     \/             |
  <---- reflected pulse is INVERTED |
```

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  AT A RIGID BOUNDARY                                         |
  |                                                              |
  |  - the boundary CANNOT move, so displacement there = 0       |
  |  - the reflected wave is INVERTED                            |
  |  - PHASE CHANGE of  pi  radians (180 degrees)                |
  |  - equivalent to a PATH CHANGE of  lambda / 2                |
  |  - a CREST returns as a TROUGH                               |
  |  - for sound: a COMPRESSION returns as a COMPRESSION          |
  |    (the pressure wave is NOT inverted, the displacement is)   |
  |  - a NODE always forms at a rigid boundary                   |
  |                                                              |
  |  Incident:  y = A sin(omega t - k x)                          |
  |  Reflected: y = A sin(omega t + k x + pi)                     |
  |           = - A sin(omega t + k x)                            |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## (b) REFLECTION AT A FREE (RARER) BOUNDARY

```
  Incident pulse ---->
                                       ring free to slide
        /\                             on a smooth rod
   ____/  \_____________________ o

  After reflection:
                        /\
   ____________________/  \_____ o
  <---- reflected pulse is UPRIGHT
```

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  AT A FREE BOUNDARY                                          |
  |                                                              |
  |  - the boundary is free to move as much as it likes          |
  |  - the reflected wave is NOT inverted                        |
  |  - PHASE CHANGE = ZERO                                       |
  |  - a CREST returns as a CREST                                |
  |  - for sound: a COMPRESSION returns as a RAREFACTION         |
  |  - an ANTINODE always forms at a free boundary               |
  |                                                              |
  |  Incident:  y = A sin(omega t - k x)                          |
  |  Reflected: y = A sin(omega t + k x)                          |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## The one-line summary table

| Boundary | Displacement wave | Phase change | Sound: compression returns as | Forms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RIGID (denser) | inverted | pi (180 deg) | compression | NODE |
| FREE (rarer) | not inverted | 0 | rarefaction | ANTINODE |

> **TRAP:** For **sound** the displacement wave and the pressure wave behave in
> opposite ways. At a closed (rigid) end of a pipe there is a **displacement
> node** but a **pressure antinode**. Board questions usually ask about
> displacement — say "displacement node at the closed end".

**Everyday example:** an ECHO. Sound reflects from a wall (rigid boundary) and
comes back to you. You need at least 0.1 s gap to hear it as separate, which
means the wall must be at least 17 m away (since 332 x 0.1 / 2 = 16.6 m).

# TOPIC 13 — Stationary (standing) waves

## How they form

```
  Two waves of the SAME amplitude and SAME frequency travelling in
  OPPOSITE directions through the same medium superpose.

  Usually this happens when a wave REFLECTS off the end of the medium
  and runs back into the wave still coming forward.
```

## The derivation (write this in the exam)

```
  Wave going right:      y1  =  A sin( omega t  -  k x )

  Wave going left:       y2  =  A sin( omega t  +  k x )

  By superposition:      y  =  y1 + y2

                         =  A [ sin(omega t - kx) + sin(omega t + kx) ]

  Use   sin C + sin D  =  2 sin( (C+D)/2 ) cos( (C-D)/2 )

  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |      y   =   2 A  cos( k x )  x  sin( omega t )                |
  |                                                                |
  |              \____________/     \____________/                 |
  |               depends only       depends only                  |
  |               on POSITION        on TIME                       |
  |                                                                |
  |      AMPLITUDE of the particle at x  =  2 A cos( k x )         |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Read what that means:** the `x` and the `t` have SEPARATED. There is no
`(omega t - k x)` bundle any more, so **nothing travels**. Each particle just
vibrates in place with its own fixed amplitude `2A cos(kx)`.

## NODES and ANTINODES

```
  NODE       -  a point that is ALWAYS AT REST. Amplitude = ZERO.
                Happens where  cos(kx) = 0.

                     k x  =  pi/2 , 3pi/2 , 5pi/2 , ...

                              lambda   3 lambda   5 lambda
                     x   =   -------- , -------- , -------- , ...
                                4          4          4

                     = odd multiples of  lambda / 4

  ANTINODE   -  a point vibrating with MAXIMUM amplitude = 2A.
                Happens where  cos(kx) = +/- 1.

                     k x  =  0 , pi , 2pi , ...

                              lambda    2 lambda
                     x   =  0, -------- , -------- , ...
                                  2           2

                     = integer multiples of  lambda / 2
```

## The distances — these are asked directly as 2-mark questions

```
  +--------------------------------------------------+---------------+
  |  Distance between two CONSECUTIVE NODES          |  lambda / 2   |
  |  Distance between two CONSECUTIVE ANTINODES      |  lambda / 2   |
  |  Distance between a NODE and the NEXT ANTINODE   |  lambda / 4   |
  +--------------------------------------------------+---------------+
```

```
   N       A       N       A       N       A       N
   |       .       |       .       |       .       |
   |    .     .    |    .     .    |    .     .    |
   | .          .  | .          .  | .          .  |
   +---------------+---------------+---------------+
   | .          .  | .          .  | .          .  |
   |    .     .    |    .     .    |    .     .    |
   |       .       |       .       |       .       |

   |<-l/4->|
   |<---- l/2 ---->|
   |<------------ lambda ---------->|

   N = node (never moves)     A = antinode (moves most)
```

## PROGRESSIVE vs STATIONARY WAVES — the comparison table (4 marks, very common)

| | PROGRESSIVE WAVE | STATIONARY WAVE |
|---|---|---|
| Motion of the wave | travels forward | does not travel; stays in place |
| Energy | transports energy | **no** net transport of energy |
| Amplitude | **same** for every particle | **different** for each particle (0 to 2A) |
| Nodes / antinodes | none | present, alternately, lambda/2 apart |
| Phase | every particle has a different phase | all particles between two nodes are **in phase** |
| Particles at rest | none is permanently at rest | the nodes are always at rest |
| All particles pass mean position | at different times | **at the same instant** |
| Pressure/density change | maximum at compressions | maximum at nodes |
| Equation | y = A sin(omega t - kx) | y = 2A cos(kx) sin(omega t) |

## WORKED EXAMPLE 13.1

*A stationary wave is given by `y = 5 sin(0.1 pi x) cos(100 pi t)` where x and y
are in cm and t in seconds. Find (i) the wavelength (ii) the frequency (iii) the
distance between consecutive nodes (iv) the speed of the component waves.*

**Solution:**
```
  Compare with  y = 2A sin(kx) cos(omega t)

  (i)    k = 0.1 pi rad/cm

                     2 pi        2 pi
         lambda  =  ------  =  --------  =  20 cm  =  0.2 m
                       k        0.1 pi

  (ii)   omega = 100 pi rad/s

                omega       100 pi
         f  =  -------  =  --------  =  50 Hz
                 2 pi        2 pi

  (iii)  node separation  =  lambda / 2  =  20 / 2  =  10 cm

  (iv)   v  =  f lambda  =  50 x 0.2  =  10 m/s
         (or directly  v = omega / k = 100 pi / 0.1 pi = 1000 cm/s = 10 m/s)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 13.2

*In a stationary wave, the distance between a node and the next antinode is 5 cm.
Find the wavelength.*

**Solution:**
```
  Node to next antinode  =  lambda / 4

  lambda / 4  =  5 cm     ->     lambda  =  20 cm
```

# TOPIC 14 — Stationary waves in a stretched string  *** LAQ — 8 marks ***

## The set-up

Take a string of length L, fixed at BOTH ends, under tension T.

```
  The two ends are FIXED, so they cannot move.
  ->  A NODE MUST FORM AT EACH END.

  That is the only condition, and everything follows from it.
```

Between the two ends you must fit a whole number of half-wavelengths:

```
                    lambda
        L    =  n x --------           n = 1, 2, 3, ...
                       2

                       2 L
        lambda   =   -------
                        n
```

Now use v = f lambda together with v = sqrt(T/mu):

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |            v          n v          n      +-      -+          |
  |   f   =  --------  = --------  =  ----- x |  sqrt  | T / mu   |
  |           lambda       2 L         2 L    +-      -+          |
  |                                                               |
  |                                                               |
  |          n      +-             -+                             |
  |   f  =  ----- x |     sqrt      |   T / mu       n = 1,2,3,... |
  |          2 L    +-             -+                             |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## THE MODES OF VIBRATION (draw these — they carry marks)

```
  n = 1   FUNDAMENTAL  /  FIRST HARMONIC
                                                    1 loop
      N                             N               L = lambda1 / 2
      |         ..........          |               lambda1 = 2 L
      |    .....          .....     |
      +----------------------------+                     1     +-    -+
      |    .....          .....     |               f1 = --- x | sqrt | T/mu
      |         ..........          |                    2L    +-    -+
      N              A              N

  n = 2   FIRST OVERTONE  /  SECOND HARMONIC
                                                    2 loops
      N            N            N                   L = lambda2
      |    ...     |            |                   lambda2 = L
      | ..     ..  |  ..     .. |
      +------------+------------+                   f2 = 2 f1
      |            |  ..     .. |
      |            |     ...    |
      N      A     N      A     N

  n = 3   SECOND OVERTONE  /  THIRD HARMONIC
                                                    3 loops
      N        N        N        N                  L = 3 lambda3 / 2
      |  ...   |        |  ...   |                  lambda3 = 2L / 3
      | .   .  |  .  .  | .   .  |
      +--------+--------+--------+                  f3 = 3 f1
      |        |  .  .  |        |
      |        |        |        |
      N    A   N   A    N    A   N
```

```
  ALL HARMONICS ARE PRESENT in a stretched string:

        f1 : f2 : f3 : f4 : ...  =  1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : ...

  n-th harmonic  =  (n-1)-th overtone
  Number of LOOPS = n = the harmonic number
  Number of NODES = n + 1        Number of ANTINODES = n
```

> **TRAP:** *Harmonic* and *overtone* are NOT the same number.
> 1st harmonic = fundamental. 2nd harmonic = 1st overtone. 3rd harmonic =
> 2nd overtone. Read the question carefully — this trips up half the class.

## THE LAWS OF TRANSVERSE VIBRATIONS OF A STRETCHED STRING

Take the fundamental (n = 1):

```
              1      +-      -+
     f  =   -----  x |  sqrt  |  T / mu
             2 L     +-      -+
```

Now hold two of the three things constant and vary the third.

```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |  LAW 1 — THE LAW OF LENGTH                                      |
  |                                                                 |
  |  When T and mu are constant, the fundamental frequency is       |
  |  INVERSELY PROPORTIONAL to the vibrating length.                |
  |                                                                 |
  |          f  ~  1 / L         i.e.   f L  =  constant            |
  |                                                                 |
  |  (Halve the length -> double the frequency. This is why a       |
  |   guitarist presses the string down to get a higher note.)      |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |  LAW 2 — THE LAW OF TENSION                                     |
  |                                                                 |
  |  When L and mu are constant, the fundamental frequency is       |
  |  DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL to the SQUARE ROOT of the tension.       |
  |                                                                 |
  |          f  ~  sqrt( T )     i.e.   f / sqrt(T)  =  constant    |
  |                                                                 |
  |  (Tighten the string -> higher note. This is tuning.)           |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                 |
  |  LAW 3 — THE LAW OF MASS (linear density)                       |
  |                                                                 |
  |  When L and T are constant, the fundamental frequency is        |
  |  INVERSELY PROPORTIONAL to the SQUARE ROOT of the linear        |
  |  density.                                                       |
  |                                                                 |
  |          f  ~  1 / sqrt( mu )   i.e. f x sqrt(mu) = constant    |
  |                                                                 |
  |  (The thick low strings of a guitar give the deep notes.)       |
  |                                                                 |
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## THE SONOMETER

The instrument used to verify all three laws.

```
   TUNING           movable bridges
   FORK          A                B
    |            |                |
   ###   +-------+----------------+--------+
         |       ^                ^        |==== pulley
         |    ===o================o===============  wire
         |                                 |        |
         |  hollow wooden box (resonance)  |        |
         +---------------------------------+       [ ]  hanging
                                                   [ ]  weights = TENSION T
              |<------  L  ------>|
                vibrating length
```

```
  HOW IT WORKS
    - A wire is stretched over a hollow wooden box by hanging weights.
      The weights give the TENSION T = M g.
    - Two movable bridges A and B fix the VIBRATING LENGTH L.
    - A small paper rider is placed on the wire at its middle.
    - A vibrating tuning fork of known frequency is pressed on the box.
    - L is adjusted until the wire RESONATES with the fork.
      At resonance the rider is violently thrown off. That is the signal.
    - At that moment    f(fork)  =  (1/2L) sqrt(T/mu)

  TO VERIFY THE LAW OF LENGTH   :  change the fork, keep T fixed,
                                   check that  f x L  is constant.
  TO VERIFY THE LAW OF TENSION  :  keep the fork, change the weights,
                                   check that  L ~ sqrt(T).
  TO VERIFY THE LAW OF MASS     :  use wires of different thickness,
                                   check that  L ~ 1 / sqrt(mu).

  The hollow box is there to make the sound LOUDER by forced vibration —
  it does not change the frequency.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 14.1

*A wire of length 1 m and linear density 0.01 kg/m is stretched with a tension of
64 N. Find the fundamental frequency and the frequency of the third harmonic.*

**Solution:**
```
              +-      -+                 +-      -+
  v  =  sqrt  |  T/mu  |  =  sqrt  |  64 / 0.01  |  = sqrt(6400) = 80 m/s

              v            80
  f1  =  ---------  =  ---------  =  40 Hz
            2 L          2 x 1

  Third harmonic:   f3  =  3 f1  =  3 x 40  =  120 Hz
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 14.2

*The fundamental frequency of a sonometer wire is 300 Hz. What is the new
frequency if (i) the length is halved (ii) the tension is made 4 times?*

**Solution:**
```
  (i)   f ~ 1/L .  L -> L/2  gives  f -> 2f  =  600 Hz

  (ii)  f ~ sqrt(T) .  T -> 4T  gives  f -> 2f  =  600 Hz
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 14.3

*A sonometer wire vibrating in its fundamental mode has length 50 cm. Where must
the bridge be placed so that it vibrates at three times the frequency?*

**Solution:**
```
  f ~ 1 / L  at constant T and mu.

  To make f three times bigger, make L three times smaller.

  L(new)  =  50 / 3  =  16.7 cm
```

# TOPIC 15 — Stationary waves in air columns (organ pipes)

Sound in a pipe reflects at both ends and forms a standing wave. The rule is
simple:

```
  CLOSED END  ->  air cannot move  ->  DISPLACEMENT NODE
  OPEN  END   ->  air free to move ->  DISPLACEMENT ANTINODE
```

## (A) THE CLOSED ORGAN PIPE  (one end closed, one end open)

```
   FUNDAMENTAL             1st OVERTONE           2nd OVERTONE
   (1st harmonic)          (3rd harmonic)         (5th harmonic)

   +-----------+ open      +-----------+ open     +-----------+ open
   |     A     |           |     A     |          |     A     |
   |     :     |           |     :     |          |     :     |
   |     :     |           |     N     |          |     N     |
   |     :     |           |     :     |          |     :     |
   |     :     |           |     A     |          |     A     |
   |     :     |           |     :     |          |     N     |
   |     :     |           |     :     |          |     :     |
   |     N     |           |     N     |          |     A     |
   +===========+ CLOSED    +===========+ CLOSED   +===========+ CLOSED
                                                  |     N     |
                                                  +===========+

   L = lambda / 4          L = 3 lambda / 4       L = 5 lambda / 4

   lambda = 4L             lambda = 4L / 3        lambda = 4L / 5

        v                       3 v                    5 v
   f1 = ---              f2 =  -----            f3 = -------
       4 L                      4 L                    4 L

        f1                     3 f1                    5 f1
```

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                              |
  |   CLOSED PIPE:      f  =  (2n - 1) v / (4 L)                 |
  |                                                              |
  |                     n = 1, 2, 3, ...                         |
  |                                                              |
  |   f1 : f2 : f3 : ...  =  1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : ...                 |
  |                                                              |
  |   ONLY ODD HARMONICS ARE PRESENT.                            |
  |   The even harmonics are MISSING.                            |
  |                                                              |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**WHY only odd harmonics?** (This is a 2-mark question by itself.)

```
  A node must sit at the closed end and an antinode at the open end.
  The distance from a node to an antinode is ALWAYS an ODD multiple
  of lambda/4.  So L can only equal lambda/4, 3 lambda/4, 5 lambda/4 ...
  which gives frequencies in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : ...
```

## (B) THE OPEN ORGAN PIPE  (both ends open)

```
   FUNDAMENTAL             1st OVERTONE           2nd OVERTONE
   (1st harmonic)          (2nd harmonic)         (3rd harmonic)

   +-----------+ open      +-----------+ open     +-----------+ open
   |     A     |           |     A     |          |     A     |
   |     :     |           |     :     |          |     N     |
   |     N     |           |     N     |          |     A     |
   |     :     |           |     A     |          |     N     |
   |     A     |           |     N     |          |     A     |
   +-----------+ open      |     :     |          |     N     |
                           |     A     |          |     A     |
                           +-----------+ open     +-----------+ open

   L = lambda / 2          L = lambda             L = 3 lambda / 2

   lambda = 2 L            lambda = L             lambda = 2L / 3

        v                       2 v                    3 v
   f1 = ---              f2 =  -----            f3 = -------
       2 L                      2 L                    2 L

        f1                     2 f1                    3 f1
```

```
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                              |
  |   OPEN PIPE:        f  =  n v / (2 L)                        |
  |                                                              |
  |                     n = 1, 2, 3, ...                         |
  |                                                              |
  |   f1 : f2 : f3 : ...  =  1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : ...                 |
  |                                                              |
  |   ALL HARMONICS ARE PRESENT — odd AND even.                  |
  |                                                              |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## THE COMPARISON (learn it)

| | CLOSED PIPE | OPEN PIPE |
|---|---|---|
| Ends | one closed, one open | both open |
| Fundamental | f = v / 4L | f = v / 2L |
| Harmonics present | odd only (1, 3, 5, ...) | all (1, 2, 3, ...) |
| Length for the same f1 | L | 2L |
| Quality of note | poorer (fewer harmonics) | **richer** (more harmonics) |
| End correction | e = 0.6 r (one end) | 2e = 1.2 r (both ends) |

```
  KEY CONSEQUENCE:

  For the SAME length L, the open pipe's fundamental is TWICE that
  of the closed pipe.

       f1(open)  =  v / 2L  =  2 x ( v / 4L )  =  2 x f1(closed)

  An OPEN pipe gives a RICHER, more musical note because it contains
  all the harmonics. That is why musical instruments use open pipes.
```

## END CORRECTION

```
  The antinode does NOT sit exactly at the open end of the pipe —
  the air just outside the mouth also vibrates, so the antinode
  forms slightly OUTSIDE.

  The extra length that must be added is the END CORRECTION:

               e  =  0.6 r          (r = radius of the pipe)

  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |                                v                              |
  |   CLOSED PIPE (1 open end):  f = --------------               |
  |                                  4 ( L + 0.6 r )              |
  |                                                               |
  |                                v                              |
  |   OPEN PIPE (2 open ends):   f = --------------               |
  |                                  2 ( L + 1.2 r )              |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+

  Because of the end correction the real frequency is slightly LOWER
  than the simple formula predicts. A NARROW pipe needs a smaller
  correction than a wide one.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 15.1

*A closed organ pipe is 50 cm long. Take v = 340 m/s. Find the fundamental and
the next two possible frequencies.*

**Solution:**
```
  L  =  0.5 m

            v          340
  f1  =  -------  =  --------  =  170 Hz
          4 L        4 x 0.5

  Only ODD harmonics exist, so the next two are:

  f2  =  3 f1  =  510 Hz
  f3  =  5 f1  =  850 Hz

  (340 Hz and 680 Hz are NOT produced by this pipe.)
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 15.2

*An open pipe of the same length 50 cm, v = 340 m/s. Compare with the closed pipe
above.*

**Solution:**

          2 L        2 x 0.5

  ALL harmonics:  340, 680, 1020, 1360, ... Hz

  So the open pipe of the same length sounds an OCTAVE HIGHER
  (twice the frequency) than the closed pipe. Confirmed.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 15.3

*A closed pipe and an open pipe have the same fundamental frequency. Find the

**Solution:**

     v              v

```

# TOPIC 16 — BEATS  *** LAQ — 8 marks ***

## What you hear

Strike two tuning forks of 256 Hz and 260 Hz together. You do not hear two
separate notes. You hear **one note whose loudness rises and falls 4 times a
second** — a throbbing "waa ... waa ... waa ... waa".

```
  BEATS  =  the periodic rise and fall in the intensity of sound produced
            when two waves of SLIGHTLY DIFFERENT frequencies travelling in
            the SAME DIRECTION superpose.

  ONE BEAT  =  one maximum of loudness followed by one minimum.

  BEAT FREQUENCY  =  the number of beats heard per second.
```

## THE ASCII PICTURE

```
  Wave 1  (f1 = 10 units)
     /\  /\  /\  /\  /\  /\  /\  /\  /\  /\
    /  \/  \/  \/  \/  \/  \/  \/  \/  \/  \

  Wave 2  (f2 = 8 units)
     /\   /\   /\   /\   /\   /\   /\   /\
    /  \ /  \ /  \ /  \ /  \ /  \ /  \ /  \

  SUM  (what your ear receives)

    IN PHASE      OUT OF PHASE      IN PHASE      OUT OF PHASE
       ||              |               ||              |
      ####            #               ####            #
     ######          ##              ######          ##
    ########        ###             ########        ###
   ----------------------------------------------------------
    ########        ###             ########        ###
     ######          ##              ######          ##
      ####            #               ####            #

     LOUD           SOFT             LOUD            SOFT
     (WAXING)       (WANING)         (WAXING)        (WANING)

    |<--------- one BEAT --------->|

  The dotted outline of the loudness is called the ENVELOPE.
```

## THE DERIVATION (write it fully for 8 marks)

```
  Let the two waves at a point be

        y1  =  A sin( 2 pi f1 t )
        y2  =  A sin( 2 pi f2 t )

  By the principle of superposition:

        y  =  y1 + y2  =  A [ sin(2 pi f1 t) + sin(2 pi f2 t) ]

  Use     sin C + sin D  =  2 sin( (C+D)/2 ) cos( (C-D)/2 )

                 +-              -+         +-              -+
                 |  ( f1 + f2 )   |         |  ( f1 - f2 )   |
  y  =  2A  cos  | 2pi ------- t  |  x sin  | 2pi ------- t  |
                 |        2       |         |        2       |
                 +-              -+         +-              -+

  Read this as       y  =  R  sin( 2 pi f_av t )

  where the RESULTANT AMPLITUDE is

                          +-                    -+
        R  =  2 A  cos    |  2 pi (f1-f2) t / 2  |
                          +-                    -+

  and the sound you hear has the AVERAGE frequency (f1+f2)/2 —
  but its LOUDNESS is being switched up and down by R.
```

**Now count the maxima:**

```
  Intensity is maximum when   R = +/- 2A   i.e.  cos(...) = +/- 1

        2 pi (f1 - f2) t / 2   =   0, pi, 2pi, ...

                                     1        2
                       t   =   0 , ------- , ------- , ...
                                   f1-f2     f1-f2

  So maxima are separated in time by  1 / (f1 - f2) seconds.

  +------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                            |
  |     BEAT FREQUENCY   n   =   | f1  -  f2 |                 |
  |                                                            |
  |     Time between two beats  =  1 / | f1 - f2 |             |
  |                                                            |
  +------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Minima occur exactly halfway between the maxima, so **the number of minima per
second is also |f1 - f2|**.

## The limit of hearing

```
  The ear can only resolve beats if they are slow enough.

  BEATS ARE HEARD DISTINCTLY ONLY IF  | f1 - f2 |  is LESS THAN
  ABOUT 10 Hz.

  (Because the sensation of a sound persists in the ear for about
   1/10 second — the "persistence of hearing".)

  If the difference is larger you just hear two separate notes.
```

## APPLICATIONS OF BEATS  (always asked with the derivation)

```
  1.  TUNING MUSICAL INSTRUMENTS
      A string is tuned against a standard fork. The tension is adjusted
      until the beats disappear. Zero beats = exactly equal frequencies.
      This is the most sensitive tuning method known.

  2.  FINDING AN UNKNOWN FREQUENCY
      Sound the unknown fork with a known fork and count the beats.
      Then LOAD the unknown fork with a little wax (this LOWERS its
      frequency) or FILE it (this RAISES its frequency) and count again.
        - if the beats INCREASE after waxing -> the unknown was the LOWER one
        - if the beats DECREASE after waxing -> the unknown was the HIGHER one

  3.  DETECTING A DANGEROUS GAS IN A MINE
      Two identical whistles are blown, one by air from outside and one by
      air from inside the mine. If methane (marsh gas / fire damp) is
      present the density of the air changes, so the whistle frequency
      changes, and BEATS ARE HEARD. Beats = danger signal.

  4.  IN A SUPERHETERODYNE RADIO RECEIVER
      Beats between the incoming signal and a local oscillator produce
      the intermediate frequency.

  5.  DOCTORS / ENGINEERS use beats in ultrasonic testing, and pilots
      synchronise aircraft propeller engines by listening for zero beats.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 16.1

*Two tuning forks of frequencies 256 Hz and 260 Hz are sounded together. How
many beats are heard per second and what is the time between two beats?*

**Solution:**
```
  n  =  | f1 - f2 |  =  | 256 - 260 |  =  4 beats per second

  Time between beats  =  1 / 4  =  0.25 s
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 16.2

*A tuning fork A of frequency 256 Hz gives 4 beats per second with a fork B.
When B is loaded with wax, the number of beats increases to 6. Find the original
frequency of B.*

**Solution:**
```
  4 beats  ->  B is either 260 Hz or 252 Hz.

  Loading with WAX increases the mass, so it LOWERS the frequency of B.

  CASE 1:  B = 260.  After waxing B falls (say to 258).
           Difference from 256 becomes SMALLER -> beats DECREASE. X

  CASE 2:  B = 252.  After waxing B falls (say to 250).
           Difference from 256 becomes BIGGER -> beats INCREASE. TICK

  The beats increased, so    B  =  252 Hz
```

> **TRAP:** Learn this one rule and this question type becomes free:
> **wax LOWERS the frequency (adds mass); filing RAISES it (removes mass).**

## WORKED EXAMPLE 16.3

*Two open pipes of lengths 100 cm and 101 cm give 4 beats per second when
sounded together in their fundamental mode. Find the speed of sound in air.*

**Solution:**
```
  f1 = v / (2 x 1.00)  =  v / 2
  f2 = v / (2 x 1.01)  =  v / 2.02

  f1 - f2  =  4

      v          v
    -----  -  --------   =  4
      2         2.02

      v ( 1/2  -  1/2.02 )  =  4

      v ( 0.5  -  0.49505 )  =  4

      v ( 0.004950 )  =  4

      v  =  808 m/s (approximately)
```

# TOPIC 17 — THE DOPPLER EFFECT  *** LAQ — 8 marks ***

## What you notice every day

An ambulance races towards you: the siren sounds **high-pitched**. The moment it
passes and speeds away, the pitch suddenly **drops**. The siren itself never
changed. Your ears received a different frequency.

```
  DOPPLER EFFECT  =  the APPARENT change in the frequency of a wave
                     caused by the RELATIVE MOTION between the source
                     and the observer.

  Approaching  ->  apparent frequency INCREASES (pitch rises)
  Receding     ->  apparent frequency DECREASES (pitch falls)

  IMPORTANT: the SOURCE's real frequency never changes.
             The SPEED OF SOUND in air never changes either.
             Only the observed frequency and the wavelength change.
```

## THE GENERAL FORMULA — learn this ONE line and derive all four cases

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |                     +-                     -+                  |
  |                     |    v   +   vo         |                  |
  |        f'   =   f   |  -----------------    |                  |
  |                     |    v   -   vs         |                  |
  |                     +-                     -+                  |
  |                                                                |
  |    f   =  true frequency of the source                         |
  |    f'  =  apparent frequency heard by the observer             |
  |    v   =  speed of SOUND in air                                |
  |    vo  =  speed of the OBSERVER                                |
  |    vs  =  speed of the SOURCE                                  |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## THE SIGN CONVENTION — the only thing you have to get right

Forget memorising four separate formulas. Use this:

```
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                               |
  |  THE ONE RULE:                                                |
  |                                                               |
  |  ANY MOTION THAT BRINGS THEM CLOSER  ->  makes f' BIGGER      |
  |  ANY MOTION THAT TAKES THEM APART    ->  makes f' SMALLER     |
  |                                                               |
  |  So choose the signs to produce the effect you know must       |
  |  happen, using this pattern:                                  |
  |                                                               |
  |        TOP (observer)                BOTTOM (source)          |
  |        --------------                --------------          |
  |        towards  ->  + vo             towards  ->  - vs        |
  |        away     ->  - vo             away     ->  + vs        |
  |                                                               |
  |  A bigger TOP raises f'.  A smaller BOTTOM raises f'.         |
  |  Both approaching = biggest top AND smallest bottom.          |
  |                                                               |
  +---------------------------------------------------------------+
```

The formal statement of the convention (write this in the LAQ):

```
  Take the direction from the SOURCE towards the OBSERVER as POSITIVE.
  Any velocity in that direction is entered as POSITIVE;
  any velocity in the opposite direction is entered as NEGATIVE.
```

## CASE 1 — SOURCE MOVING TOWARDS A STATIONARY OBSERVER

```
        S  --> vs                              O  (at rest)
        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
        waves squashed together in front

  In one time period T the source moves forward vs T,
  so it "chases" its own wave and shortens the wavelength:

        lambda'  =  lambda  -  vs T  =  ( v - vs ) / f

                    v            v f              +-    v    -+
        f'   =  ---------  =  -----------  =   f  |  -------  |
                 lambda'        v - vs           +-  v - vs  -+

  Denominator is SMALLER  ->  f'  >  f  ->  PITCH RISES.  Correct.
```

## CASE 2 — SOURCE MOVING AWAY FROM A STATIONARY OBSERVER

```
        O  (at rest)                     vs <--  S
        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
        waves stretched out behind

        lambda'  =  lambda  +  vs T  =  ( v + vs ) / f

                       +-     v      -+
        f'   =    f    |  ----------  |          f'  <  f  ->  PITCH FALLS
                       +-   v + vs   -+
```

## CASE 3 — OBSERVER MOVING TOWARDS A STATIONARY SOURCE

```
        S (at rest)                       vo <-- O
        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

  Here the WAVELENGTH IS NOT CHANGED (the source is still).
  Instead the observer RUNS INTO the waves, so the RELATIVE SPEED
  of the waves with respect to him becomes  ( v + vo ).

                   v + vo             +-   v + vo   -+
        f'   =   ----------    =   f  |  ----------  |
                  lambda            +-      v      -+

        f'  >  f   ->  PITCH RISES.
```

## CASE 4 — OBSERVER MOVING AWAY FROM A STATIONARY SOURCE

```
        S (at rest)                       O --> vo
        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

  The observer runs with the waves, relative speed = ( v - vo ).

                       +-   v - vo   -+
        f'   =    f    |  ----------  |          f'  <  f  ->  PITCH FALLS
                       +-      v     -+
```

## CASE 5 — BOTH MOVING

```
  Just use the general formula and pick the signs by the ONE RULE.

  +-------------------------------------+---------------------------+
  |  SITUATION                          |  FORMULA                  |
  +-------------------------------------+---------------------------+
  |  Both approaching each other        |  f' = f (v + vo)/(v - vs) |
  |  Both receding from each other      |  f' = f (v - vo)/(v + vs) |
  |  Both moving in the same direction, |  f' = f (v - vo)/(v - vs) |
  |    source BEHIND, chasing observer  |                           |
  |  Both moving in the same direction, |  f' = f (v + vo)/(v + vs) |
  |    observer BEHIND, chasing source  |                           |
  +-------------------------------------+---------------------------+
```

## If the medium (WIND) is also moving

```
  Replace  v  by  ( v + w )  if the wind blows from the source
  towards the observer, and by ( v - w ) if it blows the other way.

                   ( v + w ) + vo
        f'  =  f  ------------------
                   ( v + w ) - vs
```

## *** NO DOPPLER EFFECT FOR PERPENDICULAR MOTION ***

```
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |                                                                |
  |  If the source and the observer move PERPENDICULAR to the      |
  |  line joining them, there is NO DOPPLER EFFECT (f' = f).       |
  |                                                                |
  |  WHY:  only the COMPONENT of velocity ALONG the line joining   |
  |        them matters. For perpendicular motion that component   |
  |        is  v cos(90) = 0 , so the distance between them is     |
  |        momentarily not changing at all.                        |
  |                                                                |
  |                       S                                        |
  |                       |  (moving straight up, at 90 deg)       |
  |                       ^                                        |
  |                       |                                        |
  |            O ---------+                                        |
  |                                                                |
  |         component of vs along SO = 0    ->    f' = f           |
  |                                                                |
  |  Practical consequence: a car passing you hears its highest    |
  |  pitch while approaching, its TRUE pitch exactly as it passes  |
  |  in front of you, and its lowest pitch after it has gone.      |
  |                                                                |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------+

  In general, if the velocity makes an angle theta with the line
  joining source and observer, use  vs cos(theta)  in place of vs.
```

## APPLICATIONS OF THE DOPPLER EFFECT

```
  1.  RADAR / SPEED GUNS — police measure a vehicle's speed from the
      Doppler shift of the reflected radio wave.

  2.  SONAR — submarines and fishing boats find the speed of a target
      from the shift of the reflected ultrasound.

  3.  MEDICAL DOPPLER ULTRASOUND — measures the speed of blood flow
      and the heartbeat of a foetus.

  4.  ASTRONOMY: RED SHIFT — light from distant galaxies is shifted
      towards the red (lower frequency), proving the galaxies are moving
      AWAY from us. This is the main evidence that the universe is
      expanding. A BLUE SHIFT would mean approaching.

  5.  Measuring the ROTATION OF THE SUN — one edge approaches us
      (blue shifted) while the other recedes (red shifted).

  6.  Air-traffic control and satellite tracking.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 17.1

*A train blowing a whistle of frequency 500 Hz moves towards a stationary
observer at 30 m/s. Speed of sound = 330 m/s. Find the apparent frequency
(i) as the train approaches (ii) after it has passed.*

**Solution:**
```
  (i)  APPROACHING — source towards observer, so BOTTOM gets a minus:

                +-     v     -+                 +-     330    -+
       f'  = f  |  ---------  |  =  500  x      |  ---------   |
                +-   v - vs  -+                 +-  330 - 30  -+

                         330
           =  500  x  ---------  =  500 x 1.1  =  550 Hz
                         300

  (ii) RECEDING — bottom gets a plus:

                        330            330
       f'  =  500  x  ---------  = 500 -----  =  458.3 Hz
                       330 + 30        360

  Check: approaching gave a HIGHER pitch, receding a LOWER pitch. Correct.
  Note the drop as it passes:  550 -> 458 Hz, a fall of about 92 Hz.
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 17.2

*A man runs at 30 m/s towards a stationary siren of frequency 500 Hz.
v = 330 m/s. What frequency does he hear?*

**Solution:**
```
  OBSERVER moving TOWARDS -> TOP gets a plus:

               +-   v + vo   -+                +-  330 + 30  -+
      f'  = f  |  ----------  |  =  500   x    |  ----------  |
               +-      v     -+                +-     330    -+

                        360
          =  500  x  --------  =  545.5 Hz
                        330
```

> **NOTICE:** Example 17.1 (source moving at 30) gave **550 Hz**, but example
> 17.2 (observer moving at 30) gives **545.5 Hz**. The two situations are NOT
> identical, even though the relative speed is the same 30 m/s. This is because
> sound needs a medium, and only the source's motion actually changes the
> wavelength. This asymmetry is a favourite EAPCET question.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 17.3

*A source of 500 Hz moves towards an observer at 30 m/s while the observer moves
towards the source at 20 m/s. v = 330 m/s. Find f'.*

**Solution:**
```
  Both approaching:  TOP +vo , BOTTOM -vs

               +-  330 + 20  -+            350
      f' = 500 |  ----------  | =  500 x  -----  =  583.3 Hz
               +-  330 - 30  -+            300
```

## WORKED EXAMPLE 17.4

*A whistle of frequency 400 Hz is on a car moving at 20 m/s. It passes a
stationary listener. Find the change in the frequency heard. v = 340 m/s.*

**Solution:**
```
  Approaching:   f1  =  400 x 340 / (340 - 20)  =  400 x 340/320 = 425 Hz

  Receding:      f2  =  400 x 340 / (340 + 20)  =  400 x 340/360 = 377.8 Hz

  CHANGE  =  425 - 377.8  =  47.2 Hz     (an audible drop in pitch)
```

> **TRAP:** In every Doppler problem, write down FIRST whether the answer must
> be bigger or smaller than f. Then check your arithmetic against that. If you
> put the sign in wrong you will get an answer on the wrong side and you can
> catch your own mistake in five seconds.
